If the boots don’t fit.  Aka  “Boots”
and “Little Johnny”. Greg Taylor
doesn’t mind what people call him.  As
an artist he has ‘let him go’.
“It’s our [artists’] job to get peo-
ple thinking”, said Greg.  He suggests
that audience perceptions are influ-
enced by the ‘baggage’ we each take to
our engagement with art.
Greg says he is a story-teller and
that his creative imperative lies
squarely with its capacity to achieve
communication.
Greg describes himself as a quiet
person, quite a conservative – he nei-
ther drinks nor smokes and never has
done, yet he is compelled as an artist to
tell stories about issues – to create a
‘reading’ of the world in which we live
– ‘little minuets of life’ -  the vernacular
‘political’ of his everyday life.  Each
piece is a little history book.
As a child Greg Taylor often pre-
ferred to spend his time chatting to the
old men in and around the village of
Quaama.  The kind of men that were
really interesting to yarn to, but who
may not have been approved of as
model examples for a boy by his par-
ents!  Perhaps these early conversa-
tions formed part of the
foundation-stone 
of
Greg’s political critique
that manifests in his
works of art.  But Greg
also grew up as the son
of a Changi survivor.
Greg’s 
father 
Sam
Taylor’s experiences as a
prisoner of war must
have had a profound ef-
fect on his acute young
mind.
Greg is taking his
sculpture If the boots don’t
fit back to the workshop
for a ‘spit and polish’ and
to replace a ‘dog-whistle’
which was lost during
its casting.  I’m beginning
to see that none of the de-
tail in Greg Taylor’s work
is incidental.  This small
detail of the digger’s uni-
form is important to the
sculpture’s representa-
tion, as it denotes what
Greg describes as ‘dog-
whistle’ politics.
Greg reckons that the statue likes
to travel but said
that ‘he’ was very
contented on the
beach at Horseshoe
Bay.  Greg was ini-
tially inspired to
create the sculpture
when visiting the
‘greeting midden’
at Reconciliation
Place 
on 
the
foreshores of Lake
Burley-Griffin in
Canberra.
 Referencing
a famous quote by
Guy Fawkes, Greg
said, 
“People
should not be afraid of their leaders;
leaders should be afraid of their peo-
ple”.  Provocateurs such as Greg Taylor
are a crucial part of a social democracy.
Work such as If the boots don’t fit stimu-
late dialogue, they draw a focus on
values and cause us to question and
reflect upon the status quo or ‘accepted’
mainstream representations and their
parallel political agendas.  In this light,
Greg Taylor’s work must be welcomed
and celebrated; just as the Bega Valley
has celebrated, and by association, is
further acknowledged for our
proactive position on global warming
through Richard Moffat’s now nation-
ally famous ‘washing machine vortex’
sculpture.
Veronica Coen

Seasons of mist and mellow fruit-
fulness. Ahh, those autumnal morn-
ings. Mist in the valleys, a hint of frost
and clear, clear days. Beautiful autumn.
Mind you, a bit more rain wouldn’t go
astray. Who else has jonquils out now?
What does one do? Cut them and hope
they reflower when they are meant to?
Petrol prices are getting ‘inter-
esting’. What’s the bet that they hit
$1.50 before spring? Even in Sydney the
prices, usually a whole 10c less a litre,
are now on parity with country prices.
The old sign on the Tilba bypass of 86.5
cents  is a reminder of how much petrol
has risen in the
last couple of
years.
Hope eve-
ryone looks at
taking up the
government’s
$8,000 rebate for
solar panels. To
all our readers: if
you are con-
nected to the
grid, think about
getting 
solar
panels. During
daylight hours
you will get free
electricity, and
maybe even feed
back into the grid
and get credits,
you will only
pay for night
time electricity.
All you will have
to pay for is an inverter, easy. Recently
reading in the Sydney Morning Herald
about the futures price of electricity,
we’d advise everybody to take up this
offer.
Is there a more divisive issue in
our community than ‘logging/no log-
ging’? Opinions are divided, misinfor-
mation given from both sides. If only it
was a black-and-white issue; but it’s
not, it’s all shades of grey. It won’t go
away because it is a small nutshell that
reflects the huge nutshell that faces us
all, rampant consumerism against a
healthy planet.
Blue Scope Steel have launched a
‘Tank A Day’ program where they will
give away 200 tanks to primary schools,
one a day for the rest of the school year.
Details can be found on their website
www.tankaday.com.au.
Public meeting
Clean Energy For Eternity are
organising a public meeting at the
Bermagui Country Club on Sunday 3
June at 2pm.
We are a community group that
have been working hard at raising
awareness about climate change for 12
months. With the explosion of media
interest in the last six months, I think
that most people are now realising that
climate change is a real threat. Now, it
is time to start working out what we
can do about it.
On Sunday 3 June, we will be an-
swering four questions: why do we
need to act on climate change? why we
need to do it quickly? how
can we, at a community
level, be part of a solution?
how can being part of a so-
lution offer the South
Coast enormous opportu-
nity?
The 
take-home
message will be that if we
embrace renewable en-
ergy, and set ourselves up
as a centre of excellence, we
tap into what will be the
fastest growing, most in-
novative and dynamic in-
dustry the world may
ever see. There is money to
be saved and made. There
is business to attract, and
investment opportunity.
There are tourist to entice,
and jobs to create. If we are
proactive about climate
change, it doesn’t have to
be doom and gloom, but the time to act
is now. It is all possible.
Matthew Nott

I wonder if
people are aware
that ‘Message Stick’, the long-running
Indigenous program shown on ABC TV,
is now being aired at 6pm on Monday
evenings. It is, in my opinion, compul-
sory viewing, when it comes to inform-
ing the wider population on the his-
tory of this remarkable culture, which
has survived the arrival of white oc-
cupation; the stripping of its dignity,
self-respect and cultural heritage, plus
all the negative images the main-
stream media like to confront us with.
‘Message Stick’ lets us see the true pic-
ture, ‘straight from the horse’s mouth’.
Cynthia Kelly was the first In-
digenous person I ever met. We became
friends when my daughter, Cate, who
was teaching in Bermagui, encouraged
the Wallaga Lake kids to visit the li-
brary after school hours so they could
give them some extra coaching with
their English. I got the job of ferrying
the kids in and out of the village, and
while waiting I would sit with Cynthia
in her kitchen, over a cuppa, and lis-
ten to her wonderful stories. Through
her, I got to know other families, and
gradually became everyone’s ‘Aunty
Pat’. I fell in love with these gentle peo-
ple, the way they share everything
they have with each other, and their
wonderfully ironic sense of humour.
 Of course, there are still lots of
problems, but sadly we dismiss them
as having nothing to do with our so-
cial structure. But I say we are respon-
sible, and even if we do nothing, we
should at least own up to the dreadful
mistakes we have made in the past. I
wonder how you would have felt if you
had been standing on the headland of
Botany Bay, watching a fleet of ships
arriving that would take over your
country; whether you liked it or not.
So please do yourselves a fa-
vour and visit Umbarra to view some
of the wonderful carvings and art-
work done by this very special group
of people. We owe them our recogni-
tion and respect. As Warren Beatty
said in the film Bulworth: ‘People all
over the world must keep on making
love to one another until we are all the
same colour.’                    Pat Thompson

Has it been a month already?
What with house sales, birthdays
(mine), births, deaths and marriages, it
all ends up a whirlwind sometimes. Or
should we just be doing a bit more to
promote personal rest and relaxation?
I have just finished an excellent book
by Australian writer Roger McDonald.
It is called The Ballad of Desmond Kale and
I enjoyed it so much that I didn’t want
to finish it. So now I’ve just started a
new book by Ian McEwan (an English
writer). Reading allows me to forget
the present for a time - not to ignore it
but to provide a diversion to constant
thought on a current subject.
I have had the pleasure in the
past few days to be involved a little in
a current event, which was Nic and
Erica Dibden’s on-farm sale/auction of
approximately 60 head of prized stud
Jersey cows, heifers and bulls. This was
the first public sale of Misty Glen Jer-
seys at Tilba Tilba, conducted by Elders
Dairy Division of Victoria with their
exceptional auctioneer, Brian Leslie.
Brian had both children and adults
amazed with the speed and coherence
of his delivery and his incred-
ible knowledge and memory of
genetic family lines with sires
and dams from as far away as
Canada. He is an acknowledged
expert in Australian dairy cat-
tle.
Nic, Erica, family and
staff put a huge effort into pro-
viding professional and prac-
tical infrastructure for this
event. You may have driven
past at night lately and noticed
machines working under lights
and fences being finished, and
they are fine fences too. Con-
gratulations to Nic, Erica and
their team on a polished per-
formance and achieving an av-
erage sale price of $1,780 per
head with drought and hard-
ship still so prevalent in the
farming sector.
Thanks for listening,
Ewen Genders.

one more safety message, please don’t.
An ounce of prevention really is about
growing an awareness of what is
around you and a duty of care to your-

Winter is coming on so prepare

1. Check and clean chimney flues
– some call outs for the firies to attend
house fires occur in the middle of the
night and are only flue fires. Embar-
rassing for the owners when the prob-
lem could have easily been prevented.
2. Electric blankets do have a life
span and if stored should not be folded.
Check for fraying and exposed wiring.
Ensure they are working properly –
don’t make assumptions. If you are un-
certain check manufacturers instruc-
tions.
3. Similarly if you are bringing
heaters out of storage check they are
working properly and make sure they
are free of dust and debris.

amenities.

Small communities, big hearts ....
What an amazing effort from such a
small community!! The giant BROGO
GARAGE SALE held on 5 May at the
Fire Station raised $1300 towards the
Brogo Fire Brigade. Nearly 20 local
stallholders arrived with everything
including the kitchen sink, and set up
early waiting for the hordes to arrive.

and turning unwanted junk into use-
able articles again.
The idea for the event was the
brainchild of Mary Elliot who simply
wanted to return the favour given to
the community by our Fire Brigade
during the wildfire scare last summer.
Mary also approached many busi-
nesses in Bega for a gift donation that
went toward a raffle that contributed
to the total amount raised.
Wait... there’s still more. The day
rolled into the evening with a spit roast
of succulent lamb that was grown and
provided by Brogo residents Pete and
Sue Thompson that also added to the
final total. The proceeds will no doubt
be invested in more equipment to make
the fireman’s job more efficient and our
community that little bit safer. Recent
cutbacks by the Bega Valley Shire Coun-
cil have hit the tourism sector hard. A
new Tourism Committee has just been
formed and it’s great to see Brogo local
Hallie Fernandez on board. Localities
such as ours need representation up
against the big end of town. Congratu-
lations Hallie.
What’s LETS?
Following a piece in last month’s
Brogo Babble about our LETS activity,
a few inquiries about it revealed a de-
tailed explanation was in order. LETS
stands for Local Energy Trading Sys-
tem. It originated in Canada in the mid-
1980s and arrived in the Bega Valley
in 1992. The Bega Valley LETS system
is one of the most successful in Aus-
tralia with over 200 members that
generate an exchange of around 8000
sapphires a month. Sapphires are our
currency of trade instead of the nor-
mal dollar exchange for goods and
services.
It’s a bit like a sophisticated bar-
ter system. When you join, you detail
to a central information register what
you have to offer in terms of skills and
also a list of services that you’re after.
You have the contact details of all
members and if you’re after something,
you just go ahead and trade in sap-
phires. You may wish to do so, but
there is no requirement to return that
exchange. When the transaction is
completed, a LETS ‘cheque’ is written

To log or not to log
Been listening with great inter-
est to the logging protestors – oh,
friends of the forest you have finally
arrived. I am so glad to find there are
all these people passionate about the
forest and its conservation. Now when
we need assistance to collect data on
the biota of our many forests - do koala
transects, survey for weeds, deal with
weeds, etc, etc, all these enormously
time consuming and thankless tasks -
we know we can call on you guys. Of
course, this is real roll your sleeves up
get your hands dirty work - no excite-
ment - we do not require you to camp
out disturbing and soiling habitat,
there’s no media attention, no photos
in the paper, no heroic look-at-me,
look-at-me trips in the local paddy
wagon. Just real conservation work.
Now don’t think I am against
protest - where would I be today if it
were not for the suffragettes. I couldn’t

logged again. Logging methods of the
past were a disaster but there must be
balance to these situations - the tim-
ber industry is improving in its meth-
odology and approach all the time and
many loggers are good bushies, they
are not all rednecks. Farming methods
of the past were equally disastrous and
I believe it was unfortunate that cot-
ton and rice farming were allowed to
be developed on the driest of conti-
nents.

group of people or The Triangle get all

HORSE HYPE
Horse Hype is a feature in our
Triangle sure to please all
those horse lovers out there.
There must be loads of
interesting stories to be told,
so send them in and share
them with us.
When I was asked by The Triangle
to write a small segment about horses,
I felt it would be a good opportunity to
address the issue of winter feeding of
our equine friends. Due to 90% of NSW
being in drought, the availability of all
forms of fodder for cattle
or horses is low. Costs of
buying hay or horse feed
is very high. No doubt all
horse owners have had to
make some harsh deci-
sions of late as to whether
to keep their animals or
sell them on to people
who are more able to
wear the costs of feeding.
Fodder costs are
expected to stay high for
the remainder of 2007
and into 2008. Here are a
few tips for winter feed-
ing:
Always remember to match feed
to the horse’s working conditions. A
horse uses a lot of energy in winter to
keep warm, so rugging helps keep con-
dition on. There is an old saying that a
rug is as good as a feed - this is true.
Rather than feeding one large
feed per day, feed two smaller feeds
(night and morning). A horse in the wild
or in a natural environment grazes all
day, so they need to be able to chew at
grass or hay at all times.
Never leave a horse or pony
locked in a stable or yard without am-
ple supplies of hay to pick at. Horses
should have their teeth done at least
every 12 months, by an Equine dentist.
Horses that drop their feed or
ball their feed have problems with
their teeth and this needs to be ad-
dressed. Worming is always impor-
tant. Drenches should be rotated. Al-
ways mark on your calendar when you
have done this job (roughly about
every 16 weeks).
Always put your horse into a
fresh paddock after worming. If you are
unable to rotate your horse paddocks,
picking up poo helps to keep worm in-
festation at bay.
If you are unsure as to which
feed to give your horse, never be afraid
to ask for suggestions or help.
Enjoy your horse, because given
the chance they will give you back ten-
fold what you give them.
Janelle Salway.
