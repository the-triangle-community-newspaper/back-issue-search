fabulous people and groups in the Triangle 
area who are, in many ways, as different 
as they are similar. Many individuals and 
groups put a great deal of time and effort 
into celebrating our past to ensure that 
the endeavours of those who have gone

featuring the wonderful sisters Norma

the survival of a commemorative pine to 
continue the tradition of maintaining a 
living memorial presence of Gallipoli’s 
Lone Pine.

sad time especially for families and close 
friends, it offers many of us the opportunity

efforts of those departed who have helped 
shape our past, present and future. We are 
an eclectic collection of individuals and 
the more I find out about us, the more I 
understand why we are such a vibrant, 
connected and important community.

Eurobodalla community takes a giant step towards 
emergency preparedness
On Tuesday, 14 October, the 
Mechanics Institute buzzed with energy 
as the Eurobodalla Preparedness Guide 
was officially launched to a packed 
audience of local dignitaries, emergency 
services, community support groups 
and enthusiastic residents. Among those 
present were Fiona Phillips MP and Mayor 
Hatcher, highlighting the importance 
of this essential new resource for our 
community. Von Hutcheson and I are 
locals and we created the Eurobodalla 
Preparedness Guide, designed to help 
every individual, family, neighbourhood 
and community member be better prepared 
for emergencies. With clear, easy-to-
use checklists and trusted resources all 
gathered in one handy guide, it’s your go-to 
companion to help you plan and stay safe.
The Black Summer bushfires were a 
stark reminder of how vulnerable we all 
can be when disaster strikes. For many, 
it was the first time they truly felt under 
threat and unsure of what to do. This 
guide aims to change that by empowering 
you with practical steps to take before 
and during an emergency. While we can’t 
control disasters, having a plan and being 
organised gives you a sense of control – 
easing the uncertainty and helping you 
respond confidently.
A guide to the Guide
•	
Trusted expert advice and all key 
emergency contacts in one place
•	
Personal action plans tailored to your 
needs
•	
Simple checklists to organise 
yourselves quickly
•	
Recommendations for useful phone 
apps, including the Red Cross Get 
Prepared app, Hazards Near Me, 
BOM, Live Traffic, Emergency+ and 
ABC South East Radio 103.5FM for 
up-to-date alerts
•	
Tips on packing an Emergency 
Survival Kit for easy grab-and-go 
•	
Special considerations for families 
with babies and toddlers, elderly 
and vulnerable residents, pets and 
livestock
•	
Handy fridge magnets on the back for 
quick access to important information
Between 10 and 14 November, every 
household in Eurobodalla Shire will receive 
a copy of this invaluable guide in their 
letterbox or PO Box, thanks to Eurobodalla 
Council. We encourage you to not only 
read it but take action – even ticking off 
a few items on the checklists can make a 
difference. Share it with your workplace, 
community groups, family, friends and 
neighbours. Talk about it. Together, we can 
build a safer, more prepared community.
At the launch, guest speaker, Dr 
Brett Henderson, emphasised how vital 
community connections are in emergencies. 
When neighbours know each other and 
work together, they can provide support 
quickly, especially when official emergency 
services are stretched. From checking 
on vulnerable residents to sharing food, 
water or emotional support, a connected 
community can save lives and ease the 
pressure during a crisis.
When your Eurobodalla Preparedness 
Guide arrives, take a moment to explore it. 
Use it to start conversations, make a plan 
and take steps to protect yourself and your 
loved ones. Preparedness starts with you – 
and together, we can build a stronger, more 
resilient community.
The Eurobodalla Preparedness Guide 
was primarily supported by SHASA, and we 
are grateful for the generosity of other local 
organisations such as Anglicare Recovery, 
Eurobodalla Council, Rotary Moruya, the 
NSW Supporting Spontaneous Volunteer 
Program, Eurobodalla Community Support 
Network, and SAGE.
Sandra Makdessi

Put it on your fridge – a reality check
The launch of the excellent Eurobodalla Preparedness Guide 
in Moruya was not a mundane event. It was a terrific community 
event, attended by deeply engaged volunteers and professionals. 
One of the highlights for me was the talk and slideshow presented 
by Brett Henderson, an emergency management professional, 
Board member of the Australasian Institute of Emergency 
Services, SES volunteer and a retired Police Commander. 
Brett’s talk was dense with sobering numbers and data. Each 
accompanying slide had the name of the organisation that wrote 
the report and the date – making it easy to cross-reference and seek 
more information. I’ve used bullet points to keep it as succinct 
and straightforward as possible. I’m going to focus on five slides 
in particular:
Australia’s National Climate Risk Assessment Report 2025:
•	
Climate change is already driving more frequent and severe 
extreme weather events that affect every aspect of Australian 
society.
•	
Systemic, cascading risks, such as those between infrastructure, 
supply chains and communities amplify vulnerabilities.
•	
Governance risks are rising, with maladaptation and inaction 
identified as growing threats due to inadequate coordination, 
unclear roles and under-resourcing across jurisdictions.
Australian Government’s 2025 National Adaptation Plan (NAP):
•	
The NAP recognises that climate change increases the 
frequency, intensity and complexity of disasters that are 
linked directly to bushfires, heatwaves, floods (riverine, 
coastal and flash), cyclones and severe storms, and droughts. 
These hazards are becoming more complex, cascading and 
concurrent – meaning that multiple disasters can occur 
simultaneously or trigger one another.
Insurance Catastrophe Resilience Report 2024-25:
•	
Insured losses from extreme weather events in the past five 
years were about $22.5 billion, a 67% increase on the previous 
five-year period.
•	
Between 1980 and 2020, Australia ranked second behind the 
US (among developed nations) for insured and economic 
losses per capita from extreme weather events.
The Edelman Trust Barometer (Australia). This began as a 
family-run PR firm in Chicago in 1952, but has since become a 
global entity and has been doing longitudinal surveys in Australia 
since 1987.
•	
Public institutions including state emergency services 
now operate in a low-trust environment. Communities 
may question official messages (including warnings), 
preparedness campaigns and post-disaster recovery policies.
•	
We require transparency and inclusion in planning processes 
to build legitimacy.
•	
Localised communication delivered by trusted local 
volunteers or leaders rather than central agencies enhances 
credibility.
•	
Community participation in after-action reviews and the 
evaluation of disaster responses is needed to create a feedback 
loop of accountability and mutual respect. 
The final slide looked at our capacity to respond effectively in five 
different scenarios rated from low-moderate to catastrophic. At the 
first three levels, low-moderate to high-very high, our capacity to 
respond to emergencies is adequate – we have enough resources, 
skills, staffing levels, technology etc. to manage risk and mitigate 
consequences. But when the crisis is assessed as ‘severe’, our 
actual capacity is insufficient. And once the situation is rated 
‘extreme’ or ‘catastrophic’, our capacity is completely overrun 
and, Dr Henderson said, we would be in volatile, uncertain, 
complex and ambiguous territory.
All this was delivered in a steady, undramatic manner but 
the meaning could not have been clearer – the new operating 
environment leaves us without enough time to recover between 
crises. We must rely on the strength of our communities because 
the usual first responder organisations will be spread too thinly 
to rescue everyone. So, we need to build communication within 
communities, between neighbours and individuals, and be 
prepared personally for emergencies.

What to do if you see a snake
I was running from Big Beares Beach 
to Little Beares Beach in mid-October and 
almost landed on a quick moving black 
snake as it frantically slithered into the 
scrub. I let out a little yelp but it was gone 
when I looked around.
If you do see a snake, the advice from 
Wildlife Rescue South Coast (WRSC) is to 
stop, be still and, if safe to do so, back away 
slowly. Give the snake a chance to escape. 
Most bites occur when people try to catch 
or kill them, which is illegal as they are a 
protected species in Australia.
The WRSC hotline often receives 
calls about diamond pythons and red-
bellied black snakes. Diamond pythons 
are non-venomous and a friendly snake. I 
had one of them wait for my dog and me 
to walk up the path leading away from 
the beach before slithering across into 
the scrub. Red-bellied black snakes are 
venomous but they are shy and generally 
only deliver a serious bite when harassed. 
Less common WRSC snake rescues 
are for tiger snakes and eastern browns. Be 
respectful of these snakes as they are much 
more likely to bite if they feel threatened. 
WRSC is licensed under NPWS 
to catch snakes that are injured, sick, 
displaced or posing a threat. Members 
who have completed the venomous snake 
handling course are authorised under the 
WRSC licence to attend to snake rescues. 
What to do if a snake appears on your 
property? Keep pets inside and wait for it 
to move on. Catching or killing a snake can 
incur a fine up to $10,000. 
If you need help or advice, call the 
WRSC 24-hour hotline on 0417 238 921.
You can also contact Mal Barry of 
the Narooma VRA on 0428 525 493 if 
you are in the north of the Triangle area 
or Sapphire Snake Removals, a private 
business, on 0400 355 998 if you are down 
closer to Bega.

Create your ‘grab and go’ document pack for summer
With predictions for a hot, dry summer this year, be prepared 
by keeping your important documents handy to ‘grab and go’ in 
a range of emergency situations. We can’t anticipate what might 
arise that will require us to have specific documentation, so 
planning for every possible event is a worthwhile exercise. So 
many people have been caught out, or had support or services 
delayed, because they had lost all their papers in a fire, flood or 
accident.
I kept the following documents in an expanding file (Big 
W, Kmart or a local stationery store) but, after the 2019/2020 
bushfires, I bought a fireproof satchel for an added level of security 
(Bunnings $18; online for something with a higher fire safety rate). 
You can scan all your documents and put them on a thumb drive, 
but most agencies require either an original document or a copy 
that has been certified by a Justice of the Peace (see my article in 
the February 2025 issue) – keep these together and keep them safe!
•	
Original, FULL birth certificates, marriage certificates, 
adoption papers, change of name documentation, divorce 
papers etc.
•	
A valid Will; your next-of-kin’s full birth certificate showing 
their relationship to you.
•	
Identification cards (passport, driver’s licence – anything that 
has your photo, date of birth, full name and address etc). Also 
important is a rates notice or letter proving where you live.
•	
Insurance policies, super fund membership details.
•	
Stocks, shares, bank statements, crypto-currency information, 
cash on hand and credit/debit cards.
•	
Business registration, ABN, Tax File Number; Centrelink/
DVA information. Receipts for bookkeeping and tax purposes.
•	
Advance Care Directive, Enduring Guardian and Power of 
Attorney documents.
•	
All health information including Medicare and health 
insurance cards, GP and specialist contact details, blood type, 
organ donor registration, prescriptions, medical history etc.
•	
All car, boat, bike and caravan registrations. All driver’s, 
fishing and firearms licences etc.
•	
Mortgage documentation from your bank and property 
ownership documentation from Land Titles Office.
•	
Your Rest Easy Journal or other booklets that have all your 
information listed in one place.
•	
Photographs, albums, letters, music, artwork etc., that hold 
precious memories.
Medical emergencies: It’s really helpful for ambos, nurses 
and other medical staff if you have a laminated card that lists 
your communication or care needs (especially helpful for those 
with dementia) or the needs of any person you’re caring for. Any 
allergies, conditions, recent surgeries, plus next-of-kin/carer 
contact details and how to access an Advance Care Directive if 
there is one. This card will probably need updating regularly, but 
it’s certainly worth the effort, and would be especially important if 
you got separated from others for any reason. Have a safe, happy 
summer everyone!
Shanna Provost

Montreal Gold Field up and running
After a brief winter break, tours have once again commenced 
at Montreal Goldfield, Wallaga Lake. It was a very busy three 
weeks during school holidays with over seventy people making 
their way to the goldfield to learn about Bermagui’s history and 
golden past.
A large group of 38 home-schooled children and their parents 
also enjoyed a special day with a glimpse of what an 1880’s child 
did at school. The children, aged from toddlers to teenagers, were 
shown the skills of gold panning, with the very young finding 
‘gold’ nuggets in the sand pit.
After doing a tour of the goldfield site, our resident school 
ma’ms, Sheila and Georgina, conducted a school room scenario 
where the children listened to stories, learnt games like quoits, 
knuckles, skipping ropes and dressed up in period costumes.
Group tour bookings have begun to flood in with bus tours 
booked through to February 2026! Many tours are from companies 
who have enjoyed Montreal hospitality in the past and will return 
with another eager group of travellers – usually seniors enjoying 
a day, or week, away.
All this activity is putting quite a lot of pressure on our 
already hard-working guides and an active call out is being made 
for anyone interested in volunteering and training as a guide at 
Montreal. You need to be reasonably physically fit and enjoy 
engaging with the general public – the gift of the gab is a real asset!
Montreal, like many other community groups, has struggled 
to recruit new volunteers over the past few years. We have a 
wish list – we need a marketing manager, a museum curator and 
souvenir co-ordinator, a maintenance crew to keep the goldfield 
site safe and tidy and, of course, tour guides. We need people with 
great ideas and willingness to carry them out, to take Montreal 
Goldfield into the 21st century!
If any of these positions is of interest to you, please drop us 
an email at: montrealgoldfieldnsw@gmail.com or phone Carolyne 
on 0402 064 802. 
Carolyne Banados

Are you affected by someone’s 
alcohol or drug use?
At Directions, we know that when someone is struggling 
with substance use, it doesn’t only affect them – it touches the 
lives of the people who love and care for them. Families, friends, 
and carers often carry a heavy burden, sometimes quietly, as they

Learning to live with Varroa mite
Varroa mite, an external parasite that infests bee colonies and 
reduces the health of bees, is now considered established in NSW. 
After an attempt at eradication, a Varroa mite management plan was 
approved in early 2024. This plan aims to increase resilience and 
minimise ongoing impacts of Varroa mite across Australia’s bee 
populations and bee-dependent industries. As part of this process, 
the Department of Primary Industry (DPI), has introduced has 
introduced the role of developmental officer to support and advise 
beekeepers on all aspects of Varroa management.
Recently the Bega Valley had a visit from developmental 
officer, Simon Phillips. Simon is a beekeeper with decades of 
practical hands-on experience. He visited and worked one-on-one, 
in the field, with local recreational/backyard beekeepers to assist 
them to increase their knowledge, skills and confidence levels in 
managing their hives and Varroa mite.
When Simon visited my hives, he demonstrated a wealth of 
knowledge in a short time. To watch how easily, efficiently and 
confidently he worked the hives was a great education in itself. 
He answered all my questions, including how to monitor and 
determine mite loads, the mite testing process, the different types of 
control methods available, as well as discussing a yearly integrated 
pest control plan tailored to my situation. He offered hands-on 
assistance with judging the health of the hives, and provided basic 
information such as how to use the smoker correctly and how to 
identify signs of a healthy or unhealthy queen. As a bonus, he was 
able to identify and mark the queen bees and they are now sporting 
attractive bright pink spots on the back of their heads.
With help and advice from the DPI and the developmental 
officers like Simon, our beekeeping community has a chance to 
learn how to live with Varroa mite and to help our bee colonies to 
thrive and flourish.

Hilary James
6 July 1930 – 17 Sept 2025
Hilary died peacefully, not long after her 95th birthday, 
which she celebrated as she had lived her life: with good friends, 
good food and laughter. 
Hilary moved with her husband John to the Bega Valley ten 
years ago to join their daughter Emily and her husband Steve. 
They first lived on a beautiful five-acre property overlooking 
Mumbulla Mountain, then moved to Bega. It was the last big 
adventure of her life. They made wonderful friends and became 
part of the community, joining everything! They loved the Quaama 
choir, U3A, Bega Seedsavers, food swaps, Well Thumbed Books 
and the warm Brogo community – it felt like their natural home.
Hilary was raised in the bush in Sydney, in an interesting 
family, who opened their home to refugees after the war and who 
showed Hilary glimpses of another world. Hilary was always 
adventurous, organising hitchhiking holidays with her university 
friends into the outback. Once she had met and married John, travel 
became a central part of their lives. Often rough and simple, it was 
based on the premise of saying ‘Yes’ to any interesting opportunity 
that arose. Hilary had a very positive attitude to life and always 
looked forward to the next adventure awaiting her. This included 
years of hitch-hiking in Europe and a long stint selling advertising 
in Africa which was still made up of many British colonies – a 
very different time.
Once their three children, Cassandra, Rebecca and Emily, 
arrived, it was natural to take them along too, so she was able 
to follow her dream of giving us children a free and creative 
childhood. The family moved to Europe in 1969 and lived there 
for four years, moving each season between different countries. 
They would find somewhere simple to rent in Italy, France or 
England and stay there for three months at a time. Hilary would 
home-school us and give us incredible freedom to explore whatever 
new village we were in. Wherever they lived, she would source 
yeast from a local baker (no dried yeast in those days) and make 
rustic bread by hand – this meant that we knew that we were home. 
Life for Hilary and John alternated between travel in Europe 
and Southeast Asia, and homes tucked into the bush in Australia. 
Hilary felt the need to have both travel and the ability to ‘nest’ at 
home where she would garden, cook and write. She recently said 
she had had a lovely life, and she had – it was a fortunate one 
where she was able to live her dreams with the love of her family 
and friends. 
Above all, Hilary loved people and was able to really see them 
for who they were. She was a good friend and kept those friendships 
going over many decades. She was also a gifted photographer and 
writer. She leaves a legacy of books, on Bali, Africa and our time 
as a family on the Camino, and diaries, letters and articles as well 
as thousands of photographs she printed herself. 
In her late 60s, she and John bought a whole valley in the 
Blue Mountains where they set up and ran the Crucible Centre, 
dedicated to healing the soul. This brought them both great joy, 
as well as spreading joy to many others. 
People will remember Hilary for her kindness and warmth, her 
humour and her wonderful bright clothes, including her handmade 
colourful trousers that she could never have enough of! She loved 
reading and poetry and made the lives of all her family and friends 
brighter for being there. She has left behind her best friend of 73 
years, John, who is so grateful to have been her companion and 
shared in her sense of fun and adventure for all those years.

The Brogo Hotspots Project
The Hotspots Fire Project is an award-
winning program that provides landholders 
and land managers with the skills and 
knowledge to actively participate in fire 
management for the protection of life and 
property while, at the same time, ensuring 
healthy productive landscapes in which 
biodiversity is protected and maintained.
Based on best available science 
and operational knowledge, Hotspots 
understands that well-informed and well-
prepared communities complement the 
roles of land managers and fire agencies. 
A shared approach to fire management 
is critical to any form of planning and 
management for the protection of life, 
property and the environment.
The Hotspots project consists of 
two days of workshops and is free to all 
in the Brogo area. Landholders gain a 
better understanding of fire ecology and 
risk management through spending time 
in the field with ecologists and local fire 
authorities, land managers and neighbours. 
Participants also gain an understanding of 
issues relating to risk planning, legislation, 
fire history, the protection of threatened 
species, vegetation types and animals, 
and their response to fire and different fire 
regimes. 
Landholders develop their own fire 
management plans in cooperation with 
their neighbours and begin to build a better 
understanding of the role of fire in their 
local area. The result is a map-based fire 
management plan of their property.
Landholders also participate in a 
field day event on how to put their 
fire management plans into action and 
implement a burn in a safe and efficient 
environment. The day brings together all 
the knowledge and planning from earlier 
parts of the program and provides a hands-
on experience in preparing a burn site, 
measuring fuel loads, considering weather 
and other aspects of fire behaviour, and 
undertaking risk assessments. Landholders 
will then observe and partake in a 
demonstration burn conducted by the local 
Rural Fire Service Brigade.
Our proven model of consultation, 
science and training has resulted in on-
ground actions for biodiversity, community 
preparedness and improved hazard 
reduction. This workshop will also focus 
on the effect of fire on the local endangered 
population of koala. For more information, 
please contact Inspector Phil Paterson on 
0417 128 815.
The workshops will run on 8 and 
22 November and information will be 
forwarded to registered participants.

Martin Kotvojs died peacefully in his 
sleep on 7 October 2025, aged 96. He had 
seemed invincible – just keeping on going.
Born in Vienna on 1 October 1929, 
Martin’s early life was marked by the 
rise of Nazism. He saw what hate could 
do to people and society, believing such 
evil could exist in any heart if allowed to 
take hold. After Hitler’s 1938 annexation 
of Austria, his family faced persecution 
for his mother’s Jewish heritage. While 
preparing to flee, his father smuggled 
money, valuables and children across 
the border to safety. They were granted 
Australian visas – miraculously delivered 
after surviving a plane crash – but his 
grandmother was denied one and died in 
a concentration camp.
Arriving in Australia on his tenth 
birthday, Martin began his new life in 
Sydney. Often bullied as a ‘reffo’, he met 
challenges with courage and curiosity – 
building bamboo canoes to explore Sydney 
Harbour and once ‘liberating’ a US Army 
landing craft.
His love of farming began on a 
friend’s dairy farm. After he left school, 
he worked as a jackaroo, gained a wool-
classing licence and later joined Moffat 
Virtue, installing shearing equipment 
across NSW. From 1951 to 1957 he served 
in the RAAF Reserve, learning to drive 
trucks and famously riding a 125 CZ 
motorbike around Australia. A magazine 
article on the trip began, ‘The line between 
the brave and the foolhardy is usually a thin 
one. Decide yourself about this character.’ 
It summed him up perfectly – he knew 
no fear.
Martin met his one true love, Dorothy, 
in 1951 at a bushwalking club. Ten years 
later, she realised his tall tales were true and 
agreed to marry him. Honeymooning in 
Dalmeny, they found and bought their farm 
at Dignams Creek. Together they raised me 
and my brother Greg while Martin worked 
tirelessly on and off the farm, running the 
local AI service and driving the school bus 
with Dorothy.
An innovator and community pillar, 
he was the first in NSW to breed Simmental 
cattle, installed solar power and hot-
water decades ago and helped pioneer 
local truffle growing. He served with 
the Dignams Creek Community Group, 
Cobargo Co-op, RFS, P&C and Narooma 
Bushwalking Club.
Known for his storytelling, warmth, 
generosity and moral courage, Martin 
was always first to help – pulling a calf, 
relocating snakes or standing up against 
prejudice. Even at 96, he planned to attend 
the National Day of Australians Against 
Antisemitism. His prayer captured his life: 
Spirit of life and Spirit of love; grant me 
the empathy, the wisdom and the ability 
to help make this world a little better as I 
pass through. He certainly did.

In Term 3, Year 5/6 students from Cobargo Public School 
embarked on an exciting three-day, two-night excursion to 
Australia’s capital, Canberra. This trip provided a valuable 
opportunity for students to immerse themselves in the nation’s 
rich history, government, art and science, through engaging and 
interactive experiences.
The adventure began early on Day 1 with a visit to The Royal 
Australian Mint. Here, students learned about how Australian 
money is produced, gaining insight into the sophisticated 
technology involved. After lunch, the group explored Questacon, 
the National Science and Technology Centre, where hands-on 
exhibits brought science to life, sparking curiosity and wonder. 
The day ended at the Sports and Aquatic Centre where they relaxed 
and played on the water slides.
Day 2 was filled with cultural and civic discoveries. They 
visited The National Portrait Gallery of Australia, where they 
toured galleries and participated in portrait sketching activities. 
This allowed them to appreciate both diverse art styles and 
the stories behind the works. They then explored the National 
Capital Exhibition, learning about Canberra’s history, from the 
First Nations people to the city’s development and iconic tree 
planting. A highlight was the visit to Parliament House, where 
students gained a deeper understanding of Australia’s democratic 
processes. The afternoon included play at the Arboretum Play Pod, 
an imaginative playground inspired by Australian flora.
On the final day, students visited The National Archives, 
viewing important government documents and artefacts that tell 
Australia’s story from 1901 onward. The excursion concluded 
at the Australian War Memorial, deepening students’ awareness 
of the country’s military history through powerful exhibits and 
personal stories.
Overall, the Canberra excursion was an enriching and 
memorable experience for Cobargo’s Year 5/6 students. It 
combined education with adventure, leaving them inspired to 
continue exploring Australia’s history and culture.

It takes a village – to replace a supermarket!
There has been a gaping hole in our village since the closing 
of Cobargo’s supermarket run by the Lohse family and the more 
recent Cobargo General Store. However, as happens in vibrant 
and resourceful communities, individuals and groups move in to 
fill the gap and start to replace what has been lost or is missing. 
So, rather than lamenting what has been lost, let’s recognise 
what is there and what is being gained as there are a number 
of shops and other businesses that are now stocking many 
supermarket products.
Let’s start with the Cobargo Service Station. In addition 
to their automotive items, they have a wide range of foods 
ranging from bread and milk through to canned and packaged 
goods, cheese and associated dairy products, and other non-food 
essentials for around the home.
As proprietors Kepu and Satya said, ‘We are fortunate to 
have good shelving and cold storage space to stock a wide range 
of food and other household requirements. We are also keen to 
increase the range and would welcome any community feedback 
or suggestions.’ The servo is open every day with opening hours, 
6.00 am to 9.00 pm, also a boon.
However, as we take a stroll down our main street we pass 
the bakery with its wide range of baked goods, bread, milk and 
the like. At the other end of the street, we have the butchers 
where David, the butcher, also stocks some non-meat goods 
including bags of locally grown, pesticide free, fresh salad greens. 
In between, we find the Junction Café (now open seven days a 
week), Cheryl’s Personal Support Services with a range of non-
perishable grocery items, local seasonal fresh fruit and veg at 
the Museum, honey at the Information Centre, and the Cobargo 
Co-op and the Bowerbird with other essential household items. 
The Cobargo Hotel also stocks many essential food items and has 
convenient open hours.
And let’s not forget there are always the home bakers or the 
CWA on Saturday mornings in front of Norrie’s garage.
Part of the soon to be completed Rebuild Cobargo Project 
will be a dedicated covered market area, that will be suitable for 
local community food producers or local markets.
All in all, it adds up. There may not be a supermarket or 
general store anymore but there are multiple outlets supplying 
Cobargo-based shoppers and providing vital items for the top-
up shoppers who do more of their bulk grocery and household 
buying out of town.
Glass half full or empty? Half full for me and always filling up.

Cobargo RSL Memorial Hall re-opening 
It’s official! On Saturday, 29 November, the beautifully 
restored Cobargo RSL Memorial Hall will be re-opened!
The formal re-opening will be made by the Hon Kristy 
McBain, MP, at 11.00 am. 
After the opening, all attendees will be invited to inspect our 
wonderful hall, enjoy light refreshments and have an opportunity 
to share their memories of the hall in all the stages of its life. 
The Cobargo RSL Memorial Hall was originally built by the 
community starting in 1949 and finishing in November 1952 when 
it was first officially opened. It served the Sub-branch, its veterans 
and our far south coast community well, until around 2012 when 
it was privately leased. Following community interest to reopen 
it for public use, the building was found to have deteriorated over 
time and subsequently did not meet the standard for reopening 
for general use.
However, following the devastating 2019-2020 Black 
Summer Bushfires, the Sub-branch applied for and was successful 
in obtaining a Federal Government Black Summer Bushfire 
Recovery Grant of some $1 million to undertake the extensive 
restoration and renovation project to return the Hall to community 
and Sub-branch use.
What you see today is a wonderful building extensively and 
sympathetically refurbished by craftsmen under the guidance of 
the Sub-branch’s pro-bono architect, Nigel Dickson of Dickson 
Rothschild. It has resulted in an outstanding community facility 
of which Cobargo can be extremely proud.
The project has also received an additional $100,000 solar 
and battery installation courtesy of the local Cobargo and District 
Energy Transition Group. This will provide uninterruptible 
power, ensuring that the facility is always able to deliver its core 
community refuge benefit in times of emergency.

Cobargo Community Tree Project
As many of you will be aware, in the 
wake of the Black Summer bushfires local 
blacksmiths, Iain Hamilton and Philippe 
Ravenel, began an ambitious memorial project 
to build a commemorative metal tree. In their 
words, ‘We’re going to get everyone in the 
community to come out and forge a leaf and 
develop a sculpture that will last a thousand years.’ 
The project began with Iain and Philippe doing most of the 
initial ‘heavy lifting’ by donating their blacksmith premises and 
skilled labour as well as time spent organising the donation of 
other essential services and materials. 
1500 leaves have already been completed with each one 
bearing the maker’s name but the finished sculpture will need an 
additional 500 to 1000 leaves. In addition to the leaves, the trunk 
and branches of the tree will be constructed in stainless steel 
requiring specialised welding and engineering skills.
To assist the project, the Australian Business Volunteers 
(ABV) will work to support future fundraising.
Three potential sites have been identified in Cobargo and an 
upcoming community consultation activity will ask community 
members to nominate which site they would prefer.
To assist with the required funding, Cobargo’s Bowerbird 
OpShop has donated $10k to enable the project to continue. 
As Bowerbird spokesperson, Trish Olsen, explains, ‘This is an 
important community project which deserves strong community 
support and we encourage community members and organisations 
to get behind it.’ In addition, the Bowerbird will mount a window 
display illustrating some of the finished leaves.
Iain Hamilton adds, ‘When the time comes to get more 
names on leaves, a number of stamping days will be posted on 
our Facebook page indicating where people can come and help 
stamp their name into a pre-made leaf.’ For further information 
keep an eye out on FB, other regular social media platforms and 
The Triangle.

Don Green, President of the Triangle Tool Library, came to 
live in Cobargo 25 years ago when he and his wife were expecting 
a baby. ‘We wanted to raise our children in the country. We were 
living outside of Sydney on five acres, so we started to look at 
five-acre properties for sale, and prices started dropping south of 
Narooma. On our list was Cobargo, Quaama and Candelo, and 
we ended up buying the first house we looked at in Cobargo. Not 
knocking those other places, but we’re both just so glad we chose 
to live in Cobargo because we just love it and we’re not going 
anywhere. We’re actively planning for aging in place and say the 
next move is up the hill, to the cemetery.
‘During the fires our house was saved but the whole property 
burnt. I had spent a year and a half after the Tathra fires preparing 
our property and found reading the Black Saturday inquest 
invaluable too. I expected fires to come the summer before, so I 
got another twelve months of preparation. Fire planning isn’t about 
what you do when the fire comes, it’s about preparing and that’s 
ongoing. We had a lot of water and pumps, two adult children, 
so four able-bodied people, and a fire plan, which we followed 
almost exactly. The only thing we forgot was to let the chooks 
out, but if we had, they would have died.
‘After the fires, some fantastic community brainstorming 
events were held at the Cobargo School of Arts Hall. Very 
professionally run by Deb Summer and Dave Newell, it was just 
very beneficial for our mental health to throw good ideas around 
with people we didn’t know. Together we faced the fact that this 
had happened, and we asked what are we’re going to do about it 
together? I was present when the brilliant tool library idea came 
up. It was just so of the moment, because so many people had lost 
their houses, but also their tools, their sheds, all their equipment. 
And those people are the sort of people who were going to rebuild 
their houses themselves, but could they each go out and buy 
$5,000 worth of tools?
‘I wasn’t involved in the initial set up of the Tool Library, 
because I had my own trauma here. I basically hunkered down for 
two years rebuilding my garden because I was quite dismayed that 
the fire had destroyed 20 years of gardening. For the first couple 
of years, I was borrowing tools occasionally. I didn’t lose any of 
my tools, and I had a pretty good collection but, of course, they 
started to break, and I knew of the tool library so, within a year 
of its existence, I was a member and borrowing tools. And as 
the collection expanded, I realised that I would not have to buy 
another tool in my life. You don’t have to buy tools when you 
can borrow them for a $50 membership per year. What a bargain!
‘Every single person who comes into the library for the first 
time says, “This is just such a brilliant idea – everywhere should 
have one!” And it’s true. I’ve had people from small communities 
as far away as Magnetic Island, Queensland, come in and say, 
“How do we get this?” Why I’m involved now so passionately, is 
because I really believe that this is going to be here for a long time, 
for the life of the town. We’re no longer in the reactionary phase 
of just helping people get back on their feet, we’re transitioning to 
a service, an asset that the town will use and rely on for decades.
‘For those people who aren’t members, or even those that 
are, you might not be aware that we have a woodshed with 
virtually every piece of equipment you need to build furniture, 
window frames, whatever your project is. Steve is there nearly 
every Friday, and he will help you build and make things. We 
run workshops to develop skills with chainsaws or building 
or planting, and we will be reintroducing them after a survey 
has told us what people want. I think a really important part of 
our continuing mission is to educate and build skills within the 
membership.
‘At the moment, we’ve got about 300 members, but I would 
like to see us at 500 by the end of this year, and then probably 
1000 this time next year. It might seem a bit bold but there are 
about 6,000 households so we should have 6,000 members! If 
you’re not a member, you’re losing money and missing out on 
help, on food preserving gear, on all the great new tools we are 
getting all the time.
With more members we will need more volunteers. I’m 
currently putting the horse before the cart and getting volunteers 
first. What I would like to see is the volunteers basically putting 
aside three hours a fortnight, so doing one shift over the Tuesday, 
Friday or Saturday, whatever suits them. That way no one’s going 
to get burned out or stressed out. I personally think we need 
about four or even five people on each shift, because it does get 
busy and there’s other maintenance jobs to do. I want to get our 
volunteer numbers up so that we can move forward to the next 
stage, which is getting more members. If we’re totally inundated 
with volunteers, we could be open every day, but we’ll see.’
The tool library is open Tuesdays, Fridays, Saturdays, 
9.00 am till 12.00 noon. Check out all the tools available on the 
Triangle Tool Library website or come on down and join so you 
can start borrowing!

Progeny of Bermagui’s Lone Pine
The Bermagui War Memorial proudly 
overlooks Horseshoe Bay, not unlike 
Anzac Cove at Gallipoli – a pensive 
location for all to reflect on those young 
men and women who made the ultimate 
sacrifice.
From 1965 to 2021 the Bermagui 
War Memorial housed a keeper of great 
significance, an Aleppo pine grown from 
a seed produced by the Lone Pine on 
the Gallipoli battlefield. That Lone Pine 
witnessed the fierce battle that raged there 
in August 1915.
Devastatingly, disease resulted in 
the Bermagui pine’s removal some 56 
years later. Another Aleppo pine, but 
not of Gallipoli heritage, was planted to 
continue the tradition of maintaining a 
living memorial presence.
I recently visited a secluded country 
churchyard in WA where I found a pine 
with a plaque underneath confirming its 
origin as being from the original Bermagui 
pine. Nearby another plaque remembered 
two local fallen soldiers, one of whom 
was killed near Lone Pine during the 1915 
battle at Gallipoli. He was a soldier of the 
10th Light Horse; this actual battle was 
immortalised in the final scenes of the 1981 
Peter Weir film Gallipoli.
One of their descendants planted the 
Bermagui pine seed in that WA churchyard 
and it now stands as a personal living 
memorial to those two brave young men 
who made the ultimate sacrifice. My plan 
is to propagate a seed from this pine.
This act of compassion by a WA 
family now provides an opportunity for 
the Bermagui RSL to continue the tradition 
of replanting a descendent of the original 
Bermagui pine at the memorial again. A 
significant living remembrance of our first 
major military engagement in World War 
I that forged a lasting national identity 
characterised by qualities like bravery 
and mateship. The Anzac legend, begun 
at Gallipoli, became a fundamental part 
of Australia’s historical narrative. Lest 
we forget.
Micheal Greenwood

An internationally renowned heritage expert says that none 
of the design proposals for replacing Cuttagee Bridge provide an 
appropriate heritage outcome. Professor Richard Mackay AM has 
worked in cultural heritage and planning in Australia for more than 
forty years. The current NSW Heritage Assessment criteria are 
derived from seminal work he undertook in the 1990s.
The Save Cuttagee Bridge group commissioned Professor 
Mackay to provide a professional opinion of the three design 
options that Bega Valley Shire Council has put forward to replace 
Cuttagee Bridge. On 12 November, Bega Valley Shire’s councillors 
are scheduled to vote on which of the three proposals will proceed 
to full design and construction. Cuttagee Bridge is identified by the 
council as a heritage item in its Local Environmental Plan 2013. 
Council commissioned a full heritage assessment in 2021.
Professor Mackay said the bridge had retained many of its 
original characteristics, including being single lane, timber, its visual 
quality and its context of a coastal road. ‘It is part of a sequence 
of historic bridges; having that clattering noise as you cross it and 
having to pause to cross is all part of the experience,’ he said.
He said Council should have allowed its 2021 heritage 
assessment to guide the design parameters, rather than focusing 
solely on a more functional engineering solution. ‘Option 1 is just a 
modern concrete bridge with applied timber decoration,’ Professor 
Mackay said.
Professor Mackay’s report said, ‘… the three concept designs 
do not retain the bridge’s important heritage attributes and should 
not proceed as proposed.’
The Save the Cuttagee Bridge Group called on council and 
its consultants to consider Professor Mackay’s report and intervene 
before the designs are developed further. It said it isn’t too late to 
produce a solution that reinstates heritage values as the central and 
guiding principle of the bridge project.

The power of a patch: Bermagui’s mending movement
I have the joy of co-hosting a CWA mending afternoon every 
few months in Bermagui and let me tell you: rebellion doesn’t 
always look like protest signs and megaphones. Sometimes it 
looks like darning jumpers, hemming pants, threading new elastic 
into a waistband or patching up a down sleeping bag so it can 
live to see another adventure.
In a world built on fast fashion, constant upgrades and 
landfill mountains, sitting around a table with people of all ages 
quietly stitching feels deliciously subversive. Every line of thread 
whispers, ‘We refuse to toss it out.’ Every patched hole declares, 
‘This garment still matters.’
One of my favourite parts of the afternoon isn’t the projects 
themselves, though they’re impressive, but the conversations 
across generations. Young mums learn tricks from grandmothers, 
middle-aged women swap elastic hacks and younger hands help 
older eyes thread needles. These friendships across decades feel 
rare in our modern, compartmentalised lives, yet here they bloom 
over fabric scraps and snacks.
I don’t take that lightly. I have a goal age of 103 and I’m 
convinced that intergenerational friendships are part of the secret 
recipe for longevity. Laughter with someone who’s lived twice 
your years or the spark you get teaching someone to fix a hem, 
that’s the kind of life-giving energy that keeps us held together 
too.
The truth is that mending days aren’t just about saving 
clothes. They’re about refusing a system that tells us we’re only 
as good as what’s new and shiny. They’re about slowing down, 
connecting and finding joy in the imperfect, and recognising that 
care for our garments, our planet and each other is powerful.

Corunna Street, Bermagui.

	 Jim Blanchfield – 3.4.1944 to 9.10.2025
Allen James Blanchfield (Jim) was a true country 
bloke. His mother-in-law, Cath O’Meara, and the whole 
O’Meara clan absolutely adored him. He was a rock-solid 
man, humble and quiet, who lived by honest values that 
spoke louder than words ever could. 
Jim was born on 3 April 1944, the second son born to 
Archibald and Henrietta (Ett) née Hunter. The family, like 
many at the end of WWII, struggled to make ends meet.
When Jim was young, the family lived on a farm at the 
back of Cuttagee Lake and the older boys rode their horses to 
the Murrah School. Jim was only 11 years old when his dad passed 
away suddenly in 1955, leaving Ett a young widow with four 
sons – John 15, Jim 11, Dennis and Danny only 15 months old. 
Ett moved her clan to a Quaama property, The Ville, where 
Jim worked very hard clearing blackberries, wattle and rabbits. 
Ett found companionship, support and love with Cec Dummett 
and they married in 1958. Brian was born the following year and 
was one of my classmates.
Danny remembers his mum being a great cook and always 
having a batch of scones in the oven when friends arrived. John 
and Jim worked side by side in the bush cutting sleepers at 
Dignams – a brutally hard task. 
Jim always knew what he wanted in life. The very pretty 
Catherine O’Meara caught his eye, and he asked her parents for 
her hand in marriage. They married on 20 June 1970. For better 
or worse was never a truer adage. For 55 years, Jim and Cathy 
shared a love that weathered every storm, a partnership built on 
laughter, kindness and unwavering support. 
Jim loved tinkering in his shed and could turn his hand to 
anything. It was automatic – ‘Take it to Jim – he’ll fix it!’ He was 
also a great shot and loved going hunting, whistling up foxes, 
shooting rabbits and ducks. He would also bring home a feed of 
fish or prawns.
Jim’s working life started at the Caltex, north Bega. He then 
tried his hand driving milk tankers. A dedicated, safe operator, 
he was a familiar sight behind the wheel for many years, until 
retirement.
Jim and Cathy were part of the valley’s tapestry. Often seen 
shopping or attending appointments together, visiting the Quaama 
store for paper, fuel or supplies. Jim was Cathy’s main carer and 
drove her everywhere.
They always had a host of pets including two very special 
family members: Jackson, the Rag Doll Burmese cross cat and 
Zenelle, the Maltese-Shitzu.
Once Jim made his mind up there was no turning back. 
After retiring, he set his heart on owning a horse, insisting he 
needed something to fill his time. Cath said she warned him, but 
to no avail. The horse he bought proved to be a rogue and simply 
couldn’t be tamed. Jim tried to get his younger brother Danny to 
ride him, but Danny said, ‘I’ll shoot it before I get on his back!’ 
One fateful day the horse threw Jim and hurt him badly. The 
horse had to go.
Jim enjoyed the simple things in life … a cuppa on the 
veranda watching the sun slip away. He had a sweet tooth so 
there was always a biscuit, slice or cake to wash down with his 
warm brew. During the day you would see Jim munching on his 
lolly stash. 
Jim was kind, thoughtful and reliable. We’ll remember Jim 
for his quiet strength, his warm smile and the way he always put 
others first. He had a huge, generous heart. His legacy isn’t just 
in what he built or fixed, but in the love and respect he earned 
along the journey.
Jim always said he’d ‘go out with a bang’ and, in his own 
way, that’s exactly what he did – leaving us quickly, with no fuss 
and on his own terms.

A legacy in beauty – twenty years of sculpture Bermagui
From the controversial to the 
whimsical, and the powerful to the intricate, 
for twenty years the autumn sculpture 
display at Bermagui has drawn artists and 
crowds from around the region, the nation 
and overseas.
As the event celebrates two decades 
in March 2026, it’s hard to believe the idea 
was once seen as something ‘Bermagui was 
not ready for’.
Visionary creator of the original 
Sculpture on the Edge, Jan Ireland, laughs 
as she recalls the horrified backlash of some 
locals to the idea of displaying sculpture. 
Jan, who ran the event from 2006 until 
2016, says opposition came to a head 
in 2006 when sculptor Richard Moffat, 
a local artist whose work is displayed 
around the country, exhibited a Corten steel 
abstract sculpture entitled The Knot, on the 
Bermagui Oval.
‘It was confiscated by council because 
someone complained it was “a big poo” and 
a comment on a proposed sewerage system 
for the town,’ explains Jan.
With the artwork locked in the council 
depot, Jan talked to a local councillor 
(whose wife was an artist) who had it 
liberated and taken to Four Winds at 
Barragga Bay for exhibition. Subsequently, 
Jan felt vindicated when The Knot took 
out the major prize at Swell Festival at 
Currumbin Beach in Queensland and sold 
for $30,000.
‘I also thought – enough of this – it’s 
consciousness raising time and I’m going 
to give them a sculpture event whether they 
want it or not!’
There have been other controversial 
sculptures: a gorilla nailed to a cross 
representing an existential threat to the 
natural world; and a bronze caricature of 
former PM John Howard in an over-sized 
WWI Diggers’ uniform standing guard at 
Horseshoe Bay. Deemed too political, the 
figure was removed by council staff.
The large sculpture displays at 
Endeavour Point Headland, and the small 
sculpture exhibition in the Surf Club are 
now a highlight in the south east’s cultural 
calendar.
Without Jan (a non-driver, who 
used public transport to visit sculpture 
events, sometimes transport a sculpture 
and convince artists to display at a little-
known event) it’s difficult to imagine 
that the crowds would have come. But 
come they did, with Jan’s passion for art 
and determination drawing in many well-
known sculptors across the years.
Asked why she did it, Jan says apart 
from wanting to celebrate the magnificent 
Bermagui headland with its deep resonance 
with Aboriginal culture, it was to provide 
an experience otherwise unavailable in the 
area. It also encouraged artists, established 
and emerging, and provided local children 
with an introduction to sculpture. ‘The 
artists were family, in a way. It is lifeblood 
to me.’
From a once-sceptical community, 
the renamed Sculpture Bermagui is now 
a beloved annual event drawing hundreds 
of artists and many more inquisitive locals 
and visitors to the town every March. The 
community-run event that Jan stepped 
back from in 2016, is powered by a group 
of devoted volunteers and the ongoing 
patronage of architect Philip Cox and 
author and journalist Janet Hawley, and 
many local businesses and individuals.
Paul Payten, who took the reins after 
2016, organised a committee to ensure the 
incredible event continued, ‘The first year 
we ran it, we had 110 exhibitors, including 
leading sculptor Michael Le Grand, who’d 
exhibited at Sculpture by the Sea [Sydney], 
and around 1100 visitors.’ 
Like Jan, Paul, who stepped back after 
five years, is still passionate about the event, 
cherishing it as a gift to artists to express 
themselves and expand their thinking.
Sculpture Bermagui will run from 
March 7-15, 2026.

Waterwise wicking 
Rather than risking overwatering our indoor plants and 
having water spill out of their saucers, we water them from 
below, filling the saucer and letting the soil absorb the water. 
If the plant is really dry, this needs to be repeated several times 
until an equilibrium is reached, with some water remaining in 
the saucer to be slowly taken up over the ensuing days. This 
same principle underpins wicking beds, an ingenious gardening 
system that delivers water directly to plant roots while conserving 
water and promoting healthy growth. It’s ideal for vegetables and 
flowers alike.
The idea of watering from below is not new. Ancient 
civilisations harnessed sub-surface moisture to irrigate crops long 
before modern engineering formalised the concept. In ancient 
Egypt, farmers cultivated crops on the Nile floodplains, where the 
water table remained near the surface, allowing roots to access 
moisture through capillary rise.
The modern wicking bed is largely an Australian innovation 
by engineer and sustainability educator Colin Austin in the late 
1990s. Facing water scarcity and soil degradation in Queensland, 
Colin developed a system combining a sealed water reservoir 
with a coarse aggregate layer covered by a geotextile barrier 
and growing soil above. His design stored water below the soil, 
drawing it upward as needed, drastically reducing watering 
frequency while improving plant health.
Building an effective wicking bed requires attention to a 
few key principles. The bottom layer forms the water reservoir 
and almost any watertight container can work – bathtubs, laundry 
tubs, IBC containers, large plastic bins or even wine barrels. 
The reservoir is typically filled with gravel, above which sits a 
geotextile barrier to stop soil from filtering down while allowing 
moisture to wick upward. A critical design consideration is the 
ratio of reservoir depth to soil depth, usually 1:2. For instance, a 
200 mm reservoir should be covered with around 400 mm of soil. 
Other essential features include an inlet pipe to refill the reservoir, 
an overflow outlet to prevent waterlogging and a drainage outlet 
to empty if required. There are also excellent Australian-made 
prefabricated options available, such as Foodcube, Wickwell, 
WaterUps and ModBOX.
It’s worth noting that wicking beds differ from hydroponic 
systems. Hydroponics bypass soil entirely, suspending roots in 
nutrient solutions that require close monitoring of oxygenation, 
pH and nutrient levels. Wicking beds, in contrast, retain soil as the 
medium, using the reservoir to supply the water (and sometimes 
fertiliser) through capillary action. They combine the best of 
both worlds: the biological richness of soil with the efficiency of 
sub-surface watering, essentially a self-watering flowerpot on a 
larger scale.
Meanwhile, our towns gardens are alive with spring colour in 
every shade and shape. November also brings one of our favourite 
colour combinations in the canopy as Brachychiton acerifolius 
(Illawarra flame tree) – red, Jacaranda mimosifolia (jacaranda) 
– purple, and Grevillea robusta (silky oak) – golden-yellow all 
begin their spectacular flowering shows. If you’re lucky, you 
may see all three together, though even two side by side is a treat.
November remains one of the best months for planting a 
wide variety of vegetables such as beans, capsicum and chillies, 
corn, cucumber, eggplant, melon, pumpkin, tomato and zucchini. 
Continue planting and tending beetroot, carrot, lettuce, onions, 
potatoes and silver beet.

A trio of custards
Custard is part of most people’s childhoods – served with 
bottled fruit, or a slice of cake or pie, dressed up with brandy for 
Christmas pudding. Comfort.
A simple pouring custard is silky, not too sweet and as 
rich as your heart and liver allow! For an everyday custard use 
whole milk and whole eggs, go for cream and egg yolks for a 
ridiculously rich and sumptuous dish or coconut milk to avoid 
dairy. Each of these alternatives follows the same pattern, there 
are just shifts in ratios and ingredients.

4 large eggs (5 smaller ones)

Chicken soup 
Chicken soup is the ultimate warm hug in food form – known 
to address fraught times, heart break, grief for our loved ones, the 
planet, our vulnerable communities.
Making stock
Chicken soup, so simple, nourishing and comforting, starts 
with chicken stock.
First, save your raw chicken carcases – wrap them up and 
freeze until you have two, three or more. If you want to boost the 
lovely silky quality of the stock buy a bag of wing tips and/or 
chicken necks to add to the pot.
When you have enough, place them in a large stock pot of cold 
water and add chunks of carrots, celery stalks and leaves, onions, 
a bunch of parsley, some bay leaves and black peppercorns (do 
not add salt at this point – season your soup or sauces to your taste 
when you cook them). Bring to a boil, then simmer for about three 
hours, skimming off the frothy scum a couple of times. 
You now have delicious stock to chill, remove the fat and 
transfer to your freezer. Put it in multiple containers so you can 
have it ready in useful quantities for soups, sauces or stews – label 
and add the date. Discard the exhausted bones and veg.
Nourishing soups for body and soul
Pull out your block of frozen stock (or use ready-made stock 
or cubes).
Chicken vegetable soup – Start by cooking diced onions 
and garlic in olive oil, add sliced carrots, celery, potatoes and any 
other vegetables you feel like adding (fennel, parsnips, swedes, 
corn kernels, whatever). Once the veg are softened and aromatic, 
add herbs of your choice, pour in your stock, bring to a simmer 
and add any green veg you might like (green beans, baby peas, 
cabbage, silver beet, whatever). Season with salt and black pepper 
and serve with crusty bread.
Minestrone – As above but after all the veg, add cooked dried 
beans (borlotti or kidney beans work well) and some chunky pasta. 
Top with grated parmesan. 
For Asian chicken noodle soup – Stick with Asian aromatics 
starting with garlic and ginger, some soy sauce and Chinese 
cooking wine, and a little sesame oil. Pour boiling water over some 
thin rice vermicelli noodles (or Hokkien, ramen or any others), 
wilt some bok choi (or other greens). Put greens and noodles into 
a bowl and pour hot stock over them. Add shredded chicken (if 
you want), some chilli paste if you’re looking for some fire and 
some finely sliced spring onions (or not).
