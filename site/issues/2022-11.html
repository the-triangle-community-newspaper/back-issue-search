I accidentally became the Secretary of the Dignams Creek 
Community Group Inc due to a jolly mood and a warm winter fire 
at our June AGM. After putting up my hand, the outgoing secretary 
leapt up instantly to bring me a bag of books and papers. What 
treasure I found there! Many different styles of handwriting have 
carefully recorded the projects and concerns of Diggies residents 
in minutes dating back to 1995, featuring names of people still 
here, and others long gone. The Diggies community has a long 
history of fun through koala surveys, bush picnics, the finding 
of rare plants (by Vernon Pretty), guest speakers on all kinds of 
issues and fundraisers that built our fire shed. Some of the unfun 
seems perennial too – phone line dissatisfaction, feral animals, 
bushfire preparation and wrestling with two councils about the 
state of our road.
Continuing the fun part, an idea came to Nick van Stek and 
me that we called ‘Feed Diggies’. As prices go up and it’s virtually 
necessary to take out a mortgage to buy an Iceberg lettuce, we 
thought it might be great to share skills and tools to increase the 
productivity of our gardens. We also wanted to find a way to swap 
and share veggies, eggs, bread, preserves, seedlings or help each 
other with fencing or building chook yards. We shared our idea 
through the fire-tree contact list, diligently kept by Fiona Kotvojs, 
and twenty people said YES, PLEASE! At our first gathering we 
realised that among the group, we had about 68 hours of labour 
per month, a giant list of skills in all kinds of food growing and 
animal husbandry departments, and a very generous combined 
tool shed, including four tractors. We decided to do a monthly 
working bee at someone’s place, and to share a meal afterwards, 
bringing anything we had in excess for swapping and sharing.
At our first get together at Shanti’s and Sol’s place, we started 
building the Diggies Share Shed from materials donated from 
several households, learning a bunch of Nick’s building tricks. 
It was a great first meal too, heavy on the egg-based dishes, and 
two seriously good cakes. We will soon finish the shed and use 
it to share our fruit and veggies, plants and eggs with each other, 
and sell to the wider community on an honesty basis. Duck in 
next time you visit Diggies and see what goodies you can find!

significant operating surplus
Council has just released their draft 
financial statements for the year ended 30 
June 2022 that show a net operating surplus 
of $14.9m. This result is after non-cash 
depreciation of $24.4m, but before capital 
grant revenue of $43m.
Income includes rates and annual 
charges of $56.3m which have increased 
4.4% over the year, reflecting a strong 
increase in Council’s rating base. Council 
has benefited to some degree from a one-
off increase in disaster recovery grant 
funding that was paid early to councils as 
part of the economic support provided due 
to the pandemic. 
Council is still considering a 35% 
increase in our general rates from 1 July 
2023 to ensure that Council finances can 
meet the expectation of the level of services 
that the community needs.
Council minutes of 4 May 2022 
note that the Long Term Financial Plan 
2023-2032 (scenario 2) models a single 
year special variation to rates of 35% 
permanently applied to the rate base 
commencing 1 July 2023. 
The Council CEO, Anthony 
McMahon, subsequently clarified that the 
35% proposed rate increase only relates to 
your base rates, not to your water, sewer 
and waste rates. This means for most, the 
overall ongoing rates are proposed to be 
increased by between 10% and 20%.
It is important to stress that no 
decision has yet been taken by Council in 
this matter.
In its long-term strategic plan, Council 
was assuming a population increase per 
annum of only 0.65% over the next twenty 
years.
The recent census results for the 
shire show the population has increased 
by 5.4% and occupied private dwellings 
have increased by 10% in just the five 
years to 2021.
Based on this information, Council 
should review their strategic and financial 
plans to ensure that the population and 
housing growth that is occurring is taken 
into consideration.

On Sunday 14 August, in partnership 
with the Cobargo Bushfire Resilience 
Centre Inc. (CBRC), representatives from 
the National Library of Australia visited 
Cobargo and Quaama to speak with locals 
about the National Library’s Badja Forest 
Road Fire oral history project. 
Record your story about the fire
Working with the CBRC and 
members of the affected communities, this 
project hopes to capture sixty oral history 
interviews as an enduring historical record 
of those who lived through the fire and 
its aftermath. 
Rhonda Ayliffe, Vice-chair of the 
CBRC, opened both events and Dr 
Shirleene Robinson, National Library 
Director of Curatorial and Collection 
Research, and Scott McKinnon, an expert 
oral historian who works with the National 
Library, spoke about the project and 
answered questions from the community. 
A third community information 
session will be held in Nerrigundah at 
11.00 am on Sunday 27 November at the 
Nerrigundah Agricultural Bureau Inc. 
Community Shed. The Library understands 
the need to represent the full diversity of 
experiences through interviews.
About oral history 
The National Library has the largest 
oral history collection in Australia, 
covering many eras and subjects. Oral 
histories are carefully structured audio 
recordings where an interviewee works 
with an oral historian to tell their own 
story in their own words. It’s entirely up 
to the interviewee what is shared in an 
interview, and interviewees get to decide 
on the access rights (that is, who gets to 
listen to the recording). 
For more information about the 
project or to express your interest in 
participating, please visit the National 
Library of Australia website and search 
for ‘Badja Forest Road Fire’. 
The recordings will be kept forever in 
the National Library. 
This project has been supported by the 
NSW Government Bushfire Community 
Recovery and Resilience Fund.

In August, U3A Bermagui members 
attended a talk by Garry and Paul Campbell 
from the Wallaga Lake community. 
Garry and Paul are identical twins. They 
were born here and joined a long line of 
Campbells in our area, with official records 
(white colonial) dating back to 1848.
We learnt a lot about local indigenous 
history, the history of the now Wallaga 
Lake Koori Village, previously The 
Mission, of the present conditions and 
of the concerns regarding past, present 
and future developments encroaching on 
Indigenous heritage without consultation.
In September we went walking 
with Garry, Paul and their wives, Miriam 
and Marian. We were also joined by the 
delightful Patrick.
It was cold and wet but that didn’t 
dampen the joy. We started at the Fairhaven 
Point midden, the biggest in southern 
Australia, estimated to be six metres 
deep and used for millennia. It is an 
extraordinary asset to archaeologists, 
the local community and tourism. And 
Patrick read us his beautiful poem about 
Wallaga Lake.
We then went to Camel Rock. More 
talking, more questions, more gems of 
ancient knowledge.
Our last stop was the Bermagui sacred 
waterhole. The site is severely neglected 
and overgrown with exotic weeds so no 
access can be gained and the signage 
cannot be read. There is also a block of 
units encroaching on one side, despite 
objections from the Yuin people. This 
situation is very frustrating for the Yuin 
people and the Campbell family who were 
the traditional ‘Watchers’ and who whole-
heartedly wish for it to be restored but have 
not been able to do so.
It is heartbreaking to see such a 
significant site, gazetted as an Aboriginal 
Place in 2009, with a long and important 
indigenous history, so neglected.
Members of U3A have been following 
up with Council, the National Parks and 
Wildlife Service and Crown Lands in an 
attempt to understand how this situation 
can be rectified.
Merrimans Land Council has now 
granted permission to begin restoration 
work and U3A look forward to 
this occurring.

Business innovation
Native forests are a very familiar part of life on the Far 
South Coast. However, we also have native marine forests of 
which Golden Kelp is a major part – its natural habitat extends 
from northern NSW, down the east coast, across all the Australian 
south coast and up the west coast to Geraldton, an area which is 
known as the Great Southern Reef. These massive kelp forests 
are in decline due to a combination of factors that include rising 
ocean temperatures and, particularly in this region, an increase 
in sea urchin numbers. Sea urchins feed on young kelp and there 
appears to be a decrease in the numbers of mid-sized fish and 
lobster, the natural predators of sea urchins. Kelp farming is 
considered an environmentally sound way to reverse some of these 
trends while potentially reducing ocean acidification, improving 
local biodiversity and improving the overall environment by 
storing carbon. 
In March 2021, I wrote about the work that Jo Lane and 
Warren Atkins are doing at their state-of -the-art kelp laboratory 
at Tilba Tilba. This is the next chapter in their journey. Following 
a Churchill Fellowship trip in 2019 to research kelp farming 
overseas, Jo and Warren have been developing ways to create 
a kelp seedbank – the first step in kelp farming. To their credit 
and with a lot of hard work, they have successfully established a 
process to produce baby kelp – the only non-university entity in 
Australia currently doing this. The fascinating aspect of this is that 
the kelp seedbank, made up of tiny male and female gametophytes, 
can be held in limbo for years under red light, increasing in density 
but not reproducing. Once placed under white light they will 
reproduce and attach to fine marine-grade string, all done under 
strict laboratory conditions. When these spores grow to a certain 
size, they are ready to be farmed. Jo is currently working with 
NSW Marine Parks on pilot Golden Kelp restoration projects that 
include a better understanding of sea urchin behaviour.
Kelp farming has many possibilities in the circular economy. 
Apart from its well-known health benefits – which is the basis of 
Jo’s primary kelp business, Sea Health Products – it can be used 
in cosmetics, can be made into plastic, makes wonderful fertiliser 
and has great potential for flooring and building materials and for 
plant-based meat. 
However, to start a kelp farm requires fortitude and financial 
resources to navigate a complex and not easily understood 
regulatory framework. Jo has successfully won the tender for 
three marine areas – Bermagui, Pambula and, more recently, 
Eden. To get the required permits for farming though, requires an 
Environmental Impact Statement at an estimated cost of $400,000 
and, further, is deemed to be a State Significant Development 
with additional processes to fulfil. These requirements are 
similar to what is required of a large multinational company 
and seem grossly excessive for a proposal that has the potential 
to add great value to our marine environment, with little if any 
negative effects. Rather, the regulatory process does not encourage 
investment or innovation to the point where a business, like Jo’s 
and Warren’s, may seek to move to another jurisdiction that has 
a more supportive and financially achievable process, such as 
that found in South Australia. This means the potential loss of a 
first – kelp farming in the ocean – for NSW.
Jo is doing all she can to comply – seeking funding, making 
contact with people who may be able to assist and talking to local 
politicians. Federal MP Kristy McBain visited the day before my 
visit and was supportive and encouraging and, as it is primarily

Friends from far and wide gathered on 24 September in the 
Small Hall at Central Tilba to pay tribute to our recently departed 
friend, John Small.
A fascinating exhibition of his artwork, chalkboards, 
photography, signwriting, poster and banner art, cartoons, clever 
words and dry wit was on display in the Small Hall, showing 
us all how enormously prolific John was and how he excelled 
at everything he turned his hand and mind to. The display 
demonstrated how intrinsically involved John has been in the life 
and fabric of the Tilba district over four decades including his 
seminal role with local 1990s band The Predicaments, his tireless 
work for the Tilba Festival and School of Arts, the creation of a 
time capsule and photos of the children at both the Tilba Primary 
School and the Tilba Stiltzkidz.
Many fine words were spoken including a précis of an article 
written for the National Library of Australia by local historian 
Laurel Pacey featuring John’s social contribution through 
photographing the life and times of the community since the 
early 1980s. He is recognised as the contemporary equivalent of 
William Henry Corkhill.
An old friend, Phil Hall, reminisced about John as a young 
man in 1977 in Canberra where he was a founding member of a 
motorbike club. Phil recalled how John had snapped a famous 
moment in motor bike history that is still seen throughout the 
world today. A mellow peaty whiskey was proffered by long-
time friend, Waz, and we all raised a glass to our mate. He too 
shared many funny and poignant stories about John. Others who 
paid tribute included Sean Burke, Viv Bowe, Sol Clarke and the 
writers of this article.
Our dear friend Smally succeeded in balancing his internal 
and worldly discontents with an heroic output of creativity, flair, 
generosity of spirit and a uniquely irreplaceable sense of humour. 
Much laughter was heard, tears were shed and many a 
moment remembered. Vale John.

Considerable support for nominating the Tilba district as 
a Biosphere Reserve emerged from a recent public forum of 
over seventy people in Central Tilba on Monday 17 October. 
Jointly organised by Tilba Environment Landcarers (TEL) and 
the Landscape sub-committee of the Tilba Strategic Plan chaired 
by Mark Stubbings, the forum considered the implications of 
putting the region forward for accreditation by UNESCO as a 
Biosphere Reserve.
Biosphere Reserves are areas of terrestrial, marine 
and coastal ecosystems managed with the aim of balancing 
biodiversity, conservation and the sustainable use of natural 
resources. There are 738 Biosphere Reserves in 134 countries, 
with five already operating in Australia. Geoff Pryor, President of 
TEL, said, ‘Our aim was to consider how the region’s landscape, 
culture and community could be protected by being declared as 
a Biosphere Reserve.
‘Judging from the positive responses to the presentations and 
in general discussion, there was strong support for this challenging 
idea. This is encouraging, as developing a nomination is a time-
consuming process requiring extensive community engagement 
and consideration of the values we want to protect in our region.’ 
Garry Campbell of the Merrimans Local Aboriginal Land 
Council began the forum with a Welcome to Country.
Forum keynote speaker, Professor Peter Bridgewater of the 
University of Canberra and Australian National University, has 
thirty years of experience working on Biosphere Reserves around 
the world and in Australia. 
Professor Bridgewater said, ‘Biosphere Reserves ensure local 
communities are fed, kept healthy, make room for nature and build 
better, stronger community networks based on accepted values. 
These structures are not another layer of governance.’ 
David Moore, Manager of Environment and Sustainability 
Policy at Queensland’s Sunshine Coast Council, who joined 
the meeting by video conference, shared the Sunshine Coast 
Council’s journey to be recognised as Australia’s most recent 
Biosphere Reserve in June 2022. After considering several 
different national and international mechanisms, UNESCO’s 
Man and the Biosphere Program was identified by the Council 
as being best placed to support further recognition of their vision 
of the region’s sustainability and strategic directions, values, 
credentials and future initiatives. He pointed out that the process 
took several years and required significant resources to both 
prepare a nomination and undertake planning for implementation, 
in which the involvement of the community and local First Nations 
peoples was critical. 
Eurobodalla Shire Councillor, Anthony Mayne, attended the 
meeting along with three senior Council managers and provided 
an overview of relevant policies and actions taken by the Council, 
indicating that there is a keen interest in the concept. 
It is anticipated that steps will be initiated within weeks to 
take the Biosphere Reserve concept further. For more information 
contact Geoff Pryor, TEL President, on 0428 930 196.

When Tammie Gee (née Corby) was 
four years old, she was at her father’s 
butchery in Cobargo. While putting meat 
into a century-old mincing machine, 
young Tammie’s left hand was caught 
and it immediately became trapped. Local 
emergency services couldn’t devise a way 
to safely extract the mangled hand, so they 
rang the police rescue squad. The upshot 
was that a plan was made to dismantle the 
mincer from its base and Tammie, with 
mincer attached, was flown to the Prince 
of Wales Children’s Hospital in Randwick. 
There they laid Tammie on a mattress 
outside, erected a tarpaulin so she couldn’t 
see anything and proceeded, with great 
care, to cut along the length of the mincer’s 
outer casing using a quickcut tool. It took 
almost an hour and involved the careful 
use of a large industrial cutting implement, 
hosed down regularly to minimise the heat 
generated by the cutting from burning 
her arm. The final phase involved using 
a hammer and chisel along the cut, an 
equally difficult task that required the use 
of force. 
With the mincer finally off, Tammie 
was rushed into the hospital for what would 
be over seven hours of microsurgery. Apart 
from losing the tip of one of her fingers, 
Tammie has been able to regain the 
mobility of her hand.
Talking to Tammie about it, she says 
she has very few memories of the event 
but can remember the flight and telling 
them that her arm was heavy – it had a 
mincer attached!
The mincer ended up in the Rescue 
and Bomb Disposal Unit Museum.

Bermagui artist, Bethany Thurtell, has 
a vision to paint a portrait of every member 
of our community. Knowing how many 
of us still yearn to have our stories heard, 
Bethany is moved to capture a tapestry 
of our beautiful diverse community, in 
the form of a series of portraits. Bethany 
is a nationally-renowned portrait painter 
who is volunteering her time to create this 
project. Funding for materials has been 
granted from the Cobargo Community 
Bushfire Recovery Fund. 
‘My aim is to paint the portraits of 
people affected by the fires in the Cobargo 
area and record their stories. [It is] such a 
privilege to listen to the stories and spend 
time with these wonderful humans.’
If you were a resident of Cobargo, 
Wandella, Yowrie, Coolagolite, Dignams 
Creek, Quaama or Brogo at the time of 
the Badja Forest Fire, you qualify for the 
grant project funding. If you are interested, 
Bethany would love to paint your portrait. 
All ages welcome. 
When I was lucky enough to see the 
first few portraits, I was touched by how 
the essence of familiar faces radiated 
from the portraits. I would love to see 
Bethany’s vision of representing our whole 
community be realised. 
If you’d like, you can have your story 
recorded while you sit for your portrait. 
This is completely optional and Bethany 
is guided by her subjects when painting. 
You will not be asked to talk about the fire. 
Portrait sessions take between one 
and two hours. These are held at Bethany’s 
studio in Bermagui, or she would be happy 
to travel to you. The session includes 
a portrait sketch, photos and optional 
story recording.

website, just google Bethany Art. This is 
a truly special opportunity to have your 
portrait painted by an incredible local artist.

Cobargo community, and the portraits will 
be on display locally early next year. You 
will also receive a free, high-quality print 
of the work.

Georgina Adamson
I have been musing lately on what a wonderful place 
Bermagui is to grow up in, a natural paradise where fresh air and 
a clean environment is taken for granted. Having seen my own 
children, and now my grandchildren, going through the various 
stages of schooling here, I have nothing but praise for the way 
these institutions respond and adapt to meet the ever-changing 
needs of kids in the world today. What a great job our educators 
are doing as well as all those who support them
Recently I attended a wonderful event, Kaleidoscope 
staged at Bermagui Public School – a celebration of place and 
people through performance and art. It was wonderful to see the 
children proudly and enthusiastically welcoming their family 
and friends into the school to view their work and show off their 
school. The rain gods smiled and it was a perfect balmy late 
afternoon to be greeted with a dance ‘spectacular’ with the whole 
school participating.
As well as displays in all corners of the school, the hall was 
set up as an exhibition space, full of the children’s work from 
painting, drawing and sculpture to interactive displays. Workshops 
were set up in some classrooms and it was great to see parents and 
children working together at various activities. There was a great 
buzz around the school and the next day people were excited about 
what a marvellous event it had been. I am continually amazed also 
with the programs the Preschool offers reflecting and responding 
to whatever is happening in the wider community in a safe and 
nurturing environment. I love to visit this happy and busy place, 
and I’m always amazed at the vibrancy of the indoor and outdoor 
space. The inclusion of the Moodji Garden is a natural extension 
of the preschool community and provides such a rich learning 
experience, about much more than just growing food. I have great 
respect for the dedication of the staff and the energy that they put 
into their roles as educators, as well as the input of a whole team 
of support staff and volunteers and the special skills of people 
from the wider community.
What’s the old adage? It takes a village to raise a child.

On 25 November, Four Winds will present the Australian 
String Quartet (ASQ) at the Windsong Pavilion. The ASQ is Dale 
Barltrop (violin), Francesca Hiew (violin), Christopher Cartlidge 
(viola) and Michael Dahlenburg (cello).
The ASQ has created unforgettable string quartet performances 
for audiences around the world since 1985. Dedicated to musical 
excellence with a distinctly Australian flavour, they create 
chemistry and amplify intimacy through experiences that connect 
people with the string quartet repertoire. 
The Quartet will perform a diverse and dynamic range of 
repertoire that conveys the potency of classical music of today 
when in their hands. The musical journey will move through a 
triptych of miniatures from an exciting generation of Australian 
voices – equal parts animated, reflective and atmospheric. The 
night will culminate with Shostakovich’s String Quartet No. 9 – a 
vibrant work of masterful contrast and counterpoint that conjures 
up ecstatic moments, brimming with energy.
ASQ’s distinct sound is enhanced by a matched set of 18th 
century Guadagnini instruments, handcrafted by Giovanni Battista 
Guadagnini between c.1743 and 1784 in Turin and Piacenza, 
Italy. These precious instruments are on loan for their exclusive 
use through the generosity of UKARIA, an extraordinary South 
Australian cultural centre founded by Ulrike Klein AO. 
‘A diverse array of sonorous string sounds – inviting listeners 
on a journey to reflect and connect,’ says Matthew Hoy, Four 
Winds Artistic and Creative Director.

When Kelly Ryner settled into the 
beautiful community of Cobargo in 2020, 
just a few months after the fires, as the trees 
started turning from black back to green, 
she was inspired to paint the returning of 
our precious wildlife.
‘It was witnessing nature’s rebirth all 
around me that made me realise that for this 
next chapter of my life I wanted to dedicate 
my time to wildlife conservation,’ she 
explains. ‘Watercolour is such a delicate 
medium, it’s the perfect fit for both feathers 
and fur.’
Known for her soulful animal 
portraits, Kelly’s watercolour paintings 
have received international critical acclaim. 
Most recently she was shortlisted for the 
Wildlife Artist of the Year Award by the 
David Shepheard Wildlife Foundation for 
her painting of Millie, a beloved kangaroo 
at the Cobargo Wildlife Sanctuary. 
Having spent most of 2022 raising 
a couple of orphaned joeys, Kelly was 
inspired to write and illustrate the children’s 
picture book, Maxie Roo is just like you! 
which will also be launched in November. 
Funny and heart-warming, this rhyming 
picture book filled with classic illustrations 
and fun facts about kangaroos is the perfect 
bedtime read for young children.
‘Developing this book has been a 
wonderful journey,’ she noted. ‘Adjusting 
my portrait style to tell stories and adding 
old-fashioned dip pen lining to my 
watercolour process has really helped me 
grow as an artist.’ 
Maxie Roo Is Just Like You! will be 
available at the Lazy Lizard Gallery for the 
month of November along with her original 
watercolours and museum-quality prints of 
Australian wildlife. Kelly has a website as 
well as an Instagram account for those who 
want to explore more about her and her art.

Bodies of Light 
Jennifer Down 
Text Publishers, $32.99
This novel won the 2022 Miles 
Franklin Award and was short listed for the 
Stella Prize. Bodies of Light was praised 
by reviewers and compared to A Little 
Life by Hanya Yanagihara and Douglas 
Stuart’s Shuggie Bain so I was excited to 
read it but aware that it may be confronting. 
Maggie has escaped her life in Australia 
and changed her name twice but an email 
from a childhood foster-care friend who 
is trying to find her throws her into the 
memories she has tried to forget and thus 
begins her account of a tragic life in the 
form of a memoir. 
Maggie is an unreliable narrator, 
sometimes lucid, even brutal, at other times 
foggy and hazy. Whatever the mood, dark 
or light, the writing is beautifully evocative 
and true. 
We follow Maggie’s life from the 
age of four in 1973 to the present. She is 
first abused at four by a family friend and 
from five years old is in a Children’s Home 
or placed in foster care. Most of these 
are unloving or even cruel and Maggie 
is again abused by her foster father. This 
novel shows the gross inadequacy of out-
of-home care in Australia and, according 
to the Royal Commission, not much has 
changed since the 1970s.
We think that Maggie may have 
found happiness in a loving marriage, but 
any happiness is marred because Maggie 
keeps her past secret, even from those she 
loves and so can never be her real self. 
She acknowledges to the reader that she 
doesn’t know who she really is. This theme 
of love lost because so much is kept hidden 
continues through the novel.
Further tragedy and loss come, and 
come, and come some more. There is 
a resemblance to the Kathleen Folbigg 
case, and it is probably here that I stopped 
believing in this novel. 
The novel is written in the first person 
as a memoir, so we only have Maggie’s 
voice and thoughts and, for me (and I 
suspect I’m in a minority here), it started to 
strike a false note. The author interviewed 
many people from similar backgrounds to 
Maggie’s but, as a fictionalised memoir, it 
didn’t entirely hit me in the solar plexus as 
it should have. And, again, that may just 
be me. I think a third person view would 
have served her better.
It is a beautifully written novel of 
tragedy and, finally, quiet survival.
Bronx

Everything Cajun
Years ago, I was researching for my thesis on Cajun music, 
and I discovered that Cajun food was as interesting (and delicious) 
as the music …
Jambalaya can also be made with seafood. It is thought to 
have been introduced to the Cajuns by local Indian tribes. Maque 
choux is a side dish, often served with jambalaya.
Maque choux
Ingredients: 
	
8 ears of corn (or  2 x 420 gm tins of corn kernels)
	
1 teaspoon unsalted butter
	
1 teaspoon vegetable oil
	
½ brown onion finely chopped
	
½ green capsicum finely chopped
	
½ teaspoon white pepper
	
½ teaspoon cayenne pepper
	
250gm chopped tomatoes
	
Salt to taste
	
1½ tablespoons whipping cream
Method:
1.	
Cut kernels from the corn cobs (if using). In a frying pan, heat 
oil and butter and cook onions and capsicum until softened. 
Add corn and peppers, cooking until corn starts to stick to 
the pan, about five to seven minutes.
2.	
Add tomatoes, salt and cream. Cook until thick, about eight 
to ten minutes.
3.	
Serve hot as a side for the jambalaya.
Chicken and ham jambalaya
Ingredients: 
	
1 kg chicken wings (bone in and cut into ‘buffalo’ wings)
	
3 cups chicken stock
	
500 g smoked ham, cubed (or chorizo will do nicely)
	
2 cups chopped cajun vegetables
	
2 cloves garlic
	
2 tablespoons vegetable oil
	
425g can chopped tomatoes
	
2½ cups long grain rice
	
2 teaspoons cajun spice mix
Method:
1.	
Fry chicken with ham in the oil in batches until browned. 
Add cajun vegetables and garlic, and cook until golden.
2.	
Add tomatoes, rice, chicken stock, cajun spice mix and 
bring to boil.
3.	
Pour into greased casserole dish, bake at 180º C for about 
one hour. The rice should be slightly crispy on top.

With the ever-increasing use of 
our outdoor spaces, the use of pots as a 
solution for growing plants is becoming 
far greater than in previous times, and the 
opportunity is right to offer some hints 
on how to successfully manage this form 
of gardening.
Probably the most important initial 
issue is to decide what do you want the 
pots and the plants to achieve? Are they to 
be used for growing vegetables, providing 
annual colour, for perennials or shrubs or, 
perhaps, a screening plant to hide a shed 
or some unsightly part of the landscape?
The pot size is absolutely the most 
crucial selection as a pot too small for the 
plant will cause it to dry out quickly, blow 
over in the wind or not be large enough to 
contain sufficient nutrients and water to 
keep the plant healthy.
Generally, glazed pots are the best 
as there are many colours that suit the 
colour scheme of the home, and these pots 
have a better moisture-holding capability 
than other styles. There are some very 
interesting forms of light-weight pots on 
the market now and these are easy to use as 
they are very light and come in some very 
interesting shapes and sizes. Terracotta pots 
are very stylish but tend to dry out, so if 
this style is to be used, seal the inside of 
the pot with a terracotta sealant.
When selecting a pot that might be 
used to gradually enlarge to the next size 
as the plant grows use a straight-sided or 
vee-shaped pot so the plant can be easily 
removed. A pot which is belly shaped 
is nigh on impossible to repot without 
causing serious disturbance to the root 
system or breaking the pot.  
When selecting the plant to be used 
consider the eventual size of the plant, 
remembering that the larger the plant 
grows, the larger the pot should be. A plant 
that is to be used for screening should be 
in a pot that will also act as an anchor in 
a windy site. Too small a pot will simply 
blow over and, in most cases, break.
If you are growing annuals, herbs and 
vegetables, a shallow pot will suffice as 
they are all generally shallow rooting and 
do not need a great depth of soil.   
The potting mix used should be a 
premium mix including some slow-release 
fertiliser and some water storage crystals. 
There are many types of mixes out there but 
the cheaper the mix, the lesser the quality. 
Cheaper mixes are basically just pine bark 
and if you are growing vegetables and 
annual flowers you will need to fertilise 
and water more often as these mixes do 
not have any moisture or nutrient holding 
capabilities. The cheaper mixes will be 
of no benefit to the plants after a season 
or two.
Watering and fertilising are critically 
important. Vegetables and annual flowers 
will need watering several times a week 
and require fertilising every ten days or 
so with a liquid fertiliser like a fish- or 
seaweed-based fertiliser. Shrubs and 
longer-living plants also need watering 
and fertilising on a regular basis although, 
with the fertilising, add a specific slow-
release fertiliser for the particular group of 
plants you have used. Do this three or four 
times a year as well as the liquid food on a 
monthly basis. During the cooler months 
this can be cut back as the plants are not 
actively growing.
Just a few other things to remember 
include checking from time to time that the 
drainage holes haven't become blocked by 
roots, rotate the pots to get even, all-over 
growth and prune when necessary.
