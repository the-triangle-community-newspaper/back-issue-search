Muddy Waters at Meads Bay
The BVSC 25-year vision plan
advocates that high growth be con-
fined to Bega, Merimbula and Eden
with limited growth in Bermagui and
smaller townships.
Regardless of this declared ob-
jective, and in spite of widespread com-
munity opposition, on June 26 this year
the Bega Valley Shire Council moved
to support fast track high growth de-
velopment for Bermagui and to pro-
ceed with the controversial Meads Bay
proposal, increasing the area of land
under review to include the lakeside
land as well as the ‘Myrtledale’ area
currently zoned 1C and the property
of Councillor Janette Neilson.
The Meads Bay proposal was
considered by the NSW Department of
Planning South Coast Sensitive Urban
Land Review Panel and was deemed
to be unsuitable for development due
to the high level of sensitivity of the
receiving waters. The area of the site
that drains into Meads Bay and
Wallaga Lake was recommended to be
zoned for conservation purposes with
the most appropriate zoning being E1
National Parks and Reserves.
This matter has been a bone of
contention in the district for some time
and the pressure continues with resi-
dents now calling for the State Gov-
ernment to uphold the findings of its
own Refshauge Report and to ensure
that the Meads Bay area is not devel-
oped.
The community waits for the
council to explain their reasoning and
justify these decisions.

26 July: On Tuesday 24 July,
Mayor Tony Allen once again
used his casting vote to support
the proposed development on
Meads Bay. Allen and Councillors
Aveyard, Skitt and McGill voted
against a recision motion that had
been aimed to overturn the Coun-
cil’s previous decision.
Councillors Taylor, Hede,
Hughes, and Buchanan argued
that the subdivision was inappro-
priate and that there was no evi-
dence put forward to rebut the
advice of the Expert NSW Govern-
ment Panel and other government
agencies. With no evidence put
forward, they argued, Council
should reverse the earlier decision
allowing the 80 lot subdivision.
With the votes tied, Mayor
Allen used his casting vote to de-
feat the motion. The Council will
now forward their recommenda-
tion to the NSW Government.
Terry Irwin, Chair of the
Wallaga Lake Estuary Manage-
ment Committee, said, “The issue
is not yet over. I have been greatly
heartened by the overwhelming
support of the Bermagui commu-
nity to protect Meads Bay. I also
agree with the strong sentiment
that is critical of Allen for using
his casting vote in this manner.
His actions show scant regard for
the delicate nature of Meads Bay
and little respect for the Westmin-
ster Parliamentary tradition when
using a casting vote.”

When Betsy and Rex Hilton’s
profoundly disabled son Zeke was 10
years old, his parents took action to
make provision for his care
as Zeke’s needs and his
parents’ capacity to meet
them changed over the
years ahead. At that time
the Nardy House site was
dedicated and a Trust was
formed with the stated aim
to ‘… provide supported
permanent accommoda-
tion for up to six people
with profound disabilities’.
The name for this lo-
cal Accommodation and
Respite Facility for the pro-
foundly disabled was
given to the Trust 13 years
ago by Aboriginal Elder,
Guboo Ted Thomas when
the land was dedicated for
the facility. This local crea-
tion story of Nyaadi and
Toonkoo and the flame red
waratah, which denotes
Nyaadi’s constancy and strength of
heart, is indeed reflected in the vigour
and determination of the Nardy House
Committee in Quaama. A non-Indig-
enous story of this kind of dogged de-
termination by Quaama residents is
also told in Betty Taylor’s first Quaama
history publication, Dry River – Quaama:
The Past Hundred Years 1877–1977.
Throughout the history of Quaama
the residents have fought for whatever they
believed were their rights, never backing down
… the (Education) department commented
on their stubborn determination to get their
own way. (1977, p.5)
Now that this stage of Quaama’s
ongoing battle against the conditions
of remoteness and the protracted re-
current funding dispute with NSW
Nardy House Opening Soon
DADHC for the completed stage of this
beautiful facility is resolved, Betsy
Hilton can at last look forward to some
‘real respite’ from more than 12 months
continuous caring for her son Zeke.
The respite services Nardy
House will soon be providing are now
made possible by funding for two staff
– 24 hours x 7 days per week. Project
Manager, Denise Redmond said that,
‘Carers of people with very high level
needs should contact DADHC South-
ern Region to book respite at the Nardy
House specialised respite facility.
Carers are eligible for up to nine weeks
respite per annum before their pensions
are affected’.
This service is also available to
visitors to the region who can at last
have a holiday whilst receiving respite
and support from staff who are quali-
fied to understand and meet these spe-
cial clients’ needs. Following an initial
two-year partnership with the De-
partment for the first two years, the
Nardy House board plans to
apply its expertise to inde-
pendent and autonomous
management of the facility.
Whilst work on Stage 2
– supported permanent ac-
commodation for up to six peo-
ple – is about to commence,
Denise Redmond emphasises
that recurrent funding for this
aspect of the service is still un-
der 
negotiation. 
Denise
strongly asserts that, ‘Both the
Federal and State Govern-
ments acknowledge that this
is their ongoing social prob-
lem, involving the most vul-
nerable group of people with
disabilities and it needs to be
addressed”.
The building on Stage 2
will be started with some seed
capital from fundraising, with
the rest of the $850,000 needed
to complete the stage to be sourced
from appropriate Government Fund-
ing Bodies, service clubs, philanthropic
groups as well as from submissions
made for corporate sponsorship.
Denise said, ‘Community assist-
ance has been critical and has led ulti-
mately to the recurrent funding of the
facility … Community can assist with
the owner-builder construction of
Stage 2 by continuing to contribute, by
offering donations in the form of
money, time and expertise, and by be-
ing supportive as they have been in the
past’.
Workers in the field are advised
that advertising for positions at the
facility will be placed in local papers
in the coming weeks.

Garden Magic
In last month’s issue we spoke
about all the fruiting plants which are
dormant in winter. Considering the
prohibitive prices of fruit and veg in
the supermarkets, some serious pro-
ductive gardening needs to be under-
taken perhaps.
Of course, August heralds tomato
time. Don’t plant early in the season if
you are in a frost prone area. There are
varieties to suit every taste, from the
sweet cherry tomatoes to the Roma
(egg) types to the old original Grosse
Lisse, and everything in between. How-
ever, unless you sit on the right hand of
God, you are going to have to spray or
dust regularly after the plants are es-
tablished. Always check the ingredi-
ents of insecticides – I think you will
find that the dust and the spray con-
tain exactly the same constituents, but
the spray is twice as expensive.
Other vegetables to plant now
are broccoli, cabbage, snow peas, on-
ions, cauliflower, bok choi, silverbeet
etc. Leafy vegetables require regular
applications of high nitrogen fertilis-
ers (defined by ‘N’ on the back of the
packet). (Not too much nitrogen for the
carrots or they will get tuft.) Others
need high potassium – sulphate of pot-
ash is a good old standby. Don’t forget
to apply potash to your passionfruit
vines in spring.
For complete self-sufficiency,
add some citrus to the mix of plantings.
All cope with frost to about -2 degrees.
Meyer Eureka lemons are the most
popular. Eureka fruits three times a
year, so that is the one for serious lemon
lovers. Washington navel orange is a
good choice because it ripens in the
cooler months when we need the Vita-
min C. Ellendale mandarin is very
sweet and juicy. Marsh grapefruit is
the best performer.
Gotta run – I’m off to St. George
to get a loan for some bananas.
Until next time,
