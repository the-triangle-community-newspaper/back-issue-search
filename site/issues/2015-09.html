My son Don, who is 
teaching in Kenya, competed in 
the annual Half Marathon race in 
the Lewa Wildlife Conservancy 
last month. Although he trained 
for the event and is superbly fit 
he found it gruelling. He also 
found it very inspiring. 
Not just an ordinary 
marathon, but one that runs 
through a conservation wildlife 
park in order to raise money for 
animal protection – it benefits the 
environment and the livelihood 
of people in rural areas. 
The event, for which 
participants have to raise $1000 
in order to compete, attracts 
runners from all over the world 
and takes place along bush 
tracks in undulating country at 
a high altitude within sight of 
Mt Kenya. 
The heavily protected 
61 000 acre wildlife park is 
home to rhinos, elephants, 
giraffes, zebra, lions and other 
wild animals.
Runners, in turn are 
protected by helicopters and 
spotter planes, which buzz 
dangerous animals, but most 
animals don’t venture near the 
competitors.  
D.B. from Cornwall wrote 
facetiously, “There are crowds 
of spectators at the beginning 
and end, but don’t expect crowd 
support all the way as they might 
have been eaten.” 
How ironic that in Kenya 
the people have learnt the moral 
and financial importance of 
animal conservation, while here 
at Narooma’s festival of hunting 
on the Nature Coast of New 
South Wales the Eurobodalla 
Shire council has permitted the 
selling of selling guns and the 
promotion of animal safaris to 
Africa against the wishes of 
the residents as expressed in 
petitions, rallies, presentations 
to council, door knocks and 
submissions.   
Shoot with a camera. Not 
with a Gun!
Susan Cruttenden.

The Creative Workers and Producers 
of Cobargo Association Inc is a not for 
profit organisation that was established 
in 2011 following a community meeting 
called to explore ways to strengthen the 
Cobargo Community and with the intention 
of establishing an association to showcase 
the skills and talents of people from the 
surrounding communities. 
As a result of that first meeting 
and initially under the guidance of Suzy 
McKinnon, Cobargo Creators began by 
establishing the Black Wattle Gallery 
showcasing unique artisan works and 
quickly building a reputation for excellence. 
One of the driving concepts was a 
determination that all works offered for sale 
must be high quality and produced locally 
by residents in the area. All exhibitors 
were required to be members of the 
Association and there was an undertaking 
that the commission taken on sales would 
be as low as possible while still covering 
costs. This philosophy continues and is 
maintained by the 129 members registered 
since Cobargo Creators began. There are 
about 90 active members currently and 
while some are supporters, most are active 
producers selling their work through the 
organisation’s display spaces. Volunteers 
are an essential component of the Cobargo 
Creators, their work is much appreciated 
and the Centre could not function without 
them. New volunteers are of course always 
welcome and they are not required to be 
members.
In the years 
since it opened the 
Black Wattle Gallery 
has established itself 
as a showcase space 
for art and beautiful 
things. It has been 
joined by Wares 
Gifts and the Sapling 
Gallery in what is 
now known as The 
Cobargo Creators 
Centre. Wares Gifts 
offers quality local 
handmade items that 
range from handcrafts to preserves, and the 
recently opened Sapling Gallery displays 
work by younger artists aged under 
eighteen, there is no commission charged 
on works sold through this space. 
The Cobargo Creators Centre 
supports a number of other initiatives in 
the area, hosting workshop programs, and 
coordinating the weekly Laneway Market, 
adding a bright note to Saturday mornings 
in Cobargo. 
There is close cooperation with many 
of the local creative businesses and not 
for profit community groups, as well as 
involvement in local events such as the 
Eat, Think, Create ETC weekend and the 
annual open day at Cobargo Farms.
In the immediate future there are 
plans to raise the profile of the Sapling 
Gallery and in line with this, the next 
feature exhibition will be Joy and Abandon 
and will consist of works by the pupils of 
the Cobargo Preschool. 
The Centre intends to increase the 
number of events that are relevant to 
young people. In addition it is also hoping 
to offer a lively and varied workshop 
program and to increase the number of 
people involved in behind the scenes 
work of the association. This last can be 
seen as a vital part in creating stronger 
self-sustaining communities. While there 
may still be fund raising efforts needed to 
support particular projects, in general it is 
felt that the association is on a sound and 
sustainable financial footing.
The philosophy driving the Cobargo 
Creators is the development of a strong 
community through vibrant creativity and 
there are many opportunities for residents 
and visitors to engage with this aspect 
of the developing cultural hub that is 
Cobargo. 
Drop into The Cobargo Creators 
Centre, check out the galleries, volunteer, 
take part in or suggest a concept for a 
workshop. The Joy and Abandon exhibition 
of work from the Cobargo Preschool opens 
in the Sapling Gallery on 12 September.

Enter the Quaama Dragon’s Den 
After many successful events and 
fairs, and adding in the Quaama Quisine 
takings, the Quaama Progress Association 
is flush with $$$. What should we do with 
it? 
Since we’re a non-profit community 
organisation, we want to give it back to 
the community in the best way possible, 
but how?
Have you seen that BBC TV 
show, Dragon’s Den, where budding 
entrepreneurs present their ideas to a 
panel of cashed-up investors? We thought 
we’d do that, but over brunch, and without 
the nerves and serious faces. And OK, 
let’s face it, without the million pound 
cheques…
To bounce around ideas, we’d love 
you to come along to a brunch that we’ll 
put on for anyone who shows up, to discuss 
how to best invest in the community (bring 
a plate! or don’t, it doesn’t matter)
Some ideas that have been tossed 
around are: tree planting in the centre 
of town; mapping the lost graves of the 
Quaama Cemetery; starting a deck chair 
outdoor cinema during the summer; 
clearing paper-road walking tracks; or 
supporting established local groups that 
have ideas already so they can afford to 
take the next step. 
We’ll provide tea and coffee and 
will be cooking up a storm (as usual), 
serving standard, vegetarian and gluten 
free offerings including some version of 
a bacon and egg roll, gluten free waffles, 
frittata, bircher muesli with home grown 
stewed fruit, you get my drift.
So, have a think about what you’d be 
excited to see happen around town, grab 
your friends and family and come along! 
If you’re so inclined, have a chat over the 
fence as well and help us drum up interest.
See you then!

Currawongs
I really can’t remember another influx 
of currawongs like we saw around the 
village in August, in terms of numbers, 
noise and duration of stay. They hustled 
all our local magpies out of town, as they 
do, like a nasty gang of tough guys. I’m 
told they’re migratory and tend to move on, 
and as I write they do seem to be thinning 
out. But I did miss the maggies. I saw one 
solitary magpie in the back paddock this 
morning, silently scouring the ground, 
ear cocked. In stealth mode, by the looks, 
laying low.

Lamont Street pedestrian safety crossing stalled
Bermagui residents may be wondering 
if or when Council is planning to complete 
the pedestrian crossing on the town side of 
the Bermagui River bridge. For some 
time now the kerb cuts enabling 
bikes, prams and scooters to mount 
the footpath, and the raised concrete 
“blisters” that protect people waiting 
to cross, have been completed. 
But there is no sign of the central 
pedestrian refuge island that was part 
of Council’s original plan.
The pedestrian crossing, if 
completed, would allow school 
children, parents with toddlers, 
shoppers, dog walkers, mobility 
scooter users and cyclists to safely 
cross Lamont Street– a road that 
will become busier as summer 
approaches. The crossing links up 
with the shared pathway to the public 
wharf area.
The delay in completing the crossing 
is due to objections raised by the Bermagui 
Chamber of Commerce. The Chamber’s 
objection was on safety grounds: they 
feel that the road is too narrow for large 
vehicles to pass safely on either side of 
a central pedestrian island. They asked 
Council to halt work on the crossing.
The project is funded jointly by Bega 
Council and Roads and Maritime Services 
(RMS), whose engineers passed it through 
design standards before work started.
There has been some anecdotal 
evidence of difficulties, even minor 
injuries, to cyclists heading towards town 
who have tried to turn left off the road 
onto the shared pathway. It’s true that this 
would be a tricky manoeuvre but it can be 
avoided easily by using the footpath (part 
of the shared pathway) on the other 
side of the bridge instead of riding 
on the road. There are also stories 
of blown tyres before the signage 
and road markings were completed.
I asked Wayne Sartori, 
Council’s Infrastructure Group 
Manager, if it was common practice 
for Council to halt an infrastructure 
project due to opposition from a 
community group. His response, via 
email, had no answer to this but he 
did say, “Community consultation 
occurred through the exhibition of 
and adoption of Bega Valley Shire 
Bike Plan.” 
Both sides claim safety is their 
major concern. But should the views 
of any community group take precedence 
over the expert analysis of Council and 
RMS engineers?
Jen Severn

Move over cine, we’re going digital
We have been archiving photos and 
recording interviews for several years 
now, and another important venture is to 
collect and convert old movie footage of 
the district.
Cine film footage is of course much 
rarer than photographs. Perhaps the earliest 
known filming in Bermagui was done 
to cover the exploits of big game angler 
and film star, Zane Grey during 1936. He 
travelled with his own cameraman and 
after visiting Bermagui, he made the movie 
White Death.  
The story was inspired by Grey seeing 
a Great White shark captured locally. We 
have the movie digitized at a cost of $332 
by the National Film and Sound Archive.
Films made by students at the Public 
School in the 1960s 
and 1970s have 
been digitized. 
Titles include The 
Runaway Pram 
and Shark Attack, 
both of which were 
hilarious slapstick 
productions.  Also 
acquired by gift 
from a generous 
donor is digitized 
footage taken of 
the Tuna Festival, 
Surf Carnival and a fire which destroyed 
Melody Hall.
As a historical footnote cine film is 
very volatile and deteriorates over time. It 
was cine film catching fire in a projector 
which caused the destruction of Kings Hall 
on Lamont Street in 1951.
Following a generous donation from 
Bermagui Country Women’s Association 
(CWA), we are in the processes of 
converting further film footage. If any 
of our readers have old cine film of local 
interest of the district, please get in touch. 
We can get a digitised copy back to you 
following digital conversion, whilst 
maintaining a copy for the museum 
archive.
Meanwhile, some recently acquired 
photographs have been incorporated into 
the forthcoming 2016 calendar. Sales will 
begin on Fathers’ Day. Look out for Errol 
on 6 September, (Fathers’ Day raffle) or 
pop in to 777 and get your copy in plenty 
of time for Christmas. We have held the 
price at $12 again this year.

No Lights No Lycra goes back to the 1960s: any requests? 
Spring has sprung and No Lights 
No Lycra Cobargo wants to dance off the 
freezing sub zero temperatures we have 
been having. We are turning back to the 
clocks and will be dancing to 1960s music.
The 60s was a fun and unique time 
because of the dance craze during that 
period. A flourishing culture of musical 
groups began to emerge and in Liverpool 
alone there has been estimated that there 
were around 350 different bands active, 
often playing ballrooms and halls.
So NLNL Cobargo is going to get the 
big hall shaking on Monday 7 September. 
Come down and kick up your heels and 
dance in Cobargo. The 60s music will be 
pumping out of the speakers at 6pm: Elvis 
Presley, The Beetles, Van Morison, Rolling 
Stones, Little Eva, Chubby Checker, 
everything from the nostalgic 60s and the 
crowd is guaranteed to be grooving and 
dancing.
No Lights No Lycra (NLNL) Cobargo 
is a weekly dance event for fun on 
Mondays in the semi dark. It is an entirely 
sober dance party that attracts eager dance 
groups in over 50 locations around the 
world - and now it’s in Cobargo. 
“There’s not a lot of spaces to dance 
in Cobargo other than the pub,” explains 
Gabrielle Powell, who is utilising the 
concept which originated in Melbourne. 
“At NLNL there’s no drugs, no uninvited 
grinding, no steps, and no mirrors or lights 
to feed your self-consciousness.”
NLNL all started in 2009 when 
rebellious Australian dance students Alice 
Glenn and Heidi Barrett grew sick of 
critiquing their own bodies in the mirror, 
and decided to ditch the choreography 
and leotards to scale up the mini lights 
dance jams they’d occasionally have in 
their living room. Little did they know 
the idea would spread as far as Shanghai, 
Christchurch, Chicago and Cobargo!
So get on your dancing shoes and 
come down to Cobargo for the 60s dance 
night on Monday 7 September at Cobargo 
Hall, Bermagui Road, Cobargo every 
Monday night 6-7pm. Entry is $5, bring 
water and wear comfortable 1960s clothes!
For more information or to send in 
your musical requests, please contact 
Gabrielle Powell, Coordinator, Women’s 
Resource Centre.

The other side of Anzac
Recently I heard the story of an 
American social worker whose job in 
the 1990s was to scour the mountains of 
Alaska, seeking the bolt-holes of Vietnam 
veterans who had decided to remove 
themselves from society, living off the land 
in isolated shacks. In the 20 years since the 
Vietnam War, more and more American 
vets had come to the grim realisation that 
they were now unable to coexist with their 
families and friends; to hold down jobs; to 
partake in society in any meaningful way. 
P o s t -
traumatic stress 
disorder. PTSD.
The social 
worker’s task was 
to bring the vets 
into Anchorage to 
live in a halfway 
house and receive 
psychological 
therapy until they 
were willing and 
able to re-enter 
society. Of the 
degree of his 
success, I’m not 
sure.
In Australia, 
t h e ‘ A n z a c 
Spirit’ has found 
a f i r m p l a c e 
in our collective 
s u b c o n s c i o u s , 
especially in the last 20 years. Now an 
expression used to summon the sentiment 
of ANZAC troops in all wars since 1915, 
it brings to mind bravery, mateship, self-
sacrifi ce – as if these concepts were unique 
to Australians and New Zealanders. In its 
jingoistic rally-cry, the long, lethal legacy 
of modern warfare is forgotten.
Australian Vietnam veterans are 
nearly 14 times more likely to attempt 
suicide than the general population. And 
it’s a measure of the downstream effects of 
PTSD that the wives of vets are six times 
more likely than their peers to attempt 
suicide, and their children three times 
more likely. 
Sharing these stark statistics with us, 
on 5 August at the Bermagui Institute Public 
Dinner, was retired infantry company 
commander Graham Walker, AM. Graham 
spent 21 years in the Australian Defence 
Forces in Vietnam and other confl icts. He 
has spent the years since his retirement in 
1981 advocating for the rights of returned 
Vietnam vets.
PTSD first came to attention 
after WWI. An epidemic of symptoms 
amongst returned servicemen, ranging 
from unfocused anxiety and loss of 
concentration to paralysis, blindness and 
convulsions, with no discernible physical 
cause, was labelled as shell shock and 
derided as cowardice by the military.
Even after WWII, when it was 
accepted that there was a psychological 
cause for the symptoms, it was claimed 
– again, by the military – that there was a 
genetic predisposition for the condition: 
“real men” would not be affected.
Not until 1980 was PTSD in soldiers 
identified as a consequence of a life-
threatening experience on the battlefi eld 
– the fault of the war, not the soldier. 
Since then, almost half the Australian 
veterans repatriated from Vietnam have 
fi led successful compensation claims for 
mental conditions. As Graham had said on 
ABC Local Radio on the morning of his 
talk, “The war doesn’t end, for very many 
veterans, when the shooting stops.”
I knew that Graham had been a rock of 
emotional and moral support for countless 
returned soldiers over the last 30 years and 
more. I had heard 
stories. Again I 
checked the notes 
I took on the night: 
no mention of any 
of that. But I wasn’t 
surprised. Like his 
career, Graham’s 
address was all 
about others.
Perhaps the 
m o s t e l o q u e n t 
t e s t a m e n t t o 
Graham’s legacy 
was from former US 
Marine and fellow 
Vietnam veteran 
Bruce Olson, who’d 
travelled down 
from Tweed Heads 
to have the honour 
of introducing Graham 
to the capacity crowd 
in Bermagui that night. Bruce’s voice 
trembled as he recounted the public disdain 
for troops returning from Vietnam in the 
70s, compounding the PTSD prevalent 
amongst soldiers in that messy war, and 
the blind eye the government turned to their 
invisible wounds. Graham Walker, he said, 
was “the fi rst senior offi cer to put his head 
above the parapet” for the lower ranks. 
Bruce has been just one benefi ciary of 
Graham’s staunch support over the years. 
But my take on it is this: it wasn’t 
nerves that made his voice quaver, or the 
memories of those early betrayals. The clue 
was in the look exchanged between the 
two as they gripped hands when Graham 
rose to speak. It was a look of profound 
understanding, a decades-long dialogue 
between warriors. A conversation that 
had been to hell, turned around and made 
its tortured way back. The other side of 
Anzac, indeed.
Jen Severn

have relations.

To prepare beanball mix: Mash the 
beans in a mixing bowl until no whole 
beans are left. Start with a potato masher 
then use a fork. You don’t want a smooth 
paste, you still want to recognise beans. 
Add the flavourings, 2 tbsp oil, garlic, 
zest, crumbs and herbs, use a fork to mix 
everything together. Then use your hands 
to knead the mixture for about a minute 
until everything is well combined and firm. 
Roll the mixture into walnut sized 
balls. You can either bake the beanballs in a 
preheated oven 180C on a oiled oven dish, 
drizzling a little more oil over them to coat 
for about 15 minutes till lightly browned on 
the bottom, turning them over to bake for 
another 10 minutes. Pour 2 cups of heated 
sauce over the balls, carefully turning them 
over to coat. Bake for another 5 minutes or 
so. Strew over fresh coriander.
Or you could pan fry the beanballs. 
Preheat a large frypan over medium heat. 
Heat about 4 tbsp oil before adding your 
beanballs. You may have to do it in batches 
as you don’t want overcrowding. Cook for 
about 15 minutes, till brown on all sides. 
Add 2 cups of the Moroccan sauce, coating 
the balls carefully and cook for another 10 
minutes. Add fresh herbs and serve with 
couscous.

There is nothing so comforting as meatballs in a tomato 
sauce. The difference in this recipe is that it is a versatile Moroccan 
Tomato Sauce that can be used as a base for all sorts of recipes. I’ve 
provided a vegan alternative to the Morocan Meatballs -Beanballs! 
I also  like using the  sauce to cook a simple Moroccan chicken dish 
using either seared chicken thigh pieces or drumsticks with the

Moroccan Sauce
2 tbsp olive oil
1 onion finely diced 
2-3 cloves garlic crushed
1 tsp fine black pepper
½ tsp cayenne pepper, more if you like heat
2 tsp ground cardamom 
4 cm piece of fresh ginger, peeled and chopped finely
2 tsp each ground cumin, coriander and cinnamon 
2 x 400g tomatoes, diced
2 tbsp brown sugar 
Salt to taste
To make sauce: fry onion and all flavourings in oil 
until softened. Add tomatoes and sugar and simmer for 
15 minutes. Season to taste. Makes about 4 cups. Sauce 
can be kept in fridge for a few days.
Moroccan Meatballs
Mix 500g lamb mince, 120g crumbled feta, 2 cloves 
crushed garlic,1 egg,1 tsp ground cumin, the finely grated zest 
of 1 lemon and 2 tbsp chopped mint leaves in a bowl. Form 
mixture into walnut sized balls. 
Heat 2 cups of the sauce in a large pot or frypan. When 
simmering, add meatballs and simmer without stirring over a 
medium heat until cooked through (approx 15 minutes). 
Or you could bake for 30 minutes in a preheated oven 
180C. Serve with couscous or rice. You could stir in chopped 
coriander and mint and sprinkle chopped pistachio nuts on top.

With another spring upon us I 
thought it opportune to discuss one of 
the most popular groups of perennials 
– Salvia.
With their extraordinary diversity 
of colour and habit there are over 1800 
species with new varieties added almost 
all the time. Salvias are truly a gardeners’ 
delight providing rich and interesting 
rewards for every garden.
This spell binding genus has blues 
to rival the colours of the ocean and sky 
as well as rich reds, pinks, yellows and 
white and with every colour combination 
of these dominant colours.
Their diversity in leaf colour and 
shape is second to none with leaves that 
feel like velvet to long narrow leaves of 
varying colours. Many of the varieties 
are used for culinary purposes and have 
delightful scents that can be used to 
fl avour foods of all kinds.
Most Salvia species are adaptable to 
an extensive range of soil types from clay 
to open sandy soils. Where soils might be 
in need of improvement it makes sense 
to add some organic matter like animal 
manures or compost.
Good drainage however is generally 
advisable and without it there is a greater 
risk of the less hardy varieties surviving 
a long wet winter. In gardens where the 
soil is wet for long periods it is advisable 
to slightly raise the planting area to allow 
better drainage.
When preparing soils for Salvia 
refrain from adding excess nitrogen. Too 
much nitrogen will give lush foliage at the 
expense of fl owers. Soil acidity/alkalinity 
play no great part in the placement of the 
plants although many of the species are 
derived from locations where limestone 
is dominant.
Nearly all varieties prefer a full sun 
position with the exception of a few that 
will grow in and tolerate shade.
Some taller varieties have a brittle 
branching habit and thus should be planted 
in a location that is free of strong winds. 
Many of the lower growing forms are 
excellent in a seaside garden.
Like all perennials, Salvias should be 
pruned on a regular basis to keep the plant 
in a compact form and to promote several 
periods of fl owering throughout the year.
They are generally pest and disease 
free with aphids and some powdery 
mildew being a problem in the summer 
months.
They are a great plant to grow in a 
container for those who have a diffi cult 
location or to brighten up an area, such 
as on paving etc. where other plants 
might have failed. Due to their ability 
to handle periods of dryness they are an 
ideal pot plant.
Often overlooked these days are the 
extensive range of annual forms that are 
available to brighten up the fl ower garden 
where seasonal change is required. 
Colours of reds, white, blues and purple 
are available and should be planted at the 
rear of the garden. Low growing annuals 
like Petunia, Marigold, Lobelia and other 
summer fl owering annuals are a great 
companion planting to Salvia.
Check out your local nursery for the 
exciting range of Salvia both perennial 
and annual that are now available and 
brighten up your garden with these 
rewarding plants.
