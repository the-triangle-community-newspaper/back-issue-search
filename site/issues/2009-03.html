Funds donated or granted by the
Mumbulla Foundation, Friends of the
Libraries, Rotary, and Robin Hood
Service Clubs, have made possible new
Bega Valley Shire Library services for
borrowers with low vision. These
services add to the existing audio tapes
and books on CD. Four devices are
currently available at both Bega and
Merimbula libraries. In time they will
be available in Bermagui. Borrowers
without good vision can now borrow
an Audio Navigator© - a computerised
device something like an iPod© in size
but which plays audio books rather
than music. It reads books straight into
Local Library Services for Vision Impaired
the user’s home speakers, ear phones
or car stereo system. A borrower
simply selects the book of choice from
a catalogue of titles currently
available from Bega or Merimbula.
The chosen book is then loaded onto
the device at the library, and the
device is then borrowed in the normal
fashion at the circulation desk.
Bega Valley Friends of the Libraries,
Inc. initiated the introduction of the
Navigators to the Shire’s library
services and are seeking local donors
to 
help 
purchase 
additional
Navigators so that borrowers in the
northern area of the shire can benefit.
Each device costs $600. Anyone
considering contributing all or part of
the cost of a Navigator, or the cost of
new book titles, is invited to telephone
Helen of the Bega Valley Friends on
6494 6613. For more information about
the service itself phone Ros at the Bega
Library on 6499 2127. At the moment,
because there are only eight Navigators
in the library system, their use is
restricted to borrowers with low vision.
However, their potential use is
enormous, for example for reluctant
teenage readers, and as soon as more
are available the use restrictions will be
lifted.

Angophora tree decline
It’s been a big month for
ecological misinformation in The
Triangle. First the beach-washed
mutton bird furore, and now the
bizarre explanation offered in Brogo
Babble for the poor health of
Angophora trees: displacement of
bellbirds by noisy “minors” (sounds
like the neighbours’ kids on trail
bikes) leading to increased insect
attack on trees. In fact both Bell
Miners (a.k.a. bellbirds) and Noisy
Miners have similar effects on tree
health by driving other insectivo-
rous birds out of their territory,
increasing insect attack on trees. But
Bell Miners live in forest and Noisy
Miners live in open woodland and
farming areas, and their paths
would seldom cross. The so-called
BMAD (Bell Miner associated
dieback) has been listed as a Key
Threatening Process in NSW.
Anyone wanting more details can
look up the following web address:
www.environment.nsw.gov.au/
determinations/bellminerfd.htm.
No-one has yet nominated
“NMAD” as a threatening process,
perhaps because it doesn’t threaten
forestry and hence does not have an
easily quantifiable economic cost. As
for the Angophoras, I think the long
drought is responsible for their
current condition, combined with
the fact that many trees exhausted
their reserves by flowering profusely
in 2007, only to be plunged back into
drought in 2008. Not only
Angophoras are affected: red gums
are in a similar poor state, and many
other trees, including some in forest.
I thought at first that the poor health
was more pronounced in areas
remote from the forested valley
edges, which might imply a role for
insects, since predators of insects are
likely to be fewer in isolated trees.
However, I have seen equally sick
looking trees on the forest edges
(where neither type of Miner is
present), so this explanation does
not hold water. Conversely, trees
with access to a bit of extra water,
such as the one in our yard, and
ones growing close to dams, appear
in better health. Hence I would pin
the blame on the drought. Let’s hope
it ends soon, before too many more
trees give up the ghost altogether.
Jackie Miles
 (Ecologist) Brogo

Lament for Marysville
Eucalypt and mountain ash,
etched against a blood-red sky,
crushed beneath the summer’s lash,
and withered by a futile cry.
This day-dream town of dappled light,
cloaked by forest, deep and green,
lies wasted by an awful blight; empty,
brooding and obscene.
The seductive curve of shop-front path,
the tea and scones of rustic charm,
are embers in the aftermath
of loss and deeply rooted harm.
Can autumn days cool the pain?
Shall passing years temper grief?
Regrowth buds will shoot again,
and the ash will green the leaf.
Ian McFarlane,

When did you first arrive in the
Triangle?
When Bill and I bought a farm at
Verona in 1990, we moved there and set
it up as a dairy farm with our
neighbour. I had no idea what
adventures were before me, being an
inner city girl!
What type of work do you do?
I have my own consulting
company, and work as a strategic
facilitator on social and environmental
issues with large companies and the
community. That means that a
company will ask me to work with
them to help understand how they can
set up a process to get people external
to the company together with their staff
to design a better way of doing
business. Sometimes it’s with ANZ on
their indigenous housing policy, or
BHPbilliton on corporate responsibility
listening to NGO leaders from around
the world, or Reconciliation Australia
planning and running their annual staff
retreat. I get to work on controversial
issues with people who have more in
common than they think. 
Obviously your work takes you
away from the area. What makes
My Triangle
Sheena Boughen
returning so special?
Well I really love where I live,
the physical beauty of the coast, and
the people I live with. I think to be in
a place with a small population and
in a rapid state of change brings out
interesting tensions and also great
possibilities. I am very excited about
the way in which we are designing the
future here, and think the role of the
arts and culture can be a great driver
for sustainable growth.  I love the idea
that the script for the future isn’t
written yet ... so why shouldn’t we
design it and make it innovative,
sustainable and profitable for all? 
What do you most enjoy about
living in the Triangle?
I like the silence and the natural
beauty, and the optimism of the
people who are putting in so much
effort for our future. The ones who
believe in a low energy society, high on
cultural and agricultural activities that
will make money, having great fun and
a rich life from encouraging diverse
ways of thinking and living.
You are currently the Chair of
the Four Winds Festival at Bermagui.
What benefits does the community
gain from this bi-ennial festival?
Our audience constantly tells us
that they adore being in an outdoor
amphitheatre near the beach, with the
best of Australia’s musicians and
special ones from overseas, who
surround us with the magic of classic
music from around the world. For three
whole days! Then they get to eat
delicious local food, and be nourished
by the generosity of the volunteers who
plan it all with such passion and
enthusiasm. Putting world class music,
natural beauty and food together with
friends is our definition of luxury. And
the 1200 people per day who come
spend a lot of money while they are
here!
What leisure activities do you
enjoy in the Triangle?
Listening to the surf, yoga with
Lois Irwin, weights with Bear, eating
and drinking with all the great cooks
and gardeners, and being so inspired
by the huge array of brilliant artists and
crafts-people here.

Readers may recall our feature on
Stan Whiffen of Tilba in My Triangle in
September. Sadly, Stan passed away on
25 January 2009, aged 86 years.
Stan was a
veteran of World
War II, a proud
dairy 
farmer
who 
first
ploughed 
by
draft horse, and
loved fishing and
dancing. 
His
strong 
work
ethic was the key to his success. Above
all, he was regarded as a man of his
word. Stan is survived by wife, Cathy,
their two sons and six grandchildren.
Stan died just days before Cathy and his
fiftieth 
wedding 
anniversary,
illustrating a strong, loving and
enduring partnership. It is exemplified
by his actions shortly before he died.
When he was recently admitted to
hospital due to deteriorating health, he
pulled Cathy close to him during one
of her visits and said, ‘Cathy, you look
beautiful today, give me another kiss.
How did I find a girl like you? You’re
beautiful’. Stan is one of the last of his
generation in Tilba, and will be missed
by all who knew him.

A relief campaign was started on
Monday 9th February, with the Brogo
RFS in Warrigal Range Road opening
its doors each afternoon during the
week, from 4.00 to 6.00pm for two
weeks, accepting donations from the
community. From the moment we
opened the doors, we had goods
coming in steadily each day.
All the goods were sorted,
boxed and marked accordingly.
Amongst the goods we received were:
lots of toiletries from soaps to perfume;
boxes of  cutlery; kitchenware,
including a coffee machine, cups and
coffee, crockery, glassware, toaster,
mugs, etc; several boxes of woollen

This month I
found some lovely
Golden 
Nugget
pumpkins that are
grown locally and tried
them several ways.
Their round shape lends to cutting them
in half, filling and baking.
Golden Nugget Bake
Cut in half and scoop out the
seeds, make the bottom level by cutting
it flat. Fill with peas and a decent dob
of butter, some chopped mint, a dessert
spoon of water and pepper and salt.
Cover lightly with aluminium foil and
place in a moderate oven till the
pumpkin is cooked, about half an hour.
These are nice with a roast or barbeque.
For a meal in a shell fill with a
savoury mince, any leftovers or a good
curry and bake. You can get carried
away and cover the filling with mashed
potatoes. Grate a little parmesan cheese
on top and you have a good meal. Nice
too with vegetables in season cooked in
a cheese sauce and then baked.
Onion and Thyme Marmalade
This makes 1 litre.
2 kg onions cut into rings
3 cups malt vinegar
6 peppercorns
2 bay leaves
3½ cups firmly packed brown
sugar
2 tablespoons fresh thyme
leaves
1 teaspoon salt
10 sprigs of thyme 3cm
Place the onions and vinegar in
a large pan. Put the bay leaves and
peppercorns in a square of muslin or
a new Chux and tie up. Add to the pan,
bring to the boil, then reduce heat and
simmer for 40-45 minutes, or until the
onion is very soft.
Add the sugar, thyme leaves
and 1 teaspoon of salt. Stir until all the
sugar has dissolved. Bring to the boil,
then reduce heat and simmer for 20-
30 minutes, or until thick and syrupy.
Skim any scum off with a slotted
spoon. Discard the muslin bag and stir
in fresh thyme sprigs. Spoon pulp
immediately into sterilised jars, top up
with the syrup and seal the jars. Label
and date. Leave for a month before
opening to allow flavours to develop.
To sterilise jars: place clean jars
on a tray in the oven at 110 degrees for
20 minutes. Place lids in a bowl and
pour boiling water over them, drain in
a colander.
Try it with fresh crusty bread
and camembert cheese … good for a
picnic or drinks.

‘My Country’
Gardening in
February may be
summed up in a few
words -  trees died,
shrubs fried and vegetables bolted to
seed. I recited ‘My Country’ to myself
every time I went outside.
What an incredible climate we
live in. One end of the country is
drowning and the other end is burning,
so I suppose our garden problems are
fairly minor. Nonetheless, it’s hard to
see our favourite shrubs shrivelling
through the combination of heat and
lack of water.
The fundamentals of gardening
– fertiliser, mulch and water –  will
protect your plot to a certain degree, but
these principles are becoming
increasingly difficult for those of us
who aren’t on town water.
Nonetheless, I still won’t resort
to those so-called water holding
crystals and other related products
because they’re petroleum based.
What I’ve done is had a big
clean-up and thrown out all that stuff
in pots that I keep meaning to plant,
but never get around to. Besides that,
the earth is so hard I swear I need a
bobcat to dig a hole. Anyway there’s a
bit of a water saving in sacrificing
plants that are runty or not really
healthy.
I wouldn’t be pruning anything
for a month or so because as unsightly
as some of the foliage is, it will protect
the leaves that are coming on in the
event of another hot spell before
autumn.
Don’t forget the Bermagui

I’ll be on the Potoroo Palace stand. Go

Fisherpeople!
Well things have started to pick
up offshore. After a quiet start the
Marlin have finally shown up, with
several caught recently.
Kingies and Bonito are being
caught at Montague Island although
seals can be a nuisance; I’ve fished the
island several times recently and
caught plenty of  fish but kept very
few over legal size. Some customers
have been more fortunate with a lady
from Churchill, Victoria, catching a
very nice kingie of 88cm as well as
some good snapper and morwong.
There have been plenty of
sharks about. One angler hooked a
nine foot hammerhead half a mile off
Bermagui entrance.
I hooked and lost a
mako on the 12 mile reef,
around 180-200 kilos, a
fortnight ago.
Other anglers have
caught makos, bronze
whalers and hammerhead
recently. Bottom bouncing
has improved as well; one
customer recently had a
good day out catching 33
tiger flathead, ten morwong
and 15 snapper on the six
mile reef. The Six Mile and
Brothers  reefs have also
been producing some fair
catches.
The surf has slowed a
little 
after 
the 
mad
Christmas rush of salmon,
where customers were
catching them by the dozen
up and down the coast.
However bream and salmon
are still about.
The Estuaries are fishing very
well; last weekend I fished Wapengo
with my boys for ten bream over a
pound and seven good flatties up to
two kilos.
The Bermagui River is fishing
well at present, customers report good
flathead between the bridge and
downstream 
to 
the 
entrance,
particularly in the evening.
My young fella Billy caught his
first good dusty flathead off the corner
the other night, around  65cm.
Things are looking very
promising for the upcoming comps,
the sea is alive with baitfish so
hopefully we are in for a promising
game fishing season.
All the best and good fishing.
