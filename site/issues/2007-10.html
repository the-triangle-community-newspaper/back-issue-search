It was a little scary coming out
in public to present an information
evening about permaculture, but judg-
ing the response from the packed
crowd at the River Rock Café back in
September, well worth the effort. I had
felt a sense of frustration during the
current climate change debate that the
voice of permaculture was not being
heard and that at the Earth Steward-
ship response to energy descent was
simply ignored as some remnant odd-
ity from the hippie era and totally un-
recognised. So What! It’s just a funky
way of growing food isn’t it?
Well … it’s not really. Screening
the film ‘The Power of Community’
puts it into another league. Cuba was
faced in the early 1990s with an oil cri-
sis following the collapse of commu-
nism in the Soviet Union. A further
embargo was placed by the US with
cargo ships entering Cuba not allowed
entry to the US for six months. They
went through what they called a ‘
pecial period’ for about six years in
Lessons
From Cuba
which their entire country was re-de-
signed. How they grew food, their
transport, housing and energy all
needed to adapt to oil depletion.
Permaculturalists from Aus-
tralia played a prominent role during
this period in setting up on-ground ex-
amples of what worked and training
Cubans in permaculture design. In par-
ticular the area of urban agriculture
was developed where every available
spare space was dedicated
to the production of local
food supply. The result
seen today is that the capi-
tal of Havana now pro-
vides 80% of its food needs
within Havana and that
all Cuban agriculture is
80% organic. The rooftops
of buildings became pre-
mium space for growing
vegetables and ‘chook
tractors’.
Bicycles were im-
ported from China as
transport and large pub-
lic transporters, known as
‘The Camel’, became an
ingenious use of prime
movers to cart hundreds of people
around. Hitchiking was encouraged
and it was against the law for a Gov-
ernment vehicle not to pick up hitch-
hikers.
Energy provision was fast
tracked toward solar panels and wind
turbines while they continue to experi-
ment with bio-fuels. Waste from sugar
cane harvesting can now provide 30%
of electricity usage to Cuba for three
months of the year.
During the special period, vital
social services such as education, hous-
ing and medical provisions were main-
tained and further developed, which
are often the first areas cut when coun-
tries face an economic crisis. Cuba now
exports doctors to assist in the health
of the Third World and other Latin
American countries.
The power of this story, how-
ever, is the Cuban people themselves
and how they faced this crisis together,
as a community and the empower-
ment this gave them. That’s what
permaculture is about … it’s about
people, food, working relationships

Ronald Arthur Benny –
‘Cobber’
Happy 
90th 
birthday
Cobber.
What a pleasure it was to
spend an hour with Cobber Benny.
I heard on the grapevine that
Cobber had turned 90 last month
and thought it would be a good
opportunity to have a chat with
him.
Cobber 
was 
born 
in
Cobargo in 1917, number 4 of 9
siblings. Cobber’s father was al-
ready a Cobargo butcher. He origi-
nally set up shop with a mate –
Welch and Benny Butchery, later
going out on his own in what we
now all recognise as Benny’s Butchery.
Cobber’s original shop was where
Benny’s Butchery is, but was destroyed
by the great flood. The present build-
ing was built after the flood.
Cobber went to the local school,
leaving at the age of 14. His teacher said
to him, ‘We can take you no further,
you may as well leave’. Cobber seemed
pretty happy with that decision!
On day one of Cobber’s very long
working life, his dad gave him a bicy-
cle and his first job was to ride round
Cobargo taking orders and then deliv-
ering them, neatly wrapped, the next
day. But by the time he had turned 16
he could do everything, slaughter,
clean, cut and sell. When I asked Cobber
if he ever took holidays, he smiled and
said, he worked every day, even Sun-
days. He did occasionally, though, get
some time off, and was known as a
handy runner in his youth. He partici-
pated in the local sports days and at
Narooma won 50 quid! A fortune then
to the lad from Cobargo!
His elder brother Lou was the
only other of the siblings who worked
in the Butchery with Dad Benny and
when Cobber was 25, Dad Benny re-
tired and passed the Butchery onto
Lou and Cobber. They worked it to-
gether until 1970, when Lou decided
he’d had enough and Cobber bought
him out. On his recent birthday Cobber
visited Lou. Lou is now 93, living in a
retirement home in Bateman’s Bay. He’s
wheelchair bound but still himself –
great genes these Bennys.
Cobber’s 25th year was pretty
special. Not only did he become part
owner of a butchery, he also met
his wife, Doreen. Doreen’s par-
ents were dairy farmers and had
moved to the area from Candelo.
Doreen was working at The Bank
Hotel in Cobargo – the building
no longer exists, it was where the
Vet’s building is.
So there you are, 65 years
later they are still happily mar-
ried, Cobber still tends the gar-
den, the citrus orchard and the
plants in the shade house and he
dries up after each meal! He
counts himself very lucky: he has
excellent health, learnt a good
trade very well and found a life
long partner.
Cobber was born to be a butcher,
he has that lovely humour that we ex-
pect from our butchers and he’s still
got ALL ten fingers, and yes I did ask,
‘Where does Cobber come from?’ It’s a
nickname from his Dad. He loved hang-
ing out with his dad as a small tacker
and his father use to refer to him as
‘his little cobber’.
Louise Brown

The Effects of Equine Influenza
After surviving the effects of five
years of drought and high feed prices,
we are once again faced with a major
dilemma.The equine influenza virus
has hit NSW and Queensland. This flu
virus has the capacity of bringing
hardship not only to the racing indus-
try, but also to local breeding studs and
any business involved in pleasure ac-
tivities – local saddleries, farriers,
equine dentists, chiropractors and
many other people who derive an in-
come from the horse industry.
The rigid controls put in place
by the DPI regarding the movement of
horses must be adhered to, if we are to
control the spread of this virus. We all
need to work together and be very dili-
gent. A veterinarian should be con-
tacted if a horse shows any signs of ill
health. The symptoms of the horse flu
are high temperatures, nasal discharge,
a persistent hacking cough, and the
horse may be lethargic and off their
feed.
Any horse displaying suspect
symptoms must immediately be quar-
antined away from other stock, and a
rigorous quarantine procedure must be
put in place. Stables, gear and all per-
sons handling the horse must go
through a decontamination process.
The property must then go into a lock
down mode.
The importance of allowing no
visitors onto a quarantined property
is the only way we are going to stop
the spread of EI. Transmission occurs
primarily between horses via the res-
piratory track, but can be spread by
people who have had contact with a
sick horse.
The effects of being isolated in a
lock down have major ramifications on
all facets of family life. Income is af-
fected greatly. Doing the grocery shop-
ping is a task with the decontamina-
tion process that must be adhered to.
The quarantine period lasts for up to
50 days after the horse has caught the
virus, and the mortality rate is of course
higher in very young foals and older
horses.
All up and coming horse shows,
gymkhanas and horse events will no
doubt be cancelled. This will also have

Pat’s Piece
If only we
could be as pas-
sionate 
about
peace on earth,
climate change,
ending poverty,
as we are about sport. Watching the
self-righteous baying for Andrew
Johns’ blood recently was indeed a sad
thing. Even sadder is the fact that an
affluent society in a wealthy country
like ours, gives access to our young peo-
ple to all kinds of mind changing sub-
stances, some much more dangerous
than others. But when does the blame
game begin and end and how much
right do we have to be the judge.
I have a vivid memory of having
a drink with friends many years ago
on the verandah of the Bermagui Ho-
tel. At a table next to us was a middle
aged, well known resident who was
well and truly under the influence of
alcohol. Suddenly he started ranting
and raving about how the local teen-
agers went out to the Murrah Hall to
have their rock concerts so they could
smoke dope well out of the eye of the
local police. He was quite scathing
about these so called ‘pot heads’. The
interesting thing about this scenario
is that he himself was under the influ-
ence of a drug, because that’s what al-
cohol is and if you abuse the use of it, it
will kill you, the same as any other
drug. The fact that there are other dan-
gerous substances out there doesn’t
give us the right to blame the victim,
something that some of us old fuddy
duddies are damn good at.
While Andrew Johns and others
like him are condemned, it just means
that they have their human fallibility
like the rest of us. So many of our young
sports idol’s lives are spent in a fish-
bowl, while they have access to huge
amounts of money. The trouble is we
expect them to be perfect and if there is
one thing I have learnt in my 84 years
it is that perfect doesn’t apply to the
human condition. One of the great trag-
edies of an affluent society is it tends to
become gluttonous and encourages
abuse, not only of drugs but in our very
lifestyle.
So whilst I enjoy a quiet ‘scotch’
at five o’clock each evening knowing
that a drug is a drug is a drug, I refuse
to criticise those unfortunate enough
to be in Andrew Johns’ position. I first
hope the general public can allow him
to get on with his life, remembering
that while it’s love that makes this old
world go round, it’s also love that
makes the ride worthwhile, not the
practice of destructive criticism.
Pat Thompson

Tilba Bites
Whew! Where did that month
go – it must be getting busy in Spring.
The main street of Central Tilba has
been a hive of activity lately, not so
much with visitors having fun and
spending money but with building and
renovations. The Tilba Bakery has had
a holiday for the past few months, but
not so its proprietors Alf and Jane. They
and a crew of local tradespeople and
artisans have been working like bea-
vers, adding a new shop space to the
side of the original building and creat-
ing a very attractive outdoor space
with brick paving graced by lovely
granite stonework and picket fences.
Congratulations to you all and I feel
sure locals and visitors will enjoy
sweets, savouries and doughy things
even more. The Rose and Sparrow Cafe
has a new kerbside paved eating space,
which should get plenty of al fresco
use.
The ABC Cheese Factory had to
close their doors for a couple of days
while they had new tiles laid in the
shop area of the building. It looks good.
Layla has had another hard
working crew of local tradespeople
doing a great job of rebuilding her origi-
nal outbuildings and a small extension
to the back of the house.Welcome back
to our much-loved Tagan Dibden, who
has shrugged off her halo and returned
to earth. Tagan, who must have been
feeling like a Beaconsfield Mine disas-
ter, has had a metal frame drilled to her
skull and reaching down to her upper
body to keep her neck immobilised for
the past five months. She broke a disc
high in her spine in a car accident and
fortunately it has fused back together
without the necessity of surgery. Life
has been very frustrating for Tagan
and her family and many friends are
so excited for her.

Firmness Dignity And Respect
Dear Editor,
This letter should have been sent
much earlier than this but I have had
to be in Canberra for medical reasons
straight after our recent public meet-
ing in Bermagui re Meads Bay devel-
opment at Wallaga Lake. I can only say
that I have been appalled at the down-
right unwarranted attack on Council-
lor David Hede over his handling of the
Chair at that meeting. 
I invited Mr. Hede to take on the
position as he has paid us the courtesy
of attending every meeting we have
held re our concerns over such inap-
propriate development while other
councillors, including our own repre-
sentative, have ignored us.
During my 84 years I have at-
tended an awful lot of meetings and
Mr. Hede’s chairmanship was carried
out with firmness, dignity and respect
for the Chair. He at no time made com-
ment about the subject at hand. To at-
tack him in such a way shows a mean-
spirited, undemocratic approach to the
whole procedure. 
 I can only thank most sincerely
Mr Hede’s competence and patience
during what was at times a feisty de-
bate. As for Mr Brock’s sad story about
his caring for the environment and
being so badly done by, then I would
suggest that it is not too late for him to
realise the affect of what he wants to
do to beautiful Meads Bay and maybe
donate this piece of land back to the
community where it belongs.
Patricia Thompson

New Moorheads Beach/Keating
Headland Dunecare Group
The first meeting of the
Moorheads Beach/ Keating Headland
Dunecare Group was held at the north-
ern end of Moorheads Beach on Sun-
day, 9 September 2007. The Group is
responsible for the area between the
southern end of Moorheads Beach to
the northern end of Little Haywards
Beach. An appraisal was made of
weeds and other environmental prob-
lems associated with the area and it
was agreed that the first project of an
ongoing nature would be the removal
of Sea Spurge from the dunes adjoin-
ing the beaches.
Members were made aware of
the dangers associated with Sea Spurge
and the protective clothing to be worn
when removing it. The Group has 15
members who work either individu-
ally in their own time or as a team
member for an hour or so once a month.
The Group welcomes new members
and further information can be ob-
tained by contacting Lance Carder on
6493 3153.
