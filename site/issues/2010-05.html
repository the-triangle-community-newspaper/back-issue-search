As ANZAC day draws closer,
the contrast of the 2010 Australian
comes to mind. The larrikin, the rogue,
the mateship, the devil-may-care, all

Australian, against the myriad of rules
and regulations we
now face.
Founded as a
colony of convicts and
misfits, those traits
have 
forged 
the
‘Australian character’.
The country and our
innate ‘have a go’
attitude have lead to
an astonishing display
of inventiveness and
ingenuity.

stump jump plough,
combine harvester,
permaculture, 
the

The Larrikin versus

How can you do more with less?
By joining QuipShare to source
equipment that you don’t want to buy.
QuipShare is a community-
based equipment borrowing and
lending program. Sharing means that
more use is made of tools and
equipment that may otherwise be
under-utilised.
QuipShare was the idea of
Danuta Graham from Tilba. With the
help of some other Tilba Market
stallholders and the LETS (Local
Exchange Trading System) managers
in Bega, the system is now up and
running. Equipment is exchanged for
Quips that are equivalent to $1. You
can also use your Quips in the LETS
system. Registration in QuipShare is
free, and you can borrow before you
lend! For more information go to
quipshare.org.au or pick up a flyer at
the Tilba Growers Market.
Another good reason to get
involved is that it puts you in touch
with people who have the same
interests as you. If they have the
equipment you want, or want the
equipment you have, then chances are
they are doing what you like to do.
Do you have equipment in your
kitchen cupboard or garden shed that
you don’t use for weeks at a time? Then
get 
it 
moving! 
Register 
at
quipshare.org.au and put those items
to work. Offer to lend them and then
see what you can borrow.
If you’re not the computing type,
then contact a local QuipShare
coordinator who will act as your agent.

The Healthy Soils, Healthy
Farms Project is holding a field day at
Haxstead, Central Tilba on Friday 7
May.
Healthy Soils Healthy Farms is
a project of the Far South Coast
Landcare Association. The project will
run for four years to look at ways of
improving soil health and the
potential to increase the amount of
carbon that can be stored in the soil.
Each year soil and plant tissue
tests will be taken, pasture assessment
undertaken and biological treatments
applied. This will be followed by a field
day at each site to review the results
and analyse what changes have
occurred.
The field day will cover:
paddock sub division and location of
watering points, analysis of the soil
test results, the potential for biological
fertilisers, grazing management, plant
identification, pasture assessment and
tree planting for success.
Haxstead is one of two
demonstration sites. The other field
day is at Brianderry at Springvale, near
Bega, on Thursday 6 May.  Each site
will have six plots – one, a control plot,
the others with wide spaced trees,
conventional 
treatment, 
worm
leachate, commercial biological
fertiliser, and compost tea.
Paddock design and grazing
strategies are an important part of the
project, using smaller paddocks with
high stocking densities. The grazing
period will be short, with sufficient
recovery period so that stock does not
return until the pasture is fully
regrown.
Arrive at 9am for tea or coffee
before we start. Morning tea will be
provided. For catering purposes
bookings are essential. Please BYO
lunch.
For further information and
bookings contact Rod Logan 6491 8224
or rodney.logan@cma.nsw.gov.au.
The Healthy Soils Healthy
Farms Project has been funded by the
Australian Government through the
Caring for Our Country Initiative.

in the forest.
The scat, which appeared to be
from a young animal, was found on
Saturday two kilometres from where
the huge logging machines are
currently felling trees, and less than 500
metres from where they will move if
logging progresses as planned.
While the scat was found just
inside Biamanga National Park, it will
add 
pressure 
to 
calls 
by
conservationists to stop the logging,
which resumed earlier in April. The
scat has been verified as genuine by a
koala expert on the staff of the
Department of Environment, Climate
Change and Water.
The find comes as tensions in the
forest escalated and eight protesters
were trapped in the National Park
adjacent to the logging site for almost
12 hours. Conservationists will be
calling on the government to stop the
logging in the light of this new evidence.
This new find shows just how
inadequate forestry surveys have been

in an area that is the stronghold of the

Steve Maginnity looks just like
any other young man roused from his
second cup of coffee and the weekend
papers on this sunny morning. Get him
talking about his long association with
the Rural Fire Service (RFS) and his face
lights up and it’s easy to see his
enthusiasm and commitment to the
organisation.
Parents 
Barb 
and 
Tony
Maginnity moved their young family
from the Central Coast of NSW to Brogo
so that their children could enjoy a
country lifestyle. Steve started school
at Bega Primary and went on to Bega
High. He was a competent student and
a keen member of the Bega Swimming
Club. He also worked up to three
afternoons a week at Portelli’s (which
later became the Brogo River Bakery),
and did general farm work for the
Ypma’s of a weekend. After graduating
from school, Steve headed to the
University of NSW to study industrial
chemistry. He completed the first year
Who Lives in Brogo?
but felt that his destiny did not lie in
that direction. The following year,
local accountancy firm Kothes Pty
Limited took on this young man and
encouraged him to enrol in an
Accounting Degree at the University
of Wollongong, Bega Campus. He
worked for Kothes for a period of time
before joining the Sapphire Coast
Anglican College in June 2009 as the
Assistant Business Manager.
Tony, Steve’s father, joined what
was then the NSW Bush Fire Brigade,
as a volunteer shortly after the family
settled in Brogo. That’s over twenty
years ago now. Back then, before the
introduction of OH&S regulations,
members got together, learnt from
each other and responded to fires in
their area, without too much formal
structure. Steve loved to tag along
with the team to hazard reduction
burns around the Brogo area,
sometimes in his Dad’s old overalls,
boots and a spare helmet. He has clear
memories of a big burn on Peter
Thompson’s place, and helping (or
possibly just getting in the way) at
working bees to construct the new
(now 10 years old) fire shed.
These days, with the changes to
the NSW RFS, and the need to be more
formally trained, Steve has completed
mainstream Bush Firefighting courses
up to Crew Leader level, together with
several other arbitrary courses
including Village Firefighting,
Conduct Briefings and Debriefings,
Leadership, and OH&S. He was elected
as Captain of the Brogo Brigade in
2008. He attributes his advancement
to this role, as one of the younger
Captains in the Bega Valley, to the
mentoring he received. His father,
previous Captains including Trevor
Reeves, and Group Captain Brian
Ayliffe, as well as dedicated members
such 
as 
Kerry 
Noble 
were
inspirational and always willing to
guide him along the way.
What have Steve’s experiences
with the Rural Fire Service given to
him? His face comes alight again. He
believes that young people today do
not always have a sense of purpose
and common focus. ‘Something I was
once told, by a psychologist friend of
mine, who happened to be a young
person during that period, was that
during the War years the rate of
depression in young people apparently
tended to be very low. Although he
would never wish a war upon
anybody, he notes that during those
times the Nation came together with
a common purpose, much like it did
last year during the Black Saturday
Bushfires. The RFS, although very
different in nature to the military,
offers a sense of purpose and belonging
to those willing to give, and to that
end, giving something back into your
community can be a very uplifting
and satisfying endeavour’ he says.
He would love to see more
young people join, especially teenagers
who can join as Cadet Firefighters and
enjoy learning new skills and
experience the camaraderie which
comes from working with, and
depending on, a team in high pressure
situations such as that of a fireground.
Steve says, ‘Young Sam, who is a
fourteen year old Cadet with the Brogo
Brigade, has developed into a very
competent young person. She is
currently the only cadet in our
brigade, but she holds her own. Like
me, her father is an active member of
the brigade, but she is very
independent and gets out there and
gets on with it. If we could have more
‘Sams’ step up and volunteer; the
future of the RFS would be in good
hands, and perhaps these young people
would share in the great enjoyment
and fulfillment I have found in my
service with the RFS’.

held on Easter Saturday, was another
great day put on by dedicated
volunteers. The passing rain showers
did little to dampen the spirits. This
year the new organising committee
worked hard to create opportunities
for participation. The greasy pole was

nimble climbers make their way to
ring the bell. Recently arrived Tilba

‘eggsciting’ action. They are a great
yolk. This year the cheese roll was
moved down the street to the front of
the ABC Cheese factory. Many thanks
to ABC Cheese for their generous
sponsorship of this unique game.

Agricultural Marquee was another
new event on festival day and each
category was entered. This event will
only grow bigger and better next year
as the competition will increase.

WIRES
WIRES is Australia’s leading
wildlife rescue service and its
volunteers rescue thousands of native
animals every year. Sadly, domestic cat
attack is the most common cause of
injury to native wildlife in urban areas.
There are over three million pet
cats in Australia. Even though they are
domesticated they are still responsible
for millions of the deaths of our native
animals. On the average a domestic cat
will kill 16 mammals, eight birds and
eight reptiles a year.
This is a terrible price that our
native animals must pay for people
allowing their cats to roam free. This
figure does not include the attacks that
go unreported – the many native
animals which simply die in the bush
from their injuries.
Please never, ever dump an
unwanted cat in the bush. Feral cats
live miserable lives and are extremely
destructive to wildlife.
The local branch of WIRES is
having a Basic Training Course on 22
and 23 May near Tura Beach. If you are
interested in joining and would like to
get more details, please ring the WIRES
hotline: 6495 4150. We would love to
hear from you.

Pumpkin and Fetta Pasta
Prepare 700g pumpkin, peeled
and cut into small pieces, and baked
until tender in a 220 degree oven
sprinkled with 2 teaspoons of caraway
seeds and 2 tablespoons of olive oil.
Something other
than Pumpkin
Soup!
Meanwhile cook 250g of
spaghetti or other pasta in
boiling water and drain.
Combine the hot pasta with the
pumpkin, 250g diced fetta
cheese, 2 tablespoons of
chopped Italian parsley and 50g
of toasted pine nuts or almonds.
Drizzle with a dressing of 80ml
olive oil, 60ml lemon juice, 2
tablespoons of white wine
vinegar and one crushed garlic
clove if desired. Season, toss and
serve warm with a green salad.
A delicious variation on
pumpkin soup is the following
version using carrots, in season
now, pumpkin and the last of
the corn or some from a tin. The
following recipe will make four hearty

Green and Gold Soup
Six spring onions (or 2 medium
onions and chives). Finely slice
onions, reserving the green tops for
a garnish, and simmer until soft in
2 tablespoons of butter or oil. Add
one cup of finely chopped carrot and
one teaspoon of cumin and sauté for
a few minutes before adding 2 cups
of chopped pumpkin, corn cut off 3—
4 cobs or a 440g tin of creamed corn,
3 cups of vegetable stock and salt
and pepper to taste. Simmer until
cooked. Add finely chopped onion
tops or chives a minute before serving.

Summer has lingered, but cooler
weather will inevitably be here. It’s an
ideal time to plant trees and shrubs,
and to remember that if a plant is
worth having then it is worth
investing time and energy in the
planting process. If you are considering
planting deciduous trees for autumn
display, it’s a good time to select them
at your nursery as you will be able to
see their colour. The colour of
individuals can vary a great deal,
especially 
varieties 
such 
as
Liquidambar.These trees need a sunny
location with adequate space for their
full development.
Some camellias are already
blooming, a process which will
continue 
through 
the 
winter
months.They flower at an early age and
are useful for screening or hedging.
Get ready in
Autumn for
Spring
They are quite happy to be trimmed
or can be allowed to grow naturally;
the dark green foliage is attractive in
its own right . Now is the time to plant
spring flowering bulbs in a sunny
position where they will make a
cheery display. Bulbs should be
planted at a depth of
about twice their
diameter, 
or, 
put
another way, covered
with a layer of soil
equal to the diameter
of the bulb.
I mentioned in
last month’s column
that I would talk about
what vegies to plant
now. I won’t give a
definitive list, but
suggest some that you
might like to try - peas,
lettuce, 
beetroot,
onions, garlic, broad
beans, radish, cress
and carrots. Of course
U
there will be others, but you should be
guided by your own experience and
climatic area.
In a month or two your local
nursery will have asparagus and
rhubarb crowns available. Every
garden should have these as
permanent plantings. Also valuable
are perennial leeks, which provide
leeks for many months with minimal
effort.
Pea seed can be tricky to
germinate; if the soil is wet, the seed is
likely to rot. I get good results with an
open friable soil, just moist, the seed
pressed in a little and covered with
coarse sand. Do not water until the
seedlings begin to emerge.
With a little planning, you can
have a garden that is productive for
both the body and the soul all the year
round,
