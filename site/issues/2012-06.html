How to kill a town without really trying
The media is full of stories of small 
Australian towns struggling to maintain their 
businesses and populations. In our own area 
there are more small towns or villages than 
there are large towns and they are struggling.
If you live in one of these small towns you 
become aware of the diversity of people and the 
entrepreneurial nature of a lot of the residents. 
Ideas abound, none of them grandiose – just a 
small business, supposedly the backbone of the 
Australian economy, that someone thinks is a 
good idea and may just make them a living and 
add another attraction to the small town.
Then – smack! – they hit a wall of 
bureaucracy.
Cobargo is one of these small towns. A 
number of new businesses have submitted DAs 
to Council, to find that Council is demanding 
the business provide a disabled access toilet. 
It must be pointed out that this is not 
a Council bylaw. In May 2011 the Federal 
Government passed new anti-discrimination 
laws:
The Disability (Access to Premises - 
buildings) Standards 2010 (the Premises 
Standards) commence on 1 May 2011. Any 
application for a building approval for a new 
building or upgrade of an existing building on 
or after that date will trigger the application 
of the Premises Standards. These standards 
stipulate that toilet facilities are to be access 
friendly for disabled people.
However, and this is where communication 
between Council staff and shopkeepers falls 
down, there are grounds for exception, all of 
which probably relate to Cobargo. 
To comply with the DA, existing places 
may need to be modified to be accessible 
‘except where this would involve “unjustified 
hardship”’. Section 4 of the Australian Human 
Rights Commission Disability Guidelines give 
a comprehensive list of “unjustified hardship” 
guidelines but I have cut and pasted just two 
examples: 
Limited application to new parts and 
‘affected part’ in existing buildings
When new building work takes place in 
an existing building and a building approval 
is required for that new work, the requirements 
for upgrading access are limited to the area 
of new work and the ‘affected part’. Access 
requirements are not imposed outside the area 
of the new work.
Toilet concession
Section 4.5 provides a concession for 
existing accessible sanitary compartments. 
The concession states that certain existing 
accessible sanitary compartments do not have 
to comply with AS 1428.1–2009 as generally 
required by the Access Code.
Also on page 17 of the NSW State Anti-
Discrimination Guidelines for Local Councils 
it states:
Public toilets:
All of Council’s public toilets and baby 
change facilities — in parks, recreation 
areas, reserves, halls, and so on — need to be 
provided equally for both sexes and for people 
with disabilities (and for anyone who needs to 
accompany them). Not to do so could be either 
sex or disability discrimination. 
And it is this issue that is getting up the 
nose in Cobargo.  While Council strides through 
Cobargo stonewalling budding new businesses 
on the grounds of their toilets, Council itself is 
avoiding upgrading its own toilets.
The Cobargo Business and Tourism 
Association has been asking Council for an 
upgrade of the existing appalling public toilets 
for years and nothing has been done. Instead, 
Council is demanding that all private businesses 
comply with a law from which they would in 
all probability be found to be exempt under the 
“unjustified hardship” rules. 
And to add insult to injury, the latest ‘must 
eat in’ restaurant in Sydney, Momofuku Seiobo, 
which charges $540.00 for a dinner for two, 
doesn’t have to provide toilets for their patrons. 
They have to go outside the restaurant and use 
the public toilets!
BVSC, a reply to this would be very 
welcome.
Louise Brown

Where in the triangle do you live?
Here, where else?
How long have you been there?
I am always amused by how obsessed with 
time you humans are. I am here now and it is 
always now.
Where did you live before that?
(OK, forget that question – SG)
What do you do with your time?
Again, how amusing. I be. I’m often told that 
that’s really boring, but really, isn’t my presence 
enough?
What do you like the most about living in The 
Triangle?
I like the sun, the wind that caresses me, the 
rain. I like the birds that sing in my hair and 
the possums that scratch at my trunk. I like 
providing shelter for furry woodland creatures 
and spotted frogs. I am a natural apartment 
block. I particularly enjoy checking out those 
lady Casuarinas down the gully.
What do you like the least about living in The 
Triangle?
Well, once I had a dream that I could be a noble 
dining table or a chest of drawers or something, 
I mean I will die one day and I would like to 
continue being useful. But with the council and 
the greenies here I’ve given up on that.
What changes have you seen in the time you’ve 
been in the Triangle?
I’ve seen the proliferation of some species: 
humans, cows, dogs and the decline of many 
others. There are fewer species of birds around. 
Seems to be fewer wombats. And many of my 
friends have been cut down and carted away. 
Pity people wouldn’t listen to the greenies 
about that.
What’s next for you?
I continue to be. As I said, isn’t my presence 
enough?

Hawksbill Turtle back home, and a very lucky little bird
WIRES would like to thank two very 
caring people, Lynne Grant and Alex Dryver 
who found a very sick and exhausted Hawksbill 
Turtle at Bournda 8 months ago.  Motchy (the 
turtle) was initially cared for by WIRES member 
Janine Green.  After his stay with Janine, 
Motchy was transferred to the Merimbula 
Aquarium where he was housed and looked 
after for 8 months!  
On Monday, 26 March, Motchy was 
deemed fit to go back home to the ocean.  It was 
a very special day. Lynne and Alex, Janine and 
Michael McMaster who cared for Motchy at the 
Aquarium, and Anthony Daly, the Aquarium’s 
owner, were chauffeured from Merimbula 
to Narooma by National Parks and Wildlife 
Ranger, Craig Dickmann.
In Narooma they were all greeted by 
National Parks and Wildlife Ranger Cassandra 
Bendixsen, and skipper Ross Constable, who 
took them by boat to Montague Island for the 
release.  However, upon arriving at Montague 
Island, the rangers felt that there were too many 
fishermen around the island and so Motchy 
was taken another 300 metres NE of Montague 
Island to the shelf where he was safely placed in 
the water and he promptly dove down into the 
depths of the ocean. Motchy was so happy to be 
back home that he forgot to wave good-bye!
One Friday afternoon WIRES received 
a call to rescue a bird in Wyndham. It had 
been hit by a car. Upon arrival, the WIRES 
volunteer found this beautiful little Black- 
faced Monarch, weighing all of 23 grams. 
The driver thought he had avoided hitting the 
bird while driving home with his family to 
Wyndham. However, what he did not know 
was that it had become trapped on the front 
of his car.  One of his children discovered 
it on the ground after arriving home. The 
little bird had travelled between 10 and 15 
kilometres on the front of the car after being 
hit.  It was unbelievably quite bright looking 
but unable to fly.  At an early examination it 
appeared to be concussed but otherwise to 
only have minimal injuries.
A later vet check confirmed the 
concussion and amazingly, considering 
what had happened to it, no broken bones 
or dislocations were found. After a couple of 
days treating it for shock with warmth and 
rehydration and feeding it mealie worms at 
quite a rate, it was ready for the aviary.  It 
still could not fly but perched well and made 
its way quickly 
a r o u n d t h e 
branches. Fresh insect 
laden branches were 
put into the aviary 
twice daily and it would spend 
the time happily foraging for 
the insects. It also ate crickets 
with great gusto. After five 
days it started flying across 
the aviary and three days later 
was flying strongly and able 
to be released. It was just the 
luckiest bird not to have been severely injured. 
And lucky because it was found by a family that 
cared enough about it to call WIRES.

Narooma builder penalised for water pollution
Ed Kelly Constructions Pty Ltd pleaded 
guilty to a water pollution charge in Batemans 
Bay Local Court on Friday and was convicted 
and ordered to pay more than $24,000 in fines 
and costs.  
The NSW Environment Protection 
Authority (EPA) prosecuted Ed Kelly 
Constructions Pty Ltd for polluting the waters 
of Forsters Bay near Narooma, which forms 
part of the Wagonga Inlet and is located within 
Batemans Marine Park.
The incident occurred on December 2 and 
3, 2010, when sediment and erosion controls 
put in place by the construction company failed 
during a period of heavy rainfall. 
In handing down his judgment, Magistrate 
Degnan said the company must be alive to 
the sensitivity of the marine environment at 
Forsters Bay.
Magistrate Degnan said the construction 
company should have taken greater precautions 
to ensure its sediment and erosion controls 
would be able to withstand periods of heavy 
rainfall. 
Acting Chief Environmental Regulator 
Mark Gifford said sediment laden water posed 
a potential threat to any marine ecosystem, but 
was particularly concerning when it entered a 
declared Sanctuary Zone.
“Sediment may seem harmless, but it is 
a serious marine pollutant and Forsters Bay 
contains high conservation value species and 
ecosystems,” Mr Gifford said.
“Sediment plume pollution can reduce 
light penetration, smother the marine bed 
habitat, harm the gills of fish, crustaceans and 
molluscs, and reduce the food supply for some 
marine life.
“Parts of Forsters Bay are also used for 
oyster farming and sediment laden water can 
harm this industry significantly.
“Heavy rainfall made erosion and sediment 
control difficult for Ed Kelly Constructions Pty 
Ltd, but they had already received a formal 
EPA caution for an incident of water pollution 
that occurred on the same site on September 
10, 2010.
“Sediment is a harmful pollutant in 
waterways and construction companies must 
follow best practice and budget adequately to 
avoid allowing sediment to enter the marine 
environment,” Mr Gifford said.

Yowrie Ride with the 
Cobargo Trail Riders Club
On Sunday 20th May we held a 
great birthday ride for local identity (and 
octogenarian) Leone. The ride included a 
sausage sizzle and beautiful birthday cakes 
made by June and Trish. As for the ride, it could 
not have been better - and I say this each time I 
ride with the Cobargo Trail riders.
This time it was the 
best ever. The weather was 
perfect, the day starting 
with the beautiful drive to 
Yowrie with thick white 
mist lying in the valleys 
and the mountains clear 
and outlined above. The 
group of eleven riders rode 
out at 9.30am following an 
old historic bridle trail and forestry fire trails 
to reach the lunch spot on the Wadbilliga River 
Crossing, two and a half hours later.
The horses all went well, with several 
crossings of the fast flowing Yowrie River 
along the way. A few deep holes were unseen 
and those riders on smaller horses - and some 
of us on the bigger ones, got wet feet. 
The ride was through Aussie bush at its 
best. The sound of rapids in the creek kept 
us company for most of the way as we rode 
through great tall trees and lush understorey, 
with lyrebirds giving us a concert as we 
followed Richard home. 
On getting back to the floats the time had 
come to join the real world and head for home. 
But the memories of the day will stay with us 
all for some time to come.
A big thank you to Les and Tricia for the 
tea and sausage sizzle all set up for us when we 
arrived for lunch. The day would not have been 
as good without them.  
Judi Hearn

Bermagui Woolworths? - a move away from community to corporate capitalism  
in the name of globalisation and growth
An extract from David Arvind Condon’s 
latest film: We are the earth – what we do to the 
earth, we do to ourselves.
Corporate globalisation doesn’t know 
how to explore issues and problems in depth 
with an attitude of examination in order to 
achieve real solutions for the local community, 
the environment and humanity; they only know 
how to propagate and make money and often 
in an immoral way.
Large multinational corporations 
enjoy privileged status granted to them by 
governments; the growth and profit of large 
corporations pre-empt local and national 
interests. The law benefits the corporation at 
the expense of the people and the environment. 
This is how it works: corporate bottom line is 
profit for its directors and shareholders; most of 
the money is taken out of the local community, 
through automated checkout. Produce comes 
cheap, imported and chemically treated 
(sometimes with proven toxic effects), while 
our local farmers who produce fresh produce are 
being displaced by the chemical manufacturers 
– read the labels and research the chemicals that 
are being used. The experience of friendly face-
to-face shopping, where social contact is nearly 
as important as the transaction, is an image 
on which Woolworths have spent millions of 
dollars of TV advertising. It is a corporate 
image we have come to know to be as phony as 
the corporations that pay for those smiles; we 
are all being falsely conditioned with celebrity 
culture imagery. Next time you see one of those 
ads ask the smiley face about something local 
and imagine what they might say.
By positioning Woolworths in Bermagui, 
the strip of small independent stores will 
be displaced by the new traffic/pedestrian flow. 
This puts them at a disadvantage and what 
usually happens is that they are forced to close.
A globalisation model works like this – if 
I cut down a forest, build a logging road, hire 
labour and transport it to a buyer, I convert a 
forest to timber, a commodity, and the GDP goes 
up. Similarly if I can find a traditional society 
that uses herbs and shamanic techniques for 
healing, then destroy their culture and make 
them dependent on pharmaceutical medicine 
which they must purchase, evict them from their 
land so they cannot be subsistence farmers and 
must buy food, clear their land and hire them 
on a banana or palm oil plantation – I make the 
world richer.  I have brought various functions, 
relationships, and natural resources into the 
realm of money. This process is a conversion 
of social capital, natural capital, cultural capital, 
and spiritual capital into money. Essentially 
for the economy to continue growing and for 
the (interest-based) money system to remain 
viable, more and more of nature and human 
relationship must be monetised.  For example, 
thirty years ago most meals were prepared 
at home; today some two-thirds are prepared 
outside, in restaurants or supermarket delis. 
A once unpaid function, cooking, has become 
a paid ‘service’.  And we are the richer for it, 
right?
Corporate globalisation is the process 
of stripping away these grass roots and 
greenhouse friendly assets to feed the money 
machine’s insatiable appetite. The continuation 
of capitalism as we know it depends on an 
infinite supply of these new industries, which 
essentially must convert infinite new realms of 
social, natural, cultural, and spiritual capital 
into money. The problem is, these resources are 
finite, and the closer they come to exhaustion, 
the more painful their extraction becomes. 
Therefore, contemporaneous with the financial 
crisis we have an ecological crisis and a health 
crisis.  They are intimately interlinked.
“When plunder becomes a way of life for 
a group of men in a society, over the course 
of time they create for themselves a legal 
system that authorizes it and a moral code that 
glorifies it”.      
 - Frederic Bastiat, Economist 1801-1850
Capitalism, globalisation, deregulation 
and western civilisation debt-driven economies 
have led to a collapse of the financial sector, 
as we have seen through the global financial 
crisis in 2008, which is merely a symptom of a 
systemic crisis of capitalism itself. Capitalism/
globalisation means increased exploitation of 
workers worldwide, destruction of our forests 
and the Earth’s natural recourses, and a decrease 
in the standard of living for the average person, 
which in turn creates civil unrest. This we are 
seeing more and more throughout the Middle-
East and the worldwide Occupy movement 
- mass demonstrations in the financial districts 
in over 900 cities. They are protesting social and 
economic inequality, corporate greed, corporate 
power and influence over government, united by 
the desire for global human rights, civil liberties 
and democratic governance.
There is an idea pushed by governments 
and corporations that the economy must grow 
indefinitely, without a recognition that the 
resources are finite. Sure, you can get away 
with a certain level of growth, but the net effect 
is cannibalisation of the very resources that 
allow the economy to exist in the first place. 
Anyone who buys into endless growth hype is 
part of the problem. Now that we understand 
the consequences of this kind of thinking this 
attitude is no longer acceptable.
“You can ignore reality, but you can’t 
ignore the consequences of ignoring reality.”  
-  Ayn Rand
Free market policies are dominating 
the world through the exploitation of the 
environment and impoverishing the people 
through capitalised globalisation. The result as 
I see it is catastrophic for all of us.
That’s why I am personally against any 
form of a multinational corporation moving 
into Bermagui, displacing our local farmers 
and small independent stores and changing this 
unique area from our beautiful seaside village 
into another suburb of soulless consumerism. It 
is nothing short of environmental, architectural 
and economic vandalism.

Our Bush Tucker garden was completed 
in 2009, with the help of Merryn Carey, Les 
Kosez and funded by Australian Government 
Indigenous Co-ordination Centre. We invited 
the community and had a planting day. Teaching 
and learning notes were created as well as 
signage. There are 36 different plants in the 
garden, including ‘Orray’ Davidsons Plum, 
‘Wom-bai’ Lemon Myrtle and ‘Panaryle’ 
Snowy River Mint. 
It helps us to maintain Indigenous culture 
through community involvement, support 
new forms of Indigenous Cultural expression 
and increases public awareness of Indigenous 
culture.
There are four areas in our school – 
rainforest, orchard, screening and vegetables.  
Native animal sculptures are found 
throughout the gardens.
Students enjoy exploring and sitting in 
the gardens at lunch time. The students look 
forward to trying the tucker and finding out 
more about the local plants.

The Triangle’s Eco Edge Competition
Local company AKT is sponsoring a page in The Triangle, initially 
for six months, offering the residents of this district the opportunity 
of describing how they see, sense and savour our wonderful natural 
environment.
Maybe with the right interactions we will be able to discover those 
secrets, be they trees, animals or even a view that is held dear, but 
maybe not previously shared - those ecological niches that make our 
hearts sing and spirits soar even on cold winter mornings. 
We all have that special spot, that favourite view or that oasis of 
sensation where our soul melds in with nature. 
This will be your opportunity not to lose those images – well, at 
least not without expressing your feelings of acknowledgment and 
appreciation in print… and such imprints need to be shared.
In this adventure we encourage school students from the Triangle 
region to contribute essays of up to 500 words. On this page we will print 
those essays that express the most vivid environmental images you are 
able to describe. They may be simply a picture in words, or aspirations 
for our land, or maybe reﬂ ecting on your frustrations with the way we 
adults handle ecological matters.   
After the six months, AKT will donate two prizes of $200 to the 
schools of the students (one primary age, one high school age) with 
the most poignant essays. A sub-committee of The Triangle will judge 
the winning essays, which will be published in the December issue. 
The criteria for the winning essays will not necessarily be their literary 
merit but the ability of the essay to best sing to us songs of the bush, 
creeks or beaches.
In the meantime, every month we will publish contributions from 
the wider community. We wish for this page to remain dedicated to 
the sharing of these private or public natural treasures we have in the 
Triangle. 
All submissions will be archived and kept as possible historic records 
of what exists today, what we may lose or what is worthy of preservation. 
We therefore invite those with such messages and feelings to send 
us an essay that can further adorn that part of our environment that 
contributes to our sanity and serenity.

Gardening Gabble
Keith Mundy
Brrr – winter is here again
Wow, here we are in another season and 
with so much still to do in the garden before the 
winter hibernation gets under way.
Winter brings with it a number of many 
important jobs in the garden and topping the 
list is the pruning of fruit trees and roses and 
the winter spraying of these plants to remove 
insect and fungal pests that think they can over 
winter on the plants.
The stages of this work is important 
especially where larger plants are concerned 
as the last thing we need to do is use excessive 
amounts of chemical to eradicate the pest. 
Basically by this I mean that the pruning should 
be completed before the application of the 
chemical, thus not wasting chemical on material 
that will be pruned off.
An important factor to also 
remember when pruning is to 
wait until the plants have gone 
into complete dormancy before 
the pruning takes place. This 
will discourage plants to re shoot 
and new growth be burnt off by 
a frost.
There are generally two 
chemicals to use, one for the 
fungal problems and one for 
insect problems.
Winter oil or dormant oil is 
used for the insect problems and 
lime sulphur for fungal problems. 
It is important to use both of these chemicals 
before there is any sign of the new buds swelling 
in early spring.
Moving along from your existing fruit 
and rose plants to some new plants that you 
might be considering purchasing. Now is the 
time to do this is you are looking for the most 
cost effective way to purchase, that being open 
or bare rooted.
Should you prefer this method of 
purchasing your plants there are several 
important things to look out for when making 
your selection. Firstly is the plant healthy? Do 
not purchase any plant that has any shrivelled 
wood or bark as this indicates that the plant has 
been allowed to dry out and the plant could fail 
to regrow when the warmer weather arrives. 
Check to see that there is not evidence of any 
pest or disease. Things like scale or fungal 
disease on the stems or swellings on the root 
system that could indicate a root borne pest. 
Lastly, take some hessian or an old blanket 
with you when you go to the nursery to make 
your purchase so the roots can be wrapped up 
to prevent them drying out on the trip home. 
Either plant them straight away or “heal” them 
in to some moist loose soil until you are ready 
to plant.     
There are many other tasks that should be 
done during this month including 
the planting of strawberry, 
rhubarb and asparagus crowns. 
These are found as bare rooted 
plants in nurseries until early 
August. Also the tidying up 
of late summer and autumn 
flowering shrubs to encourage 
early spring growth when the 
warm weather arrives. Do not 
prune spring flowering shrubs 
now as you will be cutting off the 
growth that carries the flowering 
wood.
Continue with mulching 
of all the garden beds with a suitable mulch 
to protect the close to surface roots from the 
extremes of the cold frosts and to discourage 
early spring weed growth.
One very important factor we often forget 
about in winter is watering. Plants still dry out 
in winter whether in pots or in the ground. 
Although it is not done as often in winter it still 
needs to be done.
Till next time keep rugged up and enjoy 
some homemade pumpkin soup from the garden 
pumpkins of last summer.

Heather O’Connor
Carrie Tiffany
Mateship with Birds 
Picador, $19.99
This is the second novel from this 
Australian author, following her highly 
successful first offering – Everyman’s Rules 
for Scientific Living, which, according to my 
friends, is well worth reading. Her background 
as an agricultural journalist shines through, as 
does her knowledge of country Victoria. What 
is more surprising is her uncanny ability to 
recreate 1950s country Victoria – unlike me, she 
didn’t grow up in it, but everything she writes 
resonates with the community I remember.
Mateship with Birds is set in Cohuna, in 
the northern part of the State, and centres on the 
life of Betty Reynolds, an unmarried mother 
of two children, Michael and Hazel, and their 
friendship with their near neighbour, Harry. 
Harry is a dairy farmer, obsessed with his life 
as a dairy farmer, but also with the birds with 
whom he shares his property. Abandoned by his 
dissatisfied wife, Harry befriends Betty and her 
family, and takes on many of the responsibilities 
of a father to Betty’s children, including his 
attempt to provide sex education for young 
Michael, which threatens the friendship, but 
the tension is finally resolved.
The descriptions of bird life are truly 
beautiful, much of it written in free-flow poetry, 
and all the characters are totally believable – 
even the evil ones. I highly recommend this, 
especially if you can read the two hundred 
pages in one go.
I was amazed to discover that one of my 
oldest friends has never read any Kurt Vonnegut, 
so I bought him a copy of Slaughterhouse 5, and 
re-read it before handing it over. I’d forgotten 
what a great anti-war book it is – and now 
Penguin has re-released this and some of his 
other books – all for $12 – ask at Candelo Books 
if you’re interested.
