Tilba Festival 2023 
There was lots of positive feedback 
about the new-look Tilba Festival that 
popped up on Bate Street on 29 July thanks 
to a small but committed committee.
Susan Gray, President of the Tilba 
Chamber of Commerce said that despite 
the change of date (from Easter), this 
year’s festival was a great success. ‘The 
enjoyment of attendees on the day was 
obvious! There was a lovely, relaxed, 
community vibe. The response has been 
very positive since the event,’ she said.
Just five people worked tirelessly in 
the months leading up to the festival to 
get it off the ground: Jasmine Bond, the 
Festival Coordinator held it all together; 
Kristy Randall and Christine Montague 
enlisted and coordinated volunteers; Jo 
Major provided general support; and Tara 
Moore promoted the festival far and wide.
This year’s Festival drew about 1750 
visitors plus stallholders, volunteers, 
musicians etc, which is down on previous 
attendances.
So many people to thank…
A massive thanks goes out to 
Eurobodalla Shire Council for its financial 
contribution for the day from its Events 
Support Program and its support in 
promoting the event, along with Narooma 
News and 2EC Radio. 
‘Thanks to local business Poo Carters 
who donated the portaloos for the festival, 
to Shanna Provost for donating her MC 
services, to Jasmine Bond for being 
the primary organiser of the event, and 
thanks to the committee and to the many 
volunteers who provided their services for 
free – we simply couldn’t do it without 
you!’ said Susan.
Jasmine Bond wants to thank all the 
people who came along and contributed 
to the happy festive vibe. ‘An especially 
huge thank you to Kristy Randall and 
Chrissy Montague, who spent weeks 
calling people, begging them to contribute 
a couple of hours to help on Festival day 
and then oversaw the logistics on the day.
‘A huge thank you to the vollies 
who did respond to the call – you looked 
after the 101 little things that no-one 
notices. Also, to Kristy’s sister Nikki, who 
magicked together an amazing kids’ space 
with next to no notice and a tiny budget. To 
Phil May, electrician extraordinaire, who 
got thrown a massive curve ball when we 
rearranged the festival layout at the last 
minute. Lastly, a shout out to Tara, the 
Angel with the Megaphone, whose support 
before and during the event was invaluable, 
and to Bruce who turned up unannounced 
early on Saturday morning and helped 
manage stallholder bump-in.’

Be prepared! … the motto for summer. 
Everyone’s talking weather and, while 
we are enjoying the sunny days, even the 
most conservative among us agree that it is 
unseasonably warm for winter and what’s 
summer going to be like? 
Water is going to become a critical 
issue, not just for those who depend on 
rainfall to collect their own supplies, but 
towns are going to face water shortages, 
especially with the influx of visitors over 
the summer months. It is inevitable that 
there will be restrictions put in place, so 
why not start now? When there is plenty 
of it, we forget about conserving water and 
curtailing our usage. 
Not so long ago we were complaining 
about how fast the grass was growing and 
how often we needed to mow! But the 
lack of rain and high temperatures have 
quickly turned the landscape brown as we 
go into the warmer months facing another 
long, hot and scary summer. Forecasts and 
predictions, along with the heatwaves and 
fires being experienced in the northern 
hemisphere summer, all offer dire warnings 
about our coming summer.  Memories of 
the disastrous fire events that hit this region 
three summers ago are still raw, and the 
dry and parched landscape is a reminder of 
that time. There is much more information 
available now … let’s hope everyone is 
accessing it and getting prepared.
The Triangle has something big 
to celebrate … in September it will be 
21 years since the first paper was put 
together, printed and hit the streets, or 
the roads in this case, principally to the 
towns of Quaama, Cobargo, Tilba and 
Bermagui. Beginning as a seed of an idea 
in the Cobargo pub I believe, this free 
publication has faithfully appeared every 
month without fail, uniting and keeping 
the communities of this corner of far south 
NSW informed and entertained. The paper 
has kept up with technology and is now 
available online, in colour and past copies 
are easily accessed.
Thank you to the volunteer workers, 
contributors and the advertisers who make 
it all possible. Don’t forget to look out for 
those little tins on a counter where you 
pick up your Triangle, or donate through 
the 'tip jar' on our website. Especially now 
that we have so many online visits, around 
3000 last month! We appreciate and need 
your donations.
If you have any fond memories of 
particular issues of The Triangle, we would 
love you to share them with us.

A public apology - I got it wrong!
In the 1980s I was involved in a 
range of campaigns that aimed to improve 
the status of  women and girls. The main 
ones focused on education and economic 
opportunities for mature-aged women, 
particularly those living in regional areas. 
The quest for equal pay was/is never 
ending.
I remember when a very energetic 
group of women emerged to raise the 
issue of barriers facing women and girls 
in sport. I thought at the time this was at 
best a ‘second tier’ problem, at worst one 
that distracted from the serious challenges 
facing women. This small but determined 
collective of women persisted in arguing 
for more respect for sportswomen and for 
more just pay and conditions. 
Forty-five years on and the results 
of their commitment and persistence are 
being enjoyed by us all. 
Our women cricketers, netballers, 
tennis and golf players, swimmers, 
footballers of all codes dominate the 
international stage. Just as important is the 
huge number of young women and girls 
involved in community sport. 
We all revel in the success of the 
Matildas. Let’s also rejoice in our own 
local sporting teams and the countless 
volunteers who make their competitions 
possible. 
I happily acknowledge how wrong I 
got it. Sport has proved invaluable to the 
welfare and the status of women and girls. 
And how grateful we all are for the way our 
international sportswomen behave on and 
off the field – a model of how the world 
could be organised! 
Heather O’Connor

Empowering Women -  
Return to Work Pathways Program launches in the Bega Valley
A new program supporting women 
to transition back into the paid workforce 
has been launched in the Bega Valley. 
Delivered by Empowered Collective, in 
partnership with the St Vincent de Paul 
Society Canberra/Goulburn and Women 
NSW, the program aims to address some 
of the challenges of returning to training 
and paid work. 
Empowered Collective’s founding 
Director, Deborah Fulton notes that many 
women find it challenging to navigate the 
return-to-work process. ‘Our program 
focuses on eliminating some of those 
barriers, boosting self-confidence and 
providing tailored resources to facilitate 
the transition.
‘With community services at the 
centre of our work, we provide referrals 
to support services, information and 
resources, as well as skill-building and 
networking opportunities that empower 
women to navigate their future confidently.’
The program includes three workshops 
on 19 and 26 October and 2 November 
2023 from 10.00 am to 2.00 pm at the 
Bega Regional Learning Centre. Women 
will learn about updating their resumé and 
cover letter, current recruitment trends and 
job interviews. Local guest speakers will 
deliver information about managing stress, 
budgeting and personal presentation for 
the workplace.
Following the workshops, the 
participants will have individualised 
mentoring sessions to identify skills, 
experience and goals, explore career 
options, access support services and make 
informed decisions about their training and 
work pathways.
The program is free to women over 
eighteen years old who are living in the 
Bega Valley area. The program can assist 
with the cost of transport, childcare and 
respite care, to enable women to attend. 
Deborah also notes the current 
Batemans Bay program has been highly 
successful with one woman already 
securing paid work. Local businesses, 
community organisations and individuals 
are encouraged to become involved. 
For more information, to discuss 
eligibility and register, contact Empowered 
Collective on 0495 015 595 or email 
women@empoweredco.com.au. Or look 
up the Empowered Collective website for 
more information.
Deborah Fulton
Australia    
When I landed in Australia, the rain was pouring down.
I was standing at the dockside, in good old Melbourne town.
My wife, she stood beside me, her mother was with us too.
We’d travelled halfway round the world, to start in somewhere new.
The customs man was nice to us, he was kind, caring and polite
but, even so, he checked our bags. He had to do it right,
‘I bet you’re glad you left that weather, back home in your old town.’
I looked outside, up to the sky – the rain kept pouring down.
I thought Australians were funny, with the things that you hold dear:
your wife, your car, your kids, your house. But mostly it’s your beer.
You don’t seem to care about politics, that’s for the other guy.
As long as you can buy a slab, you’ll live and never die.
Pasties, meat pies and sausages – you can be sure they don’t contain beef.
They’re served so hot, you burn your lip and take the coating off your teeth.
There’s Vegemite, that dark black stuff, you spread upon your bread.
If you take too much, your eyes light up and you wish that you were dead.
There’s sportsmen here a-plenty and they are all true blue.
They’re playing for their country, they play for me and you.
Don’t worry about the money, don’t worry, have no regret –
they earn enough in one half day to pay the national debt.
The politicians love us, they need to gain our vote.
But, when they’re in power, it’s stuff you, boy. Now don’t you feel a dope?
Some want to change the flag on us and dump the Union Jack.
I see two big golden arches, large fries and a big mac.
Now for fifty or more years I’ve lived here, this land so rich and free,
it’s a land we’ve come to treasure, myself, my friends and me.
Now would I like to leave this land, go back and be a pom?
I wouldn’t want swap this place, this place where we now belong.
Thank you, Australia.
 Ray Stephens
Put your hand up for the Red Cross Aged Care Volunteer Visitors Scheme
The program
Having good social connections 
is essential for all aspects of health, 
especially as we age. Red Cross supports 
elderly  residents of the community 
to improve their wellbeing and social 
connection by matching volunteers with 
an older person who lives in an aged care 
facility or at home, has identified as being 
lonely and has minimum contact  with 
family and/or friends. Community visits 
 
have an important impact on the elderly 
person’s life: enhancing their sense of 
wellbeing, supporting good physical and 
mental health, promoting healthy ageing 
and making them more resilient to life’s 
knocks and challenges.
The opportunity
This role suits someone over the age 
of 18 who is a good listener and can commit 
to at least one hour a week for a social visit 
to an elderly person. What you do on your 
visit is up to you and who you are visiting: 
you might chat together, reminisce, listen 
to music, read the newspaper or play card 
games.
The impact
As a community visitor volunteer, 
you’ll brighten the life of a lonely and 
isolated older person by offering them 
company and friendship. You’ll also 
brighten your own life: gaining satisfaction, 
enjoyment and personal development 
through your visits. Red Cross will ensure 
you have training, ongoing support and 
access to other opportunities within the 
world’s largest humanitarian network.

opportunity please contact Belinda Rowe 
on 0472 973 690.

The Voice: Lidia Thorpe and the ‘Progressive No’
Three main Voice campaigns have 
coalesced as we approach the Referendum. 
There’s the ‘Yes’ campaign, supported 
by the Federal Government and all State 
and Territory governments, all major 
Australian faith groups, many sporting 
codes and large corporates.
There’s the ‘Conservative No’, also 
known as the ‘Racist No’, supported by the 
Federal Opposition and funded partially by 
far-right US Christian groups.
Then there’s the ‘Progressive No’, the 
position of the Black Sovereign movement, 
born with the Tent Embassy on the lawns 
of Parliament House in Canberra in 1972. 
This movement maintains that sovereignty, 
the authority for our First Nations peoples 
to self-govern, has never been ceded.
Independent Senator and Djab 
Wurrung, Gunnai and Gunditjmara 
woman Lidia Thorpe represents the Black 
Sovereign movement in Parliament. She 
spoke at the National Press Club last 
month, saying that First Nations peoples 
are still living under colonialism and the 
damage it has done since 1788. 
‘The frontier wars have never ended,’ 
she said on 16 August. ‘Same wars, 
different weapons.’ She cited deaths 
in custody and child removal, both the 
subjects of Royal Commissions with their 
recommendations largely ignored.
‘This country, your system of 
government, has been built on lies,’ she 
said. ‘And the Referendum for a Voice to 
Parliament is a continuation of these lies … 
It’s false hope because it’s tricking people 
into genuinely believing that a powerless 
advisory body is going to protect our 
Country and sacred sites, save our lives, 
keep our babies at home.’
But rather than advising a ‘No’ vote, 
Thorpe is calling for the Referendum to 
be cancelled.
Instead she wants implementation 
of the recommendations of the Royal 
Commission into Aboriginal Deaths in 
Custody, the ‘Bringing Them Home’ 
Report and the United Nations Declaration 
on the Rights of Indigenous People.
She has a point. The fact that the 
government will ‘make laws with respect 
to [the Voice’s] composition, functions, 
powers and procedures’ makes it more 
palatable to some, but gives credence to 
Thorpe’s claim that it will be powerless.
The ‘Yes’ campaign and the 
‘Progressive No’ campaign are ultimately 
after the same outcome: the justice that the 
Statement from the Heart is asking for. But 
one is willing to work with government and 
the colonial structures that we undeniably 
have, while the other believes that the 
Voice will cement the representational 
body into those colonial structures and will 
change nothing.
History is littered with broken 
promises. But an advantage of the Voice 
over previous advisory bodies, says Daniel 
James, contributor to The Saturday Paper, 
is ‘the representational structure … that 
has regional representation feeding up into 
that central Voice that will speak directly 
to power. Because history has shown that 
the further away decision makers are from 
communities, the worse the outcomes.’ 
James, a Yorta Yorta man, will be voting 
‘Yes’. If the polls are to be believed, so 
will the majority of First Nations peoples.
Jen Severn

Plant trees to assist Cystic Fibrosis Research
For forty years, Bridge House Nursery in Brogo has been 
growing trees to green the environment and provide food plants 
to the region.
In recent winters, we have been selling bare-rooted deciduous 
trees and shrubs with the profit going to Cystic Fibrosis research. 
Last winter we donated over $3,000 to the cause.
After the fires of 2019/20, people have reported that 
deciduous trees were instrumental in saving houses and other 
infrastructure due to their dampening effects on fire heat. 
Numerous other benefits include summer shade for stock and 
houses and ornamental values such as spring blossom and rich 
autumn colours. Many deciduous trees are surprisingly drought 
tolerant, merely dropping their leaves and waiting for better times. 
Some have excellent cabinet timber properties.
This is the last winter that we will be offering bare-root 
stock other than to order. Their roots are considerably longer 
than equivalent potted plants, much cheaper and will establish 
much better.
Bridge House Nursery is in a very cool spot in Brogo so we 
will have bare-root stock until the end of September so come and 
support a very worthy cause.
Species include copper beech, maples, birch, magnolias, 
mulberries, tulip trees, flowering dogwoods, Zelkova (Japanese 
elm), Fuyu persimmon and thornless berries.
For full lists of what is available, phone 0410 404 041 or email 
me at donaldofirth@gmail.com.
Funds raised will support Cystic Fibrosis research which is 
greatly extending the lives of people with CF. It remains the most 
common cause of  early death from genetic inheritance. One in 
twenty of us are carriers. 
Bridge House Nursery is open by appointment only.

Marine Rescue Open Day – something to learn for everyone
There will be lots of interesting 
activities on offer at the Marine Rescue 
Open Day on Saturday, 1 October at 
Fishermen's Wharf, Bermagui from 11am 
- 3pm.
We’d love you to come along, buy 
a book of tickets, get involved and learn 
about who we are and what we do.
You can get to know about Marine 
Rescue by going on board our rescue boat 
or by taking a tour of the radio room. Try 
your hand at line throwing or inspect a 
jet-ski or life raft.
For the kids there will be duck rescue, 
tin can alley, face painting and kid to 
captain radio.
There will be live music, a sausage 
sizzle, a raffle and a guessing competition, 
and lots of prizes, as well as dinner and 
drink vouchers to be won.
To give you a little background – 
Marine Rescue NSW is a volunteer-run 
organisation that provides a 24-hour radio 
emergency response service to boaters in 
trouble. Volunteers receive professional 
training and develop the skills they need 
to help people on the water.
Volunteers personally get a lot out of 
the role also. They have the satisfaction of 
knowing they are helping to provide a vital 
service that saves lives. 
‘We are helping people when they 
need it most. When people believe that 
they are in danger it can be a very stressful 
time for them. Being able to help them get 
to safety and get home in one piece is a 
very satisfying experience,’ one volunteer 
offered.
Marine Rescue is a not-for-profit 
organisation that receives financial support 
from the NSW Government and from 
members of the boating community. 
However, they still rely on community 
fundraising efforts like this Open Day to 
meet costs. 
So, come along, get involved in the 
activities and support your local Marine 
Rescue. And if you’re up for a new and 
exciting volunteer role, Marine Rescue 
could be the answer. Come and talk to us! 
Deb Worgan, Volunteer

Only time will tell!
Look at the goldfield for example. The Montreal Goldfield 
near Bermagui that is. Gold was discovered on the beach (now 
Howards Beach) one hundred and forty-three years ago in 
September 1880. It started a rush in the truest sense of the word. 
Look at it now!
Did you know it was the start of the Bermagui Mystery? 
When five men vanished including a Government geologist and his 
assistant, plus three fishermen from Batemans Bay. Does anyone 
know whodunnit? The why, when, where and how it happened. 
It’s a real mystery. There is still a reward for information.
For the last twenty-three years, members of the Bermagui 
community have been working to bring the old goldfield back to 
life. They have fenced off part of the area and around the shafts so 
the tour guides can take you round the site in safety while telling 
the Montreal story.  It is such a beautiful walk back through time 
amongst tree ferns and massive bloodwoods with an understory 
of geebungs and burrawangs. These trees have grown back the 
way they were before the gold rush. Why? Because the mining 
was done with a pick and shovel so the seed bed was not removed.
But back to now. We hope to have a Gold Discovery and 
Solve the Mystery Day during the next school holidays. Keep 
eyes and ears open for more details.
The Montreal Goldfield is a unique place for all sorts of 
reasons and worth a visit at any time but especially at this time 
of the year. Check out the Montreal Goldfield Facebook page and 
their website (type Montreal Goldfield into your search engine) 
or visit 'What’s On' at the Bermagui Information Centre on-line 
whenever you can.
Judy Hearn

We all know what trauma is after the 
fires and COVID. We’re still weathering

trauma experiences before that, with the

our community and understand that we are 
more than our injured selves.
For quite a few of us (more than one 
in ten) there has been a first trauma that 
the other nine in ten people know nothing 
about. It occurs when we are developing 
in utero, if one embryo dies.
The Womb-Twin survivor has great 
confusion about what happened to their 
twin. They have Baby Brain without 
cognitive ability, and no conception of 
death.

(to the outer world) and survivor guilt – 
we look after others, but not ourselves.

from their dead twin that gives extras like 
an organ or digit, mixed handedness and 
extra sexuality input, especially if the 
Womb-Twin was of the opposite sex.

changelings, often ‘away with the fairies’

The subject has been well-researched

resolve the overwhelm, reopen our hearts 
through grieving deeply and get to know 
other survivors.

Electric vehicle Tourist Drive for the Triangle area
Sales of electric cars are now growing 
rapidly, reaching around 8% of new car 
sales nationally in recent months and more 
than 20% in Canberra. But public charging 
stations can be hard to find. The good news 
is that visitors and locals alike will soon be 
able to top up their EVs at the Bermagui 
Fishermen’s Wharf and Cobargo Co-op 
car parks.  
Renewable Cobargo and Clean 
Energy for Eternity, Bermagui have 
worked with the Cobargo Co-op and 
Bermagui Fishermen’s Co-op to secure 
NSW Government co-funding for the two 
charging stations that are expected to be up 
and running in September. The two Co-ops 
have also made significant investments of 
their own funds in these stations and should 
be congratulated for their leadership.
They are ‘destination’ charging 
stations, using AC power, that are designed 
for EV users to top up for an hour or two 
while shopping, visiting a tourist attraction 
or having a meal or coffee break. A typical 
EV will be able to add around 70 km in 
a one-hour charging session, although 
this can range from 50 km to 140 km, 
depending on the EV model. Each site 
can accommodate two cars charging at 
the same time.  
The public EV charging network 
needs to include both destination chargers 
and DC very fast chargers that are much 
more expensive and often require grid 
upgrades. Governments are co-funding fast 
charging in major towns along highways, 
with new sites coming in Bega and 
Narooma.
The Cobargo and Bermagui stations 
will form part of the Chargefox network, 
owned by motoring organisations including 
NRMA. EV owners will access the stations 
and pay for the electricity they use through 
the Chargefox app.
A third station is also on the way in 
Tathra, creating one of the first EV Tourist 
Drives in NSW that will help future proof 
our tourism economy. 
Frank Muller

Cobargo CWA branch calling for urgent improvements  
to diagnosis and treatment for neurodiverse conditions      
The Country Women’s Association 
(CWA) of NSW is using its annual 
Awareness Week campaign to highlight the 
issue of neurodiversity and neurodivergent 
conditions, with a particular emphasis on 
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder 
(ADHD) and the growing number of adult 
women who are being diagnosed with the 
condition.
The 2023 campaign will run from 3 
to 9 September focussing on increasing 
awareness around neurodiversity and 
neurodivergent conditions (including 
ADHD, autism, dyslexia and Tourette 
syndrome) and is calling for urgent changes 
to improve diagnosis and treatment options 
around ADHD. 
Lynn Lawson, President of the 
Cobargo branch of the CWA, said the 
issue of neurodiversity was raised at the 
CWA of NSW’s annual State Conference in 
Bathurst in May. A motion was supported 
asking ‘governments to investigate ways 
to reduce the costs of diagnosing and 
treating adult (ADHD)’, with some branch 
members relating their own experiences of 
recent diagnosis and the challenges they 
have encountered. 
‘With an increase in the number of 
adults, many of them women, who are 
being diagnosed with or seeking diagnosis 
for ADHD, demand is currently exceeding 
the available services. Diagnostic costs are 
rising, medication supplies are periodically 
stretched and out-of-pocket treatment costs 
are putting pressure on household budgets,’ 
she said. ‘Rural and regional residents are 
really feeling this impact, with their options 
often fewer than those in our major cities.’
This year, the CWA is partnering with 
the Australian ADHD Foundation, a not-
for-profit organisation, ‘working to make 
the lives of people with ADHD easier and 
simpler’.
The ADHD Foundation has reported 
a spike in demand for their services from 
35- to 50-year-olds, many of them women, 
putting additional pressure on the health 
system, with people struggling to obtain a 
diagnosis in the first place and occasional 
shortages of certain medications. 
During this year’s campaign, the 
CWA of NSW is calling for:
•	
Increased recruitment, training 
and retention of, and support for, 
health care professionals to ensure 
client access to timely diagnosis and 
management of ADHD, particularly 
in rural, regional, and remote areas;
•	
New ways to reduce the costs of 
diagnosing and treating adult ADHD; 
•	
Extension of access to all long-acting 
medications on the PBS for late 
diagnosis ADHD;
•	
More research into long-term 
outcomes of ADHD in girls and 
women – especially work that 
investigates how and why the disorder 
contributes to difficulties across the 
life span; and
•	
More awareness of ADHD in girls 
and women to ensure early diagnosis, 
leading to better treatment and 
support.
‘This Awareness Week we want to 
highlight these issues and urge governments 
at all levels to look to how we can 
better support those with neurodivergent 
conditions. And we also want to raise 
awareness in our communities about 
what neurodiversity is, the many people 
who live with neurodivergent conditions 
and, through greater awareness, build 
understanding of difference and be more 
accepting of those differences,’ Lynn said. 
As part of CWA Awareness Week, 
the Cobargo branch will be assisting in 
the activities of the Far South Coast Group 
of CWA.
Mary Williams, Publicity Officer

Just when you think Bermagui has 
slipped into quiet winter mode with barely 
a soul around, something seems to pop up.

outlets in the town … and still counting?)
Bermagui continues to buzz (no

attracting locals and visitors.

and their Boats show.
Far South Film Festival
This year’s Film Festival is being 
held over the weekend of 19 August, once 
again at the Picture Show Man, Merimbula.

info till after publication last month, but we 
look forward to receiving a report of this

came out of it.
Whale action
Coming to a headland near you, grab 
your binoculars.
There’s been quite a bit of whale 
action in the ocean as the large aquatic 
mammals pay their yearly visit, even 
popping into Horseshoe Bay. Keep an 
eye out for the visiting whales on their 
pilgrimage south after having spent the 
winter in warmer waters to the north.
Bubbles, the whale watching boat, is 
back at the harbour ready to take tours to 
get a closer look at these giants of the sea. 
But there’s plenty to see from the shore, 
often when you least expect it. Keep your 
eye on the ocean and don’t forget to pack 
your binoculars and camera to get a special 
shot for us to publish in future Triangles.
Motel Art
An unusually balmy winter’s evening 
added to the ambience of the courtyard 
of the Bermagui Motel, this year’s venue 
for Motel Art, organised by SEACC and 
supported by Council. This is the third 
of these annual events across the shire, 
previously held in Eden and Merimbula.
Each of the seventeen rooms of the 
Bermagui Motel was dedicated to an artist 
from the region to showcase their work and 
culminated in a celebration of the talent 
and scope of creativity in the area. Various 
mediums were represented – photography, 
ceramics, painting and textiles as well as a 
room dedicated to a youth art group and Art 
in the Garage, a group exhibition. 
Organisers were thrilled with the 
event and congratulated the owners, Sue 
and John Singleton, on hosting the event at 
their motel. There was a great atmosphere 
around the town, plenty of foot traffic as 
people wandered to and from the venue. 
Outdoor braziers, lights, music, all created 
a great atmosphere. Food was available, 
catered for by Cheeky Mango, and the 
Bermagui Hotel ran a bar, Bega sound 
collective provided a DJ, adding to the 
‘vibe’ of the event. A happy night.
It is estimated that approximately 300 
people attended the preview evening, with 
about as many visiting the exhibition over 
Saturday. I think everyone is hoping there 
will be another such event.
Opening night of Motel Art, Bermagui Motel
I was lucky to catch Elise, local photographer (and 
hairdresser) posting a few portraits on this blank 
garage wall, Saturday morning of the Motel Art 
weekend. I hope she does it again, great to see art 
around town. Thanks Elise.

Wallaga Lake Bridge closure –  
community update 
Members of the Community Forum 
along with representatives from Four 
Winds and Sculpture Bermagui, met 
separately with Transport for NSW on 
Tuesday 25 July. At that meeting Transport 
for NSW said they weren’t wedded to 
either Option 1 or Option 2 and would be 
flexible in their Bridge closure timing to 
try to satisfy the needs of as many local 
residents as possible.
The one piece of promising news 
from that meeting was that Transport 
for NSW would consider leaving the 
Bridge fully open in March 2024 to 
accommodate the Sculpture Bermagui 
and Four Winds events. They would also 
consider assisting with media advice and 
signage on bridge opening times with the 
theme ‘Bermagui is still open for business’.
Notes from the  
Public Meeting held 25 July
Close to 200 local residents attended 
the public meeting hosted by Michael 
Holland, MP. Kristy McBain, MP, the 
Mayor and several councillors also 
attended. Transport for NSW reiterated 
that the Bridge maintenance is essential 
and must involve the full closure of the 
Bridge for at least two months to allow 
replacement of girders and strengthening 
of central piles. The intention is that the 
Bridge remains single lane and all timber.
The message coming loud and clear 
from the public meeting was that locals 
wanted a new two-lane concrete bridge 
to better service the town, avoid future 
bridge closures for maintenance and 
mitigate exit risks during fires. It should 
be a much longer bridge that would allow 
the reduction of the level of silt build 
up in the lake with the removal of the 
causeway. This is not on Transport for 
NSW current agenda, but member for 
Bega Michael Holland will certainly be 
taking this message back to the Regional 
Roads Minister. Notwithstanding, a single-
lane timber bridge has a certain level of 
charm and tourist appeal to many in the 
community.

Bermagui Public School report
Bermagui Public School has been a 
hive of activity this term with sporting 
events, environmental excursions, STEM 
competitions, open days and concert 
rehearsals. Stage 3 students competed 
with other schools in the Narooma High 
School RoboRebels challenge day. They 
built some cool robots and readily showed 
them off. They worked hard preparing for 
the event and had a very fun day. Five 
teams entered the challenges, and three 
teams took out first, second and third 
places in the line-following challenge. The 
winning team received an interesting hand-
built Lego trophy to display at our school. 
Second and third took home a 3D-printed 
medallion. 
We celebrated Public Education 
Day in Week 3. Many families enjoyed 
spending time in classrooms with their 
children. Students were able to show off 
their magnificent learning and artworks. 
The whole school made a variety of paper 
aeroplanes and entered a competition to see 
whose plane could fly the furthest. Flights 
took place one class at a time, conditions 
were a little breezy, but we saw some 
terrific planes. Mr Constable was a great 
flight instructor and control tower, ensuring 
all planes landed safely (no planes made it 
onto the roof).
Bournda Environmental Education 
Centre always offers cool science and 
art programs throughout the year. During 
August, a few lucky Stage 2 students were 
selected to participate in two fantastic 
programs. One was the Wild at Art 
competition, the biggest wildlife art 
competition for children. These talented 
students investigated threatened species 
and the unique plants and animals that 
call Bournda home. The second program 
was an environmental science program. 
Students learned about various mini 
beasts, their life cycles and their habitats. 
They learned how to safely search for and 
catch mini beasts before collecting and 
recording what they found. iPads were 
used to photograph and record the different 
species, and then students headed back to 
the science lab, where they further explored 
their mini beasts using a high-powered 
digital microscope from the Atlas of Life. 
Bournda always provides plenty of fun 
opportunities for meeting students from 
other schools. 
This term, classes have been working 
diligently, creating performances for this 
year’s concert. – writing scripts, making 
props and practising character lines and 
dances to impress our families. The variety 
concert will be held on 12-13 September 
with three awesome performances. Tickets 
are available through the school. We hope 
to see you there.
Our school is fortunate to have some 
student support for well-being and mental 
health through the Marymead Horizons 
program. Marymead provides free support 
in small groups and to individuals and their 
families. Support sessions occur at school. 
More information is available through 
the school office, or you can contact 
Marymead directly HorizonsSouthCoast@
marymead.org.au or phone 6162 5800. 
We are currently forming support groups 
focussing on anxiety, grief and loss, and 
anger management. We thank Marymead 
for helping us be the best we can be.

Quintessentially Quaama   	
 
Cecile Bower
We’ve all heard about forest bathing 
and rolled our eyes. I recall three-day 
bushwalks: knee trembling descents down 
mountain passes; nervous scrambles up 
unstable scree; even getting lost and 
spending the night under a dripping rock 
ledge in pouring rain. The neuroplastic 
benefits were only felt after a shower, a 
hot cup of tea and eating food that wasn’t 
reconstituted.
When I moved to Verona I learnt 
to slow down in the bush: to just sit and 
observe. I never slept so well as I did 
during my time out on Upper Brogo Road. 
Now, nearly resident in the village, I have 
come to enjoy quiet sitting, listening and 
observing in our lovely park in the middle 
of our village. Sometimes it’s just me and 
the native birds, sometimes travellers sit 
across from me under the picnic shelter 
with their homemade sandwiches and 
thermoses. Other times the park is full 
of joy with kids celebrating each other’s 
birthdays or playing after school.
Why not create some quiet time for 
yourself here. Bring your own picnic or 
toss a banger or tofu burger on our free 
barbie. Or feast on Wild Rye pies and 
sausage rolls from the Quaama General 
Store. When you are at the store you can 
also buy books by local authors. Hot off 
the press this month is Rose Roberts’s 
third book on The Adventures of Duck 
and Goose.
If you’re here on a Monday, you 
might see Quaama school kids, hand in 
hand, cutting across the park on their way 
to the Quaama Tennis Club. Hans, from 
Merimbula Tennis, is teaching them tennis 
fundamentals for the next six weeks. 
If you are here on Sunday, 17 
September, from 10.00 am to 2.00 pm, 
you can resurrect your tennis game. Our 
club welcomes all ages and abilities. 
There will be hit and giggle games, a 
barbecue, car-boot sale and songs from the 
Quaama Choir. Tennis rackets and balls 
are supplied. You only need to bring your 
game face and a sense of humour. Contact 
Di Holmes johnanddiholmes@gmail.com.
Across the road from the park, a few – 
but not enough – Quaamese are dedicating 
some of their time to keeping our beautiful 
School of Arts Hall at her best at 121 years 
old. The Hall committee have installed a 
new hot water system. Still to be completed 
are supper room renovations, repairs to the 
front door, guttering and downpipes, and 
the ongoing maintenance and care of the 
gardens and grounds. A few hours of your 
time, once a week, a month or even every 
quarter, to help with this work would be 
greatly appreciated. Contact Glennda on 
glenndah@hotmail.com.
Locals want me to mention that 
there is a call out from the Social Justice 
Advocates of the Sapphire Coast for 
the loan or donation of caravans for 
the homeless in our area. Contact Mick 
Brosnan via their website – just type their 
name into your search engine.
Finally, Paul Sheargold, Captain of 
the Quaama RFS, urges both villagers 
and property owners to get ready for a big 
fire season. We’ve had next to nothing in 
the rain gauge and property owners are 
starting to cart water. It is important to 
make your property fire ready, have a fire 
plan and share this information with your 
neighbours. For more advice head to the 
RFS Out Station, 1211 Upper Brogo Road 
for the Quaama RFS Get Ready Day on 
16 September from 1.30 pm onwards.

Theatre at the 
Murrah Hall
I recently had the pleasure of watching 
Howard Stanley performing his one-man 
play I just want to dance with words at the 
Murrah Hall.
Howard’s inspiration for this little 
gem of ‘salon’ theatre is a collection 
of short stories by the Swiss writer 
Robert Walser. Howard was working in 
a bookshop (many years past) when his 
boss tossed him the slim volume saying 
‘… there’s a play in here for you.’ The idea 
gestated and the work was born. That was 
some years ago, 1982 in Melbourne, but 
Howard clearly felt its heart still beating 
and worthy of another life, this time in 
the bush.
This production, directed by Adrian 
Guthrie is simple. With very few elements 
of set and costume, Adrian and Howard 
create a little world in which naïveté and 
profundity illuminate and intrigue the 
audience. With an old tin bath, a washing 
line, a sheet or two and a crumpled suit of 
clothes Howard does what the theatre asks 
of actors … he plays.
For seventy minutes, Howard’s 
nameless character (perhaps essentially 
and eccentrically Howard, perhaps 
Walser?) deftly and lightly interrogates the 
challenges of life’s conundrums, prosaic 
and existential, personal and universal.
Howard’s performance is engaging, 
empathetic and, at times, mesmeric as he 
breathes life into a daisy-chain of short 
stories. His unique physical imagery 
(Eastern European ’poor theatre’ in 
flavour) invites the imagination of his 
audience into his tiny world. This is the 
magic of theatre.
Howard is a veteran of the theatre, 
particularly of the small venue/intimate 
kind. Both the production and his 
performance, it was/is very much a 
collaboration.
For some years, as President of the 
Murrah Hall’s governing body, Howard has 
worked tirelessly to improve the facilities 
of the hall for audiences and performers 
alike. It is wonderful that he is now finding 
a fresh energy reviving his own performing 
career.
If you hear I just want to dance with 
words is playing again nearby, it will be 
because he’s embarking on a ‘small hall 
tour’. Get along, support our local talent 
and give Howard a good send off.

Panboola:Renewal
an exhibition by Hilary Peterson
This exhibition is a new work 
exploring the reclaimed estuarine and 
freshwater wetland area in Pambula on the 
lands of the Yuin Nation. 
Panboola provides important breeding 
and nursery areas for a large range of 
animals including birds, fish, invertebrates, 
echidna, kangaroos and provides roosting 
habitat for flying foxes.
The exhibition pays tribute to the 
importance of the local community and the 
ecological contribution many volunteers 
have made to ensure the longevity of the 
world through nurturing this habitat.
In 2021, I was awarded the Joyce 
Spencer Textiles Fellowship for regional 
NSW artists to develop a new body of 
work. I began my career as a commercial 
textile designer later transitioning to 
fine art. In the two years since receiving 
the fellowship, I have spent many hours 
walking Panboola, in Pambula, NSW, 
absorbing the atmosphere and changing 
colours through the seasons, inspired by 
them all. I’ve used fabric, paper, local 
plant dye, varied printing techniques such 
as sublimation, lino, stencil and monotype, 
painting, and drawing. Collaged together 
and integrated with stitch and embroidery 
to capture the environment, recording 
Panboola’s reclaimed wetlands, their 
diversity, unpredictability and fragility. 
The works range from large to smaller wall 
pieces, as well as artist’s books.
The exhibition will be opened 
by Alexandra Seddon on Saturday, 2 
September at 11.00 am. 
This project was supported by the 
Joyce Spencer Textiles Fellowship.

2022, I went to the spotted gum forest at Bermagui. I did some 
quick sketches. My aim was to figure out how to represent a forest 
of tall trees. I liked the idea that they shed their bark annually. 
These paintings revisit that time. I wanted to capture something 
of the spirit of the forest rather than the actual details. 
The graphic possibilities of a forest are challenging. The 
process of reworking helps bring out the shapes. My mother, 
Margaret Dredge, was an abstract expressionist. Some of her 
paintings are in the Shoalhaven Regional Gallery in Nowra. My 
mum worked in the studio from her imagination, but I like to 
spot something first.

Wifedom: Mrs Orwell’s  
Invisible Life
Anna Funder
Penguin   RRP $36.99
Two new arrivals have filled our TV 
screens, newspapers and social media 
during the last month. And both use the 
word patriarchy a lot! Both have feminist 
messages. One is the Barbie movie. The 
other is Anna Funder’s Wifedom, which 
has had more publicity, both pre- and 
post-publication, than any other recent 
book. Funder has been interviewed widely 
on 7.30, The Drum, Conversations, the 
SMH and at numerous Writers Festivals. 
Wifedom has been reviewed in most major 
newspapers, both here and overseas.
So, what is all the fuss about?
Anna Funder trained as an 
international human rights lawyer and 
has held fellowships in Berlin and New 
York. She is the author of Stasiland and 
All That I Am.
As we are told in the personal memoir 
part of this book, Funder was working on 
a deadline, writing a film script for All 
That I Am, when her highly privileged 
world started to fall apart. Her architect 
husband, her three children, a stream 
of visiting relatives, house repairs, a 
depressed French exchange student and, 
finally, the supermarket were dragging her 
away from her deadline. She felt crushed 
by ‘the motherload of Wifedom’ and, as 
anyone would do, turned to the collected 
works of George Orwell for solace. She 
then read all six biographies of Orwell, all 
written by men. And the question came. 
Where is the wife in this?
Where is Eileen O’Shaughnessy?
The answer took her six years to 
uncover and this book is the result. It is 
part memoir, part biography, part fictional 
biography, part cultural critique and part 
feminist polemic. And raises the old 
unanswerable question: do we, can we, 
should we separate the art from the person?
Funder does find Eileen. She is 
brilliant, an Oxford graduate, witty, 
whimsical and self deprecating. A treasure 
of newly-discovered letters from Eileen to 
her best friend and the writings of friends 
and contemporaries fill out a picture of this 
woman who clearly played a role in Orwell 
becoming the writer he was.
She twice saved Orwell’s life in 
Spain. Orwell writes, in the passive voice 
‘… the visas were obtained and we could 
leave Spain’. He doesn’t mention that 
Eileen obtained the visas by risking her 
own life. When and if she is mentioned, 
it is only as ‘my wife’. She is not named.
Eileen was his editor, typist and the 
main bread winner. During WW2, she 
worked at the Ministry of Information, 
writing morale lifting propaganda about 
which she would tell Orwell. Was this 
the inspiration for 1984? She studied 
under Tolkien and loved folklore. She 
told whimsical stories about the animals 
she tended on their farm. She and Orwell 
discussed Animal Farm every night. Did 
she part write Animal Farm,the novel that 
made his name and brought money in for 
the first time?
Orwell was continually sexually 
unfaithful, often with her friends, 
leaving her even more isolated. Both 
contemporaries and biographers say that he 
‘pounced’ on women. Is that a euphemism? 
Why did she stay? Was it because she 
believed in his work?
Eileen died during an operation for 
uterine cancer aged 39. Orwell was away 
(unnecessarily?) observing post-war 
Europe although he knew she was having 
risky surgery.
Does the book, showing Orwell’s 
patriarchy and disregard for his wife 
diminish the work? Each reader will 
respond differently.
His work is wonderful and important. 
As Funder said in an interview, ‘We can 
hold the man, the work, the wife and the 
life, together in our minds at the same 
time.’

What's Growin' On
Mark Evans
Spring fever
Whether you subscribe to the meteorological view that it 
begins on the first day of the month or to the astronomical view 
that it begins on the vernal equinox on the 23rd day of the month, 
September brings us into the season that many consider the 
beginning of the gardening year, namely spring.
This is our favourite time of the year to get plants into the 
ground. Planting or re-potting this month means that plants will be 
ready to get growing as soon as the weather really begins to warm 
up. We have loads of cuttings taken at the end of last summer that 
are rooted in pots, so we’ll be taking the opportunity this month 
to plant them all out and fill in a few gaps in our garden.
Another important September job is to fertilise all your 
plants, whether they are in pots or in the ground, inside or out. 
Feed everything you can with an appropriate fertiliser applied to 
the manufacturer’s specifications and, if you can, double down 
with a combined tonic of liquid fertiliser and seaweed extract from 
the watering can to really speed things along. If you love mowing 
the lawn, now is also a great time to feed that too.
Don’t forget that lush spring growth also appeals to a number 
of herbivorous insects, so keep an eye out for any infestations of 
sap-suckers, such as aphids, and leaf-eaters, such as caterpillars, 
both of which, if they remain unchecked, can reduce a plant’s 
vigour and weaken it. If caught early, you will have more chance 
of overcoming any such pesky pesty problems.
Around the town gardens, spring is yet to make a full splash 
at the time of writing, however some of the Prunus spp. (cherry 
blossom, plums etc) are beginning to put on a show, as are some 
of the deciduous magnolia trees and the Jasminum polyanthum 
(common jasmine). The spectacular flowering vine Pyrostegia 
venusta (orange trumpet vine) has also been a stand-out these 
last few weeks.
With 1 September being National Wattle Day, it is nice to 
see several Acacia species obliging and beginning to light up 
the greenery with a golden flush in the wild places on the sides 
of the road around the Triangle. Of particular interest, is the 
highly-restricted population of the locally endemic Acacia pedina 
(Bermagui golden wattle), which was only first described in 1999, 
that is currently putting on a show out in the local State Forest 
and Nature Reserves around the town.
This month in the vegie garden, the season reopens on sowing 
for some of our favourite root crops such as beetroot, carrots and 
swedes, as well as celery. If you are feeling particularly optimistic 
you could try starting some capsicum/chilli, eggplant or tomato 
seeds if you can keep them nice and warm under a cold frame 
or on a heated pad and bring them inside during the evening. 
Otherwise, continue with the onions, peas, potatoes and silverbeet.
Finally, if you have any comments, gardening questions, 
plant or pest identification queries, please send them through to 
gardening@thetriangle.org.au and we will endeavour to help.
Happy growing!

Open Sauce!  Recipes from the Triangle area 	
Georgina Adamson
Saucy Bits, Citrus Season
Recipes from Marmalade, a cookbook and more.
Some years ago, I wrote a little 
cookbook called Marmalade. No, it’s not 
just about marmalade, although that is 
included, rather a collection of much-loved 
recipes from my time spent at this Italian-
style villa south of Bermagui with my 
‘patron’. Many happy hours were spent in 
her flower-filled courtyard cutting up fruit 
from her orchard and making marmalade.
A couple of recipes follow, basic but 
favourites.
Marmalade
My approach to making marmalade is 
fairly basic, probably because I often had 
buckets of fruit to deal with. Arm yourself 
with a board, a large bowl for cut-up fruit 
and a smaller one for seeds and pith, and 
a good sharp knife. A towel for under the 
board is handy as this job will get messy.
Prepare fruit: cut into small pieces 
with not too much pith, it’s really the skin 
you need. If I am juicing fruit regularly, 
I save the skins, put them in the freezer 
and take them out when I’m making 
marmalade. Save your pips in a smaller 
bowl.
Cover the fruit in the bowl with just 
enough water to cover, don’t drown, along 
with the pips tied in net or muslin. You’ll 
get lots of pectin from these that helps set 
the marmalade.
Next day, measure the contents into a 
saucepan and add the equivalent weight of 
sugar. Slowly bring to the boil, stirring to 
dissolve sugar then turn down to a gentle 
simmer. Enjoy the citrus aroma in the 
kitchen while the marmalade cooks for at 
least an hour, testing now and then with a 
dollop on a plate to see if it is setting.
Prepare jars ... this is important. 
Place clean jars into the oven on low for 
ten minutes. Gently fill clean jars with hot 
marmalade and seal with lids that have 
been boiled. 
Use the next two recipes to make your 
own limoncello version of tira mi su (‘pick 
me up’). Apologies to the purists.
Limoncello
Peel rind off 6 large thick-skinned 
lemons. Not too much flesh. Buy a bottle 
of vodka. Put half of the vodka in another 
similar clean bottle. Stuff the lemon rind 
into the two bottles. The vodka should 
cover the rind. Seal and set aside for a 
week. No nipping!
Make a syrup with 3 cups sugar and 
4 cups water. Bring to the boil and stir till 
sugar is dissolved, simmer for five minutes 
till it’s a little syrupy. Combine this syrup 
with the lemon-infused vodka and stir well.
Strain, then decant into smaller clean 
bottles and seal. Set aside for about 14 
days.
Drink as a liqueur or chilled, poured 
over ice and topped with soda or tonic, or 
give it away.
Melt 250 g butter in a large bowl over 
a saucepan of simmering water. Add 4 cups 
caster sugar (or coconut sugar) and stir till 
dissolved. Add grated peel and juice of 6 
lemons, then 6 eggs well beaten. Stir all 
together over the heat till mixture thickens 
and coats the back of a spoon.
Put into sterilised jars and keep in the 
fridge – great to use for desserts or cakes 
or a spread.
Prepare a special limone mi su 
using your lemon butter combined with 
mascarpone as the filling for the sponge 
fingers that are brushed with limoncello. 
Layer all together and leave to infuse 
all those flavours for a couple of hours at 
least.
Enjoy these lemony recipes while 
lemons are at their best.
