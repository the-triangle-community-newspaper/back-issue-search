Things are starting to take shape for a fabulous 
festival this year, featuring outstanding local talent 
on two stages all day, delicious food, craft stalls and 
fun-filled activities for the kids and adults. 
Now held in winter to keep that Tilba spirit going 
all year, the Festival is a celebration of heritage, local 
produce, and the creative and musical talent of the 
community. The Tilba Festival has been one of the 
best days out on the NSW South Coast for over thirty 
years and a highlight of the year for the Tilba District.
The Bate Street businesses come together 
with stallholders, food producers and entertainers 
to deliver a day that all the family will enjoy and 
remember long into the future with plenty of activities 
to keep all the family members entertained, including 
the boot toss, the very popular egg toss and the 
cheese roll. 
We’ll start the day with a smoking ceremony, 
street cleansing and Welcome Dances by the Gudu 
Dancers, our very own local Indigenous dance troupe. 
The Tilba Festival is organised by a small group 
of enthusiastic local volunteers. The Festival is a 
not-for-profit community event and proceeds benefit 
the Tilba District directly through donations to local 
community groups.

All residents of the Triangle region 
live in the Federal seat of Eden-Monaro 
and all of us live in the State seat of Bega. 
However, the southern section is in the 
Bega Valley Local Government Area 
(LGA) and, in the north, the Eurobodalla 
LGA is split between the Federal seats of 
Eden-Monaro and Gilmore. 
The 2024 proposed electoral boundary 
changes will not change our inclusion in 
Eden-Monaro, but I thought some readers 
might be interested in the draft proposal 
released last week. The previous electoral 
boundary change saw Tuross Heads, 
Moruya and Batemans Bay move to the 
Federal seat of Gilmore and Eden-Monaro 
went west to include Tumut, Tumbarumba, 
Batlow (the Snowy Valleys LGA) and Yass 
and Murrumbateman (Yass Valley LGA). 
The current proposal suggests returning 
Tuross Heads to Eden-Monaro, retaining 
the Snowy Mountains towns of Cooma, 
Thredbo, Adaminaby and Jindabyne and 
gaining Goulburn and Marulan. 
Anyone really curious about these 
proposed changes, can visit the AEC 
website, type ‘Proposed redistribution of 
NSW electoral divisions’ into the search 
bar and then click on Eden-Monaro under 
the maps heading.

The weather may not be warming 
up yet, but Cobargo is getting a new feel. 
Stand in the main street and you can see 
building on six sites, more if you venture 
into the side roads.
I love to see buildings going up and 
watch the tradies who are doing the hard 
graft to make this happen.
Tradies are always a boost to a town. 
Stand in a queue at the coffee and food 
sellers and see how many of the customers 
are tradies!
However, the impact of tradies is 
not limited to appetites but also provides 
us with the welcome evidence that we 
are getting more gender diversity in the 
building workforce. Not many years ago 
a building site would have been a strictly 
male domain but not anymore. In Cobargo 
you will see a number of women working 
side by side with their male colleagues.
As a society we have (perhaps 
belatedly) started to recognise the 
need to view our total population as 
potential workers. Some workplaces 
are still traditionally female dominated, 
and underpaid, in comparison to ones 
dominated by men. However, this is also 
changing and the long held beliefs that 
some jobs are, or should be, gender specific 
are changing. 
So is it all good news? 
In part-time employment there is little 
gender disparity in terms of gender based 
pay, but as of Nov 2023 across all full-
time industry and occupations, the weekly 
female to male pay deficit is still $238.
However, we are getting better. In 
2022 we were the 43rd in the world rankings 
in terms of the greatest gender pay gap, 
we are now 24th.
Well done to those small and 
large customers and government 
instrumentalities that are actively interested 
in, and promoting, a gender diverse 
workforce and a shout out to the builders 
who are making this happen.
As a postscript, please note that I am 
not implying that any builders working in 
Cobargo are discriminating against their 
female employers in terms of wages or in 
any other way.
Change is in the air

Justices of the Peace (JPs) are 
volunteers appointed by the Governor 
of New South Wales. Their activities are 
supervised by the Attorney General of NSW 
under the Justices of the Peace Act 2002. 
JPs are usually highly respected 
individuals who come from all sections 
of the community and are located across 
NSW. A JP has to pass a knowledge test 
and take an Oath of Office before a NSW 
court. They must always comply with legal 
requirements and the JP Code of Conduct. 
A JP’s contact details are public – their full 
name, a contact number, language/s spoken, 
and the suburb or town and postcode where 
they usually provide JP services.
What can a JP do? 
JPs have three primary functions: 
• to witness a statutory declaration 
• to witness an affidavit 
• to certify a copy of an original 	
	
document. 
Generally, a JP in NSW must be 
physically present to exercise these 
functions. If authorised by the law of 
another State of Australia, a NSW JP can 
witness or certify documents for use in 
that other State. 
A JP must act honestly and fairly, 
remain independent and impartial, deal 
with requests for JP services in a timely 
manner and must not unreasonably refuse 
to provide JP services. 
What must a JP NOT do? 
• charge a fee or accept a gift for 	
	
providing JP services
• act if subject to a conflict of 	
	
	
interest.  
• reveal information that is private, 
	
confidential or commercially 		
	
sensitive 
• assist or write in a statutory 		
	
declaration or affidavit
• provide legal advice
• draw up or store copies of legal 	
	
documents
• witness or certify documents for 	
	
use outside Australia. 
How do I find a JP (NSW Justice 	
	
of the Peace) in my area?
1. Search the 'JP Online' register.  
2. Go to a local community desk.
Ask at local places if there is a JP 
at a location near you (e.g. local council 
or shire offices, a police station or a 
courthouse, the office of your local MP, 
shopping centre, library, chemist or bank.)

In the face of more frequent and 
intense events driven by climate change, 
such as the 2019 Australian Black Summer 
bushfires, we can no longer afford to ignore 
the impact of climate change on our health. 
As bushfires and floods become more 
common, health conditions such as asthma 
and heat-related illnesses will increase. 
Climate change events like storms can also 
lead to road closures or blackouts, making 
it harder to access healthcare when needed.
At Macquarie University, we are 
supporting health systems to cope with 
the new challenges brought about by 
climate change and are seeking to prepare 
communities to stay safe and healthy 
when and where climate crises strike. Our 
team of researchers come from different 
backgrounds and areas of expertise. 
We want to find ways to reduce climate 
change’s negative effects on health and 
ensure everyone, regardless of their 
circumstances, has access to appropriate 
healthcare and can be prepared.
To help us understand the needs 
of people who are managing a health 
condition during an extreme weather event, 
we are seeking people to tell their personal 
stories. 
Volunteers will take part in an 
interview to share their recent experiences 
managing a health condition during a 
disaster such as a bushfire, flood or storm. 
We aim to understand the health challenges 
they faced and the solutions they found. 
We will use this information to design 
new ways the health system can support 
the long-term needs of communities in the 
face of environmental shifts. 
Sharing your experience can pave 
the way to a more resilient, prepared 
community and help the health system 
plan for future climate-related disasters.

As she handed over my triple-shot coffee on a sunny winter 
morning, Amanda explained that locals are still discovering the 
new Honorbread outlet in Narooma. ‘When they find us, people 
are just excited to have a good bakery in town again,’ she said. Her 
next customer agreed.   
Bless you, Bermi, we all love an excuse to visit, but my olive 
and rosemary loaves taste better with reduced carbon miles. 
Standing in line, I bumped into local Narooma vet Kate, who told 
me that she too is enjoying buying bread closer to home.  
Located in mid-town next to an art shop and up from the 
Post Office, people wandering into the new Honorbread shop are 
enjoying a breakfast bun or muesli, a hot lunch with salad and, 
surely, to worship another black tahini and honey scroll.  
‘I bake, and use Honor’s sourdough starter, but I come here 
for the coffee. Great coffee is hard to find,’ Toby told me while 
tucking into some toast. Tasha nodded while munching muesli 
next to me at the inside bar table, telling me she comes in for the 
bread weekly.  
Honor explains that she and Tim had been looking for a 
location on the sunny side of the street in mid-town Narooma 
for the last five or six years. While taking a squiz at the new art 
shop, a real estate agent pointed out that the Chinese restaurant 
next door was closing down. Given the location and prior food 
approvals for the site, everything seemed to finally align.  
‘What really drives me is the fact that forty local people, 
up to fifty in the peak season, now rely on our bakery for their 
livelihoods. I want to ensure they can have enough shifts to live 
well, so we are growing. We have been open for nearly a decade in 
Bermi and it’s exciting to have the scope to keep doing new things 
and selling them in a different location. I also really enjoy making 
and sharing great bread.’
When I asked her how the business differs in Narooma, 
Honor has noticed that Narooma is more of a working town, so 
more salads and lunches fly out the door compared to Bermi. 
‘Another great thing about Narooma is that it’s close enough 
to Bermi so food quality is not lost in transportation, which 
particularly affects pastries.’ 
Keep cranking them out, Honor and team!

each year, and I have been running events since it first began 
twelve years ago. Talking about end-of-life issues has become

Parliament House

handouts that will make the process easier.

Living a good six hours’ drive from Sydney, you often feel 
like you miss out on what is on offer in the cities. However, in May 
Sydney just got a whole lot closer thanks to a live-stream event 
offered at a number of south coast libraries. This is the second 
year that events from the Sydney Writers Festival have been live-
streamed to Narooma, Moruya and Batemans Bay libraries, as 
well as to Bega Valley Shire library. What a wonderful and easy 
way to bring the best of city life directly to people living outside 
metropolitan areas!
Over four days at the end of May, the link-up offered a 
program of interviews and discussions with such well-known 
authors and thinkers as Leigh Sales, Lisa Millar, Trent Dalton, Viet 
Thanh Nguyen, Julia Baird and more. It was a great opportunity 
to hear firsthand the background to their works and get to know 
the authors on a more personal level.
Thumbs up to the Eurobodalla and Bega Valley Library 
networks – hopefully this initiative can continue to provide people 
living in regional areas with access to literary and cultural events 
without the need to travel to a capital city.

Working bees buzzing
There’s a blue whisp of aromatic 
smoke from a cottage chimney, the grind 
and clunk of a battery-powered pruner and 
a charming garden path strewn with vibrant 
autumn leaves and snappy twigs. Walk a bit 
further and you can see tree limbs swaying 
and hear the odd crack of a branch. Turn 
the corner and there they are – the Feed 
Diggies group, gloved, booted, down in the 
dirt and reaching up into bushes, working 
together to help one household get through 
some garden jobs fast. Yes, after a bit of 
a break, Feed Diggies recently met at 
Jen’s place for a working bee. Just four 
of us this time but, boy, we managed a 
lot in four hours. A comprehensive prune 
of some fruit trees and a massive hack 
back of buddleia – thanks, Angela, for the 
tips on where best to prune on the peach 
branches. Then a good old tidy and weed 
of the entrance area and chook enclosure 
as well as stacking said prunings for a later 
pile burn. Then there was the inevitable 
(and fun) sharing of cuttings from some 
prized and coveted plants. We also found 
a mystery fruit on a gorgeous tree that 
Jen had planted years ago (research and 
cross-referencing with the rest of the group 
identified it as a type of quince). 
When the jobs were done, we stood 
back and admired our handiwork. What 
a transformation! These working bees 
achieve so much – but it’s about more 
than just getting through the work of food-
gardening. It might be a cliché, but the 
whole really is worth a mountain more than 
the sum of its parts. We might feel daunted 
by the tasks to be tackled when we arrive 
at a working bee but, within moments, 
people find their flow, help, laugh, joke, 
work pleasurably in silence in the sun, 
slurp from their water bottles, pat dogs, 
ask ‘what’s that plant?’, stand up, stretch 
and stare at the impressive, beautiful bush. 
The lunch break is a chance to flop down 
and rest, enjoy some home-cooked and 
wholesome delicious food, chat, share 
stories, admire each other’s place (and 
Jen’s almost-completed, unique hand-built 
place has the ultimate wow factor). We give 
and receive wisdom and musings on plants, 
recipes, soil, compost, weeds, chooks, 
just about everything. At the end of the 
working bee, we are left feeling tired and 
elated, better connected and cherished as 
neighbours and friends. The sense of fun, 
the goodwill, laughter and the learning are 
amazing. Can’t wait till the next one.

Bermagui Community Forum 
The Bermagui River is one of the most 
popular estuaries within the Bega Valley 
for recreation and is also commercially 
important for oyster aquaculture, 
recreational fishing and marina operations 
at the harbour near the ocean entrance. All 
commercial fishing in the river has been 
prohibited since 2002.
The river is regarded as one of the 
most pristine estuaries in the Bega Valley 
Shire. The significant ecological values of 
the estuary include its clear waters, tidal 
flows, extensive saltmarsh, mangrove and 
seagrass. The estuary represents a key 
attraction for residents and tourists.

In 1963 there was a big house for 
sale on the outskirts of Cooma, for a 
very reasonable price. Too reasonable. 
A migrant couple with three growing 
boys, the father working as a panel 
beater, went to have a look. It needed 
some work but the couple, between 
them, had the skills so they bought it. 
But soon cars started arriving. Single 
men, or groups of them – the house, it 
turned out, had been a brothel.
At night, when the father was home, 
he could send them off with his shotgun 
and some harsh words. Sometimes a shot 
through the radiator was needed. But 
during the day the menfolk of Cooma 
encountered Carmel Elizabeth Conway, 
shotgun hugged to her hip, her boys under 
strict instructions to stay inside. She wasn’t 
averse to letting off a shot when required 
either. 
Present-day Triangle locals may have 
trouble reconciling this image with the 
slender, girlish woman they came to know 
and love, with her long plaits, ankle-length 
skirts and gentle Irish lilt. Carmel and 
Joe brought their sons Joe, Brendan and 
Eamon to Quaama in 1979, buying an old 
slab cottage on Bermaguee Street. Carmel 
would ride around town on her bicycle, her 
beloved cockatoo Timmi perched on the 
handlebars. But it was during her Quaama 
years that tragedy struck. First Joe Senior 
contracted cancer. Then Eamon, who had 
moved to Sydney with his brothers, died 
in a motorbike accident. That spelled the 
end for Joe Senior, who passed away three 
weeks later. Another eighteen months and 
Brendan was also dead – another bike 
accident.
In unfathomable depths of grief, 
Carmel grew a garden. Her neighbour Stan 
worked at a nursery and would bring her 
‘pots of dead sticks’, and she would bring 
them back to life. Over the years her half 
acre became a rambling, verdant sprawl 
of beds, paths, hedges and secret rooms, 
as Carmel came to terms with her losses.
In 1997 Carmel sold the Quaama 
property (this is when I, the new owner, 
was lucky to meet her) and moved to 
Bermagui. She took up golf. She met Jim 
Jubb, whose property backed onto the golf 
course, and the two fell in love – ‘a grand 
love’, she told me just a few years later 
at his funeral. It seemed that tragedy and 
death were stalking her, yet her indomitable 
love for life carried her through and 
touched all those whose paths she crossed.
Carmel, aged 89, died in her sleep 
on the night of Wednesday 29 May, after 
spending the day with her surviving son, 
Joe, visiting her special spots – Dickinson 
Point, the Blue Pool and her favourite, 
Cuttagee Headland. ‘I’ll be popping off 
now’, she’d told Joe and her dear friend 
and neighbour Madeline that day.
Rest in peace, Carmel, you’ll be 
sorely missed.

Garden Tetris is a lot like the video game, but instead of 
trying to arrange falling coloured blocks, it’s plants you are trying 
to fit together efficiently within a limited space. Like the game it 
can take some practice. That supposedly short plant you stuck 
in on the edge turns out much taller than you expected, the one 
you put in the shade needs more sun (or vice versa), that climber 
would be nicer over there instead, that plant needs dividing and 
those two plants would look much better side by side. Game on.
July is one of the best times to play Garden Tetris. In mid-
winter, many plants will have paused their growing, exhausted 
their flowering and fruit/seed production and will be gathering 
their energy for the upcoming spring. Digging them up now will 
result in minimal disturbance, and they will be settled in their new 
position, ready to grow again when the warmer weather arrives.
Before you move a plant, it is beneficial to prune it first as 
moving a plant disturbs the root system reducing the plant’s ability 
to absorb nutrients and water. The corresponding reduction of the 
above ground parts makes it easier for the smaller root ball to do 
its job and satisfy the whole plant. The other thing to do before 
you move a plant is to have the new planting hole prepared and 
ready to go, thereby limiting the time the plant is out of the ground.
Before you dig, imagine the plant in a pot. 
You should aim to dig out a pot-sized root ball at least. The more 
roots you can bring with the plant the more successful the move is 
likely to be. Chop/dig right the way around the plant first, and then 
try and lever it out from below. Larger roots should be cut cleanly 
with sharp secateurs. Support the root ball whilst transferring the 
plant to its new hole and ensure that it is sitting at the same depth 
as in the old location.
Before you backfill, flood the planting hole and root ball 
with water and let it soak away. Backfill, then give another drink, 
this time with some added soil conditioner like seaweed extract 
or fish emulsion. Hold off on fertilising until you start to see the 
first signs of new growth. Game over.
Now study the garden for another year, note your next moves, 
and prepare to play again the following July.
Meanwhile, around the town gardens, the camellias continue 
to shine and the normally non- descript, hardy succulent shrubs, 
Crassula ovata (jade plant) are bursting as they enter peak 
flowering season. The banksias and grevilleas are also having a 
flowering flush.
In the wild places, on the sides of the road around the Triangle, 
the bushland is rather homogenously green at present, with very 
few obvious flowers currently.
Finally, this month in the veggie garden we continue catering 
to the cooler weather and so are mostly still limited to planting 
leeks, shallots or onions, peas of any description as well as broad 
beans and lettuce.
Finally, if you have any comments, gardening questions or 
plant or pest identification problems, please send them through 
to gardening@thetriangle.org.au and we will get back to you 
personally.
Happy growing.

Pumpkin Time
I’m a great fan of using seasonal vegetables … 
produce that is at its peak at a particular 
time of year. At the moment, there are 
lots of pumpkins available and they 
are perfect for hearty winter dishes, 
inexpensive and can be used in so many ways. 
There is always delicious pumpkin soup, creamy 
and smooth, but here are a couple of interesting 
alternatives, one sweet, one savoury
Pumpkin and Walnut Tart
Pumpkin, chickpea and coconut curry
Ingredients:
	
1 kg (approx.) pumpkin peeled and cubed 
	
1 brown onion, diced
	
3 cloves garlic, sliced or chopped fine
	
1 fresh chilli, your choice, finely chopped
	
1 tablespoon of finely sliced ginger
	
1 teaspoon each: mustard seeds, turmeric
	
1 bunch coriander (replace with chopped parsley and 	
	
	
	
celery if you like)
	
20 curry leaves … or a tablespoon good curry mix with 	
	
	
	
those ingredients
	
1 tin chopped tomatoes 
	
2 tins coconut milk 
	
2 tins chickpeas.
Method:
	
Put ¼ cup of a good nut oil in saucepan over high heat. 
	
Add onion, garlic, chillies, ginger and cook.
	
Reduce to medium and cook till softened. Add spices or 	 	
	
curry mix. Add tomatoes and coconut milk.
	
Bring to boil, add pumpkin and chickpeas. Reduce to low, 		
	
cover and simmer for 45 mins. Add more water if too dry.
	
Take lid off and cook for a further 10 mins till sauce thickens.
	
Serve with rice, naan bread, chutneys and dips.
	
If you still have a mountain of pumpkin, cook some off, mash and 
freeze. It makes a great ‘filler’ for so many dishes and is terrific in 
your dog’s food.
