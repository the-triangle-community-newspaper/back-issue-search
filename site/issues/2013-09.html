Letters to the editors
How many of our readers have looked at 
the Eco Edge page in the Triangle and wondered 
what the environmental company AKT could be 
making or selling. Well that mystery can now 
be solved, this month the Merimbula News 
ran a story on AKT and the Kix Dehydrator, 
a waste treatment that extracts the protein 
from fish waste, spoiled supermarket food and 
other waste streams and creates feed for use in 
aquaculture or animal feed. AKT is reported to 
be investing in Pambula Engineering and that 
company will be making the Kix Dehydrators. 
AKT is supporting the district in many ways.
Technology can be a very positive 
contributor to our evolution as a society and in 
another field (not so local this time) the Waka 
Waka social enterprise have created what is 
in effect a mini power station. Designed as 
the most efficient LED solar powered light 
affordable to people living on $2 a day, the 
development and startup production was crowd 
funded. Further development (also crowd 
funded) saw the Waka Waka light morph into the 
Waka Waka mini power station that can charge 
your phone or other small devices as well as 
provide 16 hours of bright light after a single 
day of sunlight. It will charge a smart phone in a 
couple of hours. Or you can use your computer 
to charge the light.
So far 12 000 lights have been distributed 
in Haiti and 5 000 of the mini power stations 
have gone to Syria. But sales are not limited to 
third world countries, when they are sold into 
a country like Australia the sale leverages a 
donation to a third world country. I got mine 
as part of the start up crowd funding and I love 
it. You can find out more on the net at waka-
waka.com.

bloody mindedness proceeded with cutting 
down community assets that provided shade 
and amenity in the Gardens. Worse still they did 
this with undue haste on a Saturday in order to 
try and avoid further public outcry.

first to come down hard on community members 
who try to make additions or renovations on 
their homes. If a community member tried to 
avoid Council perusal by finishing a job on

recognition in our Constitution. It also shows 
why we need legislation to allow community 
members to seek urgent injunctions to prevent 
intransigent and bloody minded councils from 
proceeding with decisions that are clearly not 
in the public interest.

A Little Bird Told Me
A common sensitivity in most that live 
in or visit our Triangle is the spirit to escape 
hustling crowds and mass consumerism, to 
enjoy the inherent charm of freedom in simpler 
country life. It is a veritable journey through 
the past, where culture and craftsmanship 
survive. As individuals from a myriad of places 
and times, our good old days are reflected by 
nostalgic memorabilia, dear to our hearts. 
Rekindling tangible expressions from memory 
lane, is Christine McKay’s ethos at her quaint 
new shop, ‘A Little Bird Told Me’. Brimming 
over with displays of vintage and retro 
collectibles, upcycled to resurge the security 
and comfort of a bygone era. A restored vintage 
caravan is a quirky backdrop among bunting 
happily flapping in the breeze to celebrate 
childhood fun on beachside holidays. Chris’s 
passion is to source and exhibit rare, period 
pieces to conjure joyful memories and personal 
connections which revive that old time quality 
artisan generation. It is apparent that Bermagui 
is a destination for collectors, a vintage village 
off the beaten track, to find the unexpected. 
‘A Little Bird Told Me’, Chris’s Bower Bird’s 
nest of the eclectic and unusual, is at the end 
of Pine Drive in the Bermagui Industrial Area 
and open Thursday, Friday, Saturday and the 
last Sunday each month, from 10am till 3pm, 
or by appointment. 
The Crossing Land Education Trust – 
Model Chook Tractor Design challenge
Attention Year 6 students and teachers! 
Year 6 students are invited to design and make 
model chook tractors for a display at The South 
Coast Field Days in Bega on Saturday and Sunday 
21 and 22 September. Members of the public are 
also invited to display working chook tractors. 
Contact Annette on 6493 3400 for more details. 
What is a chook tractor? (imagine…) It is a 
mobile cage and house of some sort that protects 
a few chooks and gives them a comfortable 
place to live in and lay eggs while they do 
some garden digging, eating and fertilising. 
The structure can be easily moved to a new 
place in your garden every day or two. What 
it looks like and what extra features it may 
have are up to you and your imagination. 
Criteria - the model should fit inside a 
wheelbarrow - the design features of the chook 
tractor should be clearly labelled and explained 
- use of recycled materials is encouraged. 
 A maximum number of design features are also 
encouraged e.g. self feeding, self watering, easy 
egg collection, sun and wind control, predator 
proofing, ease of shifting etc. 
Design certificates and gifts will be 
awarded. How students can display their model 
chook tractors at The South Coast Field Days: 
1. Ask your teacher or parent to email The 
Crossing to register your name:  thecrossing@
thebegavalley.org.au 
2. Complete your model, with labels on 
design features and attach your full name and 
school. 
3.Deliver your model to Bega 
Showgrounds on Friday afternoon 20 September 
between 3.30pm and 4.30pm or between 8.00 
and 8.30am on Saturday morning 21 September 
- look for ‘The Crossing Chook Tractors’ sign. 
All chook tractor models and working 
chook tractors can be picked up at the end of 
the field days between 4 and 5pm on Sunday 
22 September. Anything left at the showgrounds 
will be put in the pavilion for Monday pickup 
at the latest. 
The Crossing is a not for profit permaculture 
designed camp on the Bermagui River that helps 
young people put sustainability into action. 
They offer Landcare and sustainability journeys 
from year 6 to year 12 and tertiary groups. 
They also offer adult workshops, including an 
annual Permaculture Design Course with John 
Champagne.

Share a path – in Bermi
A group of cyclists joined Bega Valley 
Shire workmen at the almost-completed 
shared pathway for cyclists and pedestrians at 
Bermagui on Friday August 9 to thank them for 
the excellent job that they had done. 
The pathway, between the harbour car-
park and the Bermagui River Bridge, had long 
been sought by local cyclists. 
Recent support by the Bermagui Pre-
School, the Bermagui Public School P & C 
and the Bermagui Chamber of Commerce was 
acknowledged by a spokesman for the cyclists 
who also thanked Council’s Penny Green for 
her co-ordination of the project.  
It was jointly funded by the Council and 
the Roads and Maritime Services Department 
and represents stage one of a planned upgrade 
of facilities for cyclists and pedestrians in and 
around the town.
Stage two, scheduled for completion next 
February, is designed to link the bridge to the 
school and to the existing pathway which runs 
from the northern commercial centre of the 
town to Keating drive.
Jack and Michele Miller

Twenty one tail salute 
Just relaxing on my front verandah earlier 
today, a large splash out to sea caught my eye. 
On a flat and beautiful afternoon, I often see 
diverse boats coming and going, so initially 
I thought: oh just another white fishing boat 
heading back, however… nope, not the case. 
A breaching whale it was and I grabbed 
the binoculars and watched a wonderful 
display. A tail rose and splashed, rose and 
splashed and a partial breach and then a spurt, 
followed by a wave of his or her flipper. What 
a wonderful sight! It would have been at 
least 20 times that the tail waved to me and 
it felt like a message, if not leisurely Morse 
code. It went in a line out to sea, then back a 
bit and then, unfortunately, I had to respond 
to a matter close at hand so turned away. It 
was just great and we do have a link with our 
intelligent giant friends, I am sure, especially 
after the salute I received today. 
P.S.  On the news later I heard of a similar 
display off Sydney, so it seems the wider 
communication lines were open. How can we 
ensure their safe future, as well as ours, for 
that matter?

‘In celebration of 125 years of our parish: the early 1900s’
 White ants played havoc with church 
buildings in the Parish. Their gnawing, and the 
realisation that the building was too small, led 
to the decision in February 1895 that the church 
in Tilba needed to be replaced. From then, it 
was only a little over a year until the building 
was completed. 
People in Quaama also wanted a building 
in which to worship together and started raising 
funds for this in 1905. The building was 
complete by 1907. In Cobargo, the damage the 
white ants had caused also led to the decision 
that the Church needed to be replaced. But 
Cobargo was slower than Quaama (or Tilba) and 
from the decision being made in 1907, it took 
some 15 years until it actually happened. The 
work started, it stopped, it started, it stopped, …
In the meantime, in Bermagui, the 
community erected All Saints which was 
dedicated in 1911.
Finally in 1922, Mrs Harris took over 
as Secretary of the Cobargo Church Building 
Fund. The foundation stone was laid, the 
building progressed and within two years it was 
completed - was it a coincidence that there was 
a woman at the helm? Am I biased? Perhaps 
not, at the ceremony for laying the foundation 
stone, Archdeacon Bryant “referred to the fine 
work performed by the women of the parish (in 
getting to this stage), and said it was the turn of 
the men to help solidly.”           
And for all of this building, the community 
raised funds. Dances, concerts, bazaars, sales, 
and as always, the behaviour of some at 
these concerts was not seen as appropriate 
by the standards of the time. At a concert to 
raise funds for the building in Tilba, the local 
paper reported that: “The larrikin element 
was unfortunately strongly in evidence, and 
about half a dozen hoodlums at times made 
themselves very objectionable. From what I 
have heard since, however, I think I may safely 
say there is hardly likely to be a repetition 
of such unseemly conduct. Those who made 
themselves so objectionable are well known, 
and strong precautionary measures are to be 
taken to prevent anything of the kind occurring 
again”. One wonders what they did!

At Earle Horne’s funeral on Thursday 1 
August we heard from his son-in-law Richard 
that Earle joined the army when World War II 
broke out, at just 16 years of age. In February 
1941 he was sent to Singapore with the 2/18 
Division, captured by the Japanese and held in 
Changi POW camp for two and a half years. 
He turned 21 in Changi. We heard that he used 
to break into the guards’ supplies to liberate 
Red Cross stores – food and other provisions. 
Sometimes cigarettes. Not that he talked about 
those years much. He contracted Beriberi from 
malnutrition, and his eyes were never the same.
Earle made it home in 1946, met Joan at 
the autumn dance that year, and they married in 
April 1947. They soon bought Merrydale on the 
eastern outskirts of Quaama village, clearing the 
land and building a successful dairy farm, later 
turning to beef. There they brought up five kids: 
Jennifer, Margaret, Elizabeth, Brett and Debby.
A regular at Bega Races, Earle knew his 
horses. One day he took Margaret and Elizabeth 
and backed nine winners straight. The phone 
would start ringing early on Melbourne Cup 
Day for Earle’s hot tip, and he was usually right.
Always a hard worker, he was still a 
common sight on his tractor or Gator at 88 
years of age.
Earle just hadn’t been the same since 
losing Joan in August 2011, and Debby, 
suddenly, not much later. He spent the last eight 
months at Hillgrove House in Bega, where he 
died on Thursday 25 July, aged 90.
Raymond Earle Horne: husband, father, 
farmer, punter. But as the Last Post sounded 
at the graveside on 1 August, the image that 
arose was of a young, strapping Earle, stealthily 
exiting the supply tent in the steamy, Singapore 
night and hurrying back to his quarters, his coat 
bulging with cigarettes for his POW mates.

WIRES would like to give a special ‘thank 
you’ to our local police for their generous 
help in dealing with severely injured adult 
kangaroos and wallabies. During the last few 
months we have seen increased numbers of 
these animals lying on the side of our roads due 
to car accidents.  
 If an adult kangaroo or wallaby has 
received an injury such as a broken leg or back, 
the animal must be euthanised. They cannot 
handle being in care for the length of time it 
would take to heal and would die of stress. It is 
a much kinder option to euthanise the animal 
as soon as possible rather than have it die a 
lingering death.  WIRES often calls on our 
police to help in these situations.  The police, 
though very busy, always come out as soon as 
possible and put the animal out of its misery. 
WIRES is very grateful for this support.    
If a joey is found in the pouch, it can be 
raised and released back into the wild when it 
has reached the proper age.  
Many of our native animals are out and 
about after dusk foraging for food.  So please 
take extra care when driving from ‘dusk to 
dawn’ for your safety and theirs!

This is a first novel by 26 year old 
Australian writer, Hannah Kent, who spent time 
as a teenager in Iceland where she first heard 
the story of Agnes Magnusdottir. Agnes was 
condemned to death for her role in the murder 
of two men in Northern Iceland in 1829. The 
novel tells of the last months of her life, when 
she was sent from prison to live with a family 
to await her execution by beheading, carried 
out by the brother of one of the murdered men. 
Attended by a young clergyman, who was sent 
by the authorities to urge her repentance, Agnes 
slowly reveals the story of her life and her role 
in the events culminating in the brutal murders. 
The harshness of her childhood, spent in dire 
poverty, shapes her life and the choices she 
made, explaining how she became involved 
with one of the murdered men. Rather than 
being held in jail, she is placed with a family that 
initially rejects any human contact with her, but 
as the winter deepens and she works alongside 
them to ensure their survival, the relationships 
change, particularly between Agnes and the 
wife of the couple. This is a very moving book, 
based on a true story, and has won great acclaim 
for this first-time author. It has already been 
published in over 20 countries, with release 
in Iceland due soon. The descriptions of the 
weather demand either a fire or an electric 
blanket – make sure you have either before 
you start reading!

Ingredients
750 – 1 kg smallish potatoes boiled in their skins till cooked and cold
Large cup of barely cooked peas refreshed in cold water- 
I had frozen but fresh peas which are coming in season would be nicest
7 large eggs
300 ml thickened cream or light sour cream 
About 3-4 tbsp of finely chopped herbs – eg  parsley, dill, chives, chervil 
¼ cup finely grated parmesan, handful of shredded parmesan for the top
Salt and pepper
Method
Preheat oven 180 degrees C. 
Line a 28cm removable base flan tin with baking paper. 
Peel and slice potatoes into 5mm pieces. 
Place pieces of potato into tin strewing the peas about in between. 
Whisk eggs, cream, salt, pepper, grated parmesan and herbs together. 
Pour over other ingredients until covered. Scatter shredded parmesan for scattering on top
Cook for about 40 mins. Can be served hot or cold.
