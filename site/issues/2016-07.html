From linen and lace to satin and seed 
pearls, Cobargo's community hall was 
decked-out with wedding fashion finery 
for the exhibition Courtin' Cobargo during 
the ‘etc’ weekend. This beautiful display 
of forty wedding gowns associated with 
Cobargo provided a nostalgic and romantic 
look into another aspect of our shared local 
history.  
Dresses donated for display included 
those worn by members of so many 
people including, for example, those of 
the Allen, Hoyer, Ayliffe, Doolin and 
Salway families. Two superb displays by 
the Masterson family and Motbey families 
respectively, featured several generations 
of wedding and celebration memorabilia. 
However, this was just one of several 
displays and activities organised for 
Cobargo's annual 'etc' weekend.
Now in its third year, the level of 
activity was high with a grand display 
of nine open gardens, seven garage 
sales, gallery events, literary discussions, 
children's programs, and more – each 
combining to become Cobargo's 'etc' event.
Congratulations and heart-felt thanks 
should go to those fabulous 
Cobargo people who spent 
countless hours working on 
individual activities. The 
time taken to tend and prepare 
gardens amounts to weeks, if 
not months. Preening and 
cleaning garages for sale 
items; organising captivating 
children's activities; 
presenting fine dining 
experiences; preparing 
displays and demonstrations; 
creating terrific torsos, and 
more. These were all 'labours 
of love', with the behind-
the-scenes activity, effort, 
funding and emotional zeal 
that are often overlooked.
The CTBA is also 
planning its programme of 
activities for the forthcoming 
t w e l v e m o n t h s . T h e 
committee will be seeking 
input from the community 
as to what activities they 
would like to see on Cobargo’s calendar 
of events; when these would be of greatest 
community benefit; and how these can be 
achieved.  
Without doubt our Folk Festival, 
Agricultural Show and Christmas Markets 
will be on the list, however there may be 
other events worthy of attention and effort. 
Indeed, it’s anticipated that feedback from 
the ‘etc’ weekend, in particular, will be of 
key importance. A survey for community-
wide distribution will be developed and 
issued as soon as possible. Results and 
subsequent analysis will be published.
The AGM of the Cobargo Tourist 
and Business Association Inc. will be on 
16 August, at the Cobargo Hotel. All are 
welcome to attend.
Andrew Haydon
President, 
Cobargo Tourist and Business 
Association Inc.

It is good to see that readers’ letters

One event that many of us missed was 
Courtin’ in Cobargo, held in the School of 
Arts Hall, documented Cobargo’s history 
using dress and photography. Most of us 
will also have missed the Gallery Bodalla 
photographic competition Animals in 
the Wild. This was the second annual 
competitive photographic exhibition 
and was conceived with the intent of 
challenging the Huntfest Arms Fair held 
in Narooma and offering an alternative 
vision. These are all finished and done with 
but what of the future?
An unusual project taking shape in 
the not too distant future will be a Pop 
Up Death Café. Not the most riveting 
title perhaps, it is part of ‘Dying-to-
Know-Day’, an annual day of action that 
references loss, death and dying. It would 
seem to be an exercise that offers a space 
to talk about death, how to speak about loss 
and how to support the bereaved, subjects 
that have become difficult for many of us. 
Death was once recognised as part 
of life and in some way it seems strange 
that it is now in effect hidden socially but 
ever more on display as weapons become 
ever more lethal and terrorist acts pervade 
the media.
The Pop Up café will be hosted by the 
Bega Valley Hospice Group. Details will 
be published in The Triangle.

In the last few months you may have 
seen a couple of new signs on the side of 
the highway just north of Quaama. They 
point the way to Stephen Laszuk’s Hot 
Metal and Emily Laszuk’s Pottery.
Their property, The Ponderings, used 
to be known locally as the old yabbie farm.
Steve and Emily moved there from 
their farm in Bathurst in November 2014. 
Steve is a boilermaker and small engine 
mechanic. So if you have a metal problem 
Steve can fix it for you.
Working out of his large shed or 
mobile workshop, Steve is finding lots of 
interesting jobs coming his way. Recently 
this has included welding tractor linkages 
and trailers, building stock crates and 
feeder bins along with repairs and servicing 
on ride-ons, quad bikes, chainsaws, hedge 
trimmers and boat motors. There is a 
different culture in this area. People seem 
to prefer to repair rather than replace. This 
is lovely in this day and age. 
Emily Laszuk has been a potter for 
34 years. 
All this time she has been producing 
her hand-thrown blue and white stoneware 
painted with native and farm animal 
designs. They have found their way 
into homes over much of Australia and 
overseas. 
She also makes lusterware, beautiful 
jewel-like pots with iridescence from 
silver, copper and bismuth in the glazes, 
and many layers of glaze over multiple 
firings and kiln atmospheres. 
Emily has a 
studio and gallery 
on their farm and 
also sells her work 
at Black Wattle gallery in Cobargo. Having 
access to this gallery has allowed her to 
play, expanding her work beyond her 
earlier wholesale range. This has given her 
great delight.
Both Steve and Emily revel in their 
move here. As they have settled into their 
new home, they find they are doing their 
best work yet. 
Do feel free to call in, following the 
signs to see where they lead to.

Geoff and his wife Maida lived in 
a weatherboard cottage on the corner 
of Bermaguee and Cobargo streets in 
Quaama. One winter’s day a few years 
ago he, Maida and I sat on plastic chairs 
in the sun in front of their porch while 
their old white cat twirled around our 
ankles. It was tied on a long piece of twine, 
explained Maida, “so it doesn’t wander 
onto the road.” I wondered if it was deaf; 
I knew Geoffrey was, or nearly. He was 
wearing one of those fur-lined hats with 
earflaps, but even when he lifted one flap 
he grimaced with effort when I asked a 
question.
Over the decades, locals became 
familiar with the sight of Geoff and Maida 
sitting there in the sun. But last year Geoff 
became frail and we saw less and less of 
him. Maida cared for him at home as long 
as she could, then in March this year he was 
taken to Bega Hospital, then eventually 
Hillgrove House in Bega, where he died 
on 20 April. 
Geoffrey John Jamieson was born in 
1931 in Bendoc, just over the Victorian 
border. He was snigging logs with draught 
horses for the Bendoc Sawmill when he 
met Maida Jessop, from Upper Brogo. 
The couple married in 1962, made their 
home in Quaama, and have lived here 
ever since. Geoff started an earthmoving 
business where his two sons, Don and 
Norm, would eventually work too. He 
loved fishing and hunting.
One day late last summer Geoff 
called out to me as I passed. Norm and 
Maida had planted a prodigious vegie 
garden and Geoff was proud. Together 
we picked huge handfuls of purple beans 
for me to take home. That was my last 
memory of him. 
I’ll miss seeing him sitting below 
the porch there in his earflaps, feeding 
prawns he’d netted to his cat, or driving 
his quad down the road, the cat on the 
tray. 
There’ve been a succession of white 
cats over the years, all tied with twine 
to the verandah post, or riding around 
Quaama on his quad.
Geoffrey is survived by Maida, Don, 
Norm and daughter Charmaine. Maida 
wishes to thank everyone for their cards 
and flowers, and the Cobargo Hotel and 
Red Cross for putting on the wake.
Jen Severn
 Geoff Jamieson, hunter, fisherman, died in April

Food for everyone
Food swaps are springing up all over 
Australia. These informal gatherings bring 
together people who are interested in food 
growing, sustainability, and community; 
who enjoy the pleasures of eating freshly 
picked seasonal produce and reducing the 
food-miles to our plates; and who want to 
build food security and resilience for their 
community. 
More than just sharing home produce, 
food swaps provide opportunities for 
meeting and making like-hearted friends, 
and sharing knowledge and skills. 
Do you have a glut of fruit, vegetables, 
eggs, seed from last season’s crops such as 
strawberry runners, or jars of jam etc. that 
you would like to swap for produce/plants/
cuttings that you don’t have? Food swaps 
are about sharing excess and promoting 
resilient communities so offerings aren’t 
just limited to home-grown food but think 
of fermentation cultures, compost worms, 
worm juice, manure, herbs, cuttings, bulbs, 
tubers... the list is endless!  
Food swaps are not about money or 
barter, but about sharing. Some months you 
might leave with more than you brought 
– but another month you might leave with 
less. Food swaps are for everyone: you 
may not have any excess produce at the 
moment, just a desire to join in. So please 
come along and contribute your interest 
and enthusiasm. 
The first food swap event is planned 
for Sunday 7 August, 10am to 12 noon 
at 44 Maher Lane, Central Tilba. Please 
bring a plate of morning tea to share - tea 
and coffee will be provided. Children are 
welcome as long as they are supervised at

café happening in Bermagui (and Bega)

it provides an opportunity for people to 
talk about death and dying in a respectful 
environment. This subject is so often swept 
under the carpet in our society, and yet, it 
is something inevitably central to our lives. 
In the past, death was a community event 
which has now, to a large extent, become 
professionalised. Has this been beneficial 
to the community and is it what we really 
want?
Death Cafés have popped up around 
Australia since 2013, as part of Dying-
to-Know-Day, an annual day of action 
dedicated to bringing to life conversations 
and community actions around death, 
dying and bereavement. There have been 
more than 200 to date.

Group are hosting this occasion. Look for 
more details, including place and times in

Akolele and also trunk main customers in

before use. Water can then be allowed to 
cool and stored in a clean container with a 
lid and refrigerated.

should take boiled or bottled water to 
school.

with NSW Health, the aim being a lifting of 
the Boil Water Notice as soon as possible.

towards lower more acceptable turbidity 
levels with a likelihood of the Notice being 
lifted towards the end of the week.

over winter is cauliflower. Such a sweet

This recipe for cauliflower couscous can 
be eaten as a side dish with anything in 
place of regular wheat-based couscous or 
potatoes. You can extend the basic dish by 
adding chick peas or broad beans,  chopped 
tomatoes, rocket and/or baby spinach and 
you have a gluten-free vegan dish you can 
eat at room temperature or hot.
Roast Cauliflower Couscous 
serves 4 - 6
1 medium cauliflower
drizzle of olive oil
1 tsp ground cumin
1 tsp ground coriander
ground chilli to taste
⅓ cup chopped fresh coriander, 
leaves and stalks 
½ cup toasted flaked almonds if you
want some crunch
salt and pepper to taste
Preheat oven 200C. Remove outer 
leaves and cut cauliflower into small 
florets. The easiest way to get the couscous 
effect is to pulse the florets and chopped 
bits of stalk in a food processor for a couple 
of seconds at a time till the cauli looks 
crumb-like, being careful not to overdo it as 
you will end up with a paste. Alternatively 
you could grate the cauli with the largest 
grate side and finely dice the stalks.
Line two trays with baking paper. In 
a large bowl toss the processed cauli with 
a drizzle of olive oil to moisten. Also toss 
in the ground spices and salt and pepper. If 
you had some dukkah or zataar, Morrocan 
spice mixes, you could add some in as 
well, but it’s not necessary as you can be 
as plain or fancy as you want depending 
on your pantry.
Spread the mixture onto the trays in 
a thin even layer, roasting the cauliflower 
for 12 minutes, moving it around the tray 
halfway through the cook. Roasting dries 
and intensifies the flavour. The couscous 
should be light and fluffy. Back in the bowl 
again you mix in the coriander and any 
other additions you may want. Sprinkle 
with the toasted almonds.

During the cooler months of the 
year leading into spring our gardens can 
become somewhat drab and uninteresting. 
Consideration to adding some colour to 
the garden not only makes the garden look 
great, but lifts our spirits on the cold damp 
months of winter. Colour can be added by 
the use of flowering plants 
or by plants that retain their 
leaves and have a colour 
other than green.
Start with annuals 
that can be either planted 
in pots or in the ground, 
these give instant colour and 
will last until the warmer 
weather arrives when we 
plant summer annuals 
that can stand the warmer 
temperatures. Pansies, viola, 
primula, snapdragons and 
poppies are but a few that 
come to mind. Planted in 
a full sun position with a 
compost enriched soil and 
fed with a liquid fertiliser they are a simple 
and inexpensive way to brighten up your 
day.
For a more permanent display try 
using perennials. One important factor 
to remember with perennials is that 
even though they do well in the full sun 
during winter, some might need to be 
planted under deciduous trees to give them 
protection from summer sun.
Many perennials add colour by either 
flower or foliage and the foliage forms 
can vary from deep purple to yellow and 
various shades of variegation. One of my 
favourites are the amazing Hellebore. 
These beautiful plants range in colour from 
white to pink and purple with varieties 
being single or double. Other plants to 
be considered are Polemoniums for their 
variegated foliage, Penstemon Huskers 
Red for its scarlet foliage and Heuchera for 
their interesting range of foliage colours.
If annuals and perennials don’t take 
your fancy then consider shrubs that have 
either a variegated or coloured foliage. 
Many variegated foliaged plants add 
another interesting dimension to your 
garden as their standout beauty in winter 
is highlighted by the fact that many other 
plants nearby have lost their leaves.
Abelia, a small shrub suited to either 
hedging or as a single specimen plant is a 
great addition to any garden. Two lovely 
forms are available at the moment, one 
with a green and silver variegation and the 
other green and gold. These two will take 
part shade or full sun. Other variegated 
plants include Euonymous, Aucuba and 
Metrosideros (NZ Christmas bush for the 
coastal garden).  
The last but not least, are single 
coloured foliage plants that have standout 
beauty in their foliage but also the added 
bonus of flowers. Loropetalum with its 
burgundy foliage and pink 
flowers is a beauty. There is 
also a white flowering form 
with green foliage and both can 
be hedged or used as a single 
garden specimen. Teucrium 
with its silver foliage and purple 
flowers is a good one for a 
coastal garden.
One shouldn’t forget 
the amazing assortment of 
Australian native plants 
that winter flower including 
Philotheca (Eriostemon) 
varieties of Grevillea, 
Callistemon and Leptospermum 
just to name a few. 
Moving along to tasks in 
the garden as a follow up to last months’ 
advice, July is a great time to finish off 
the winter pruning of fruit trees, roses and 
perennials. Perennials can be mulched with 
some straw to protect any new shoots that 
might try and get an early start before the 
real cold weather is over. Complete winter 
spraying of fruit trees and roses with a 
winter oil and fungicide.
Finally, for this month remember 
that plants still need water even in winter. 
It doesn’t have to happen as much as in 
the drier warmer months but it still must 
happen, particularly pots.
As always, happy gardening!
