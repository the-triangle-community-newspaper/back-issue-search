Four years ago the Eurobodalla
Shire Council approached the owners,
Nic and Erica Dibden, to purchase the
original Tilba Tilba Show Ground to
turn it into the Tilba Sports Ground, a
much-needed facility. The land was
purchased for $30,000 - not bad for 10
acres of prime agricultural land at
Tilba Tilba.
Four years later we have a locked
gate, a netball court with no fencing
and an incredibly expensive toilet
block with a portaloo still beside it!
Both the community and the Council
have raised funds and budgeted for this
sports ground. What is going on?
The Triangle emailed the
Eurobodalla Shire Council asking three
questions. The answers from the
Eurobodalla Mayor Neil Mumme are
under each question.
1. Why are the gates of the new/
old Tilba Tilba sportsground still
locked?
“The sportsground is available
for use and has been since it was com-
pleted last year. The Management
Committee decided to keep the gates
locked to prevent vehicles from driv-
ing onto the grounds and to curb van-
dalism. The Management Committee
is aware that when sporting clubs or
groups want to book the field for regu-
lar use or for larger events, they can do
so by calling Council’s Facilities Book-
ing Officer on 4472 3153. Making the
booking helps us to keep track of usage
‘Kenny’ gazumps historic toilet
and plan our maintenance and
budgets. Of course, if you want to
take your kids for a kickaround,
you can and should!”
2. When is the sports
ground opening?
“The sports ground is open,
and as explained, can be used at
any time, however, the amenities
building has taken longer than an-
ticipated, but we believe it will be
completed within the fortnight.
There is discussion with the Man-
agement Committee about an offi-
cial opening.”
3. Is it true that a building
rumoured to have cost over
$60,000 consists of one male and
one female toilet, a storage room
fit only for a mop and a bucket and
with large front and back veran-
dahs - presumably to shelter peo-
ple waiting to go to the toilet?
“This Council felt it had a
responsibility to design and build
an amenities block that reflected and
complemented the town’s history. The
hardwood cladding, for instance, is
similar to that used in other buildings
in Central Tilba and Tilba Tilba. We
also tapped into the environmental
consciousness of the locals by install-
ing solar power, a water re-use system
and a biolytic sewerage system.
Clearly these features are beyond those
normally included in a public toilet, but
we felt it was important for the build-
ing to blend in with the town’s archi-
tecture. We were also proud to engage
Tilba architect, Trevor King, to design
and project manage the building.
“The male and female toilets are
both wheelchair accessible and meas-
ure around 2.5m x 2.5m. The storage
room is around the same size and can
hold cleaning and sports equipment.
There is a smaller service room that
contains the internal mechanics of the
toilets, the solar power equipment and
the pump for the biolytic system.
“You will be pleased to know
that the soccer goalposts have been or-
dered and are due any time.
Linemarking and nets are the respon-
sibility of the user groups.
“The Tilba community success-
fully 
lobbied 
Council 
for 
a
sportsground and amenities. Now
that Council has made this significant
investment, I hope the community will
embrace this new recreational oppor-
tunity and make full use of the facili-
ties.”
Mal Dibden says, “It must be
one of the most beautiful sports
grounds in NSW. I can remember a
family from Bemboka travelling two
days in a sulky (first night at Brogo)
along the bush roads to this beautiful
location to take part in the Tilba Agri-
cultural Show.”

Editorial
Hey, how about that rain? What
a finale to the Cobargo Show! It did a
bit of damage with erosion with one
local farmer losing an entire dam, but
on the whole the smiles on the faces tell
the story.
After all the rain the dreaded
fireweed will start to break out again,
be diligent and pull it out when you see
it, remembering of course to wear
gloves when you do. The rain also
seems to have done wonders for our
insect life, has anyone else noticed the
HUGE stick insects, praying mantis and
grasshoppers? The flood of freshwater
down our rivers has also affected some
of our saltwater fish with a couple of
stingrays recently found belly up in
Bermagui Harbour.
As we go to print the Cobargo
Folk Festival winds up - we welcome
all our visitors and hope you had a
great weekend. Also coming up is the
Bermagui Seaside Fair, a great day out
we encourage you all to go along, watch
the parade, compete in the Music Idols
or enter your pet in the Pet Pageant.
Whispers have reach us that the
dreaded MSG has been ‘found/tasted’
in some of our local eateries. Surely not,
or if so, let it be known.
The Triangle Committee wel-
comed three new faces at their last meet-
ing, great to have you all on board and
thanks for volunteering your time and
expertise.
As we move into autumn we
wish you all a great month, more rain
and good fishing!

Euphorbia paralias or sea spurge is
a nasty weed that arrived in Western
Australia in the 1930s and has ridden
the currents and waves around to Tas-
mania and the NSW South Coast. Each
plant produces about 5,000 salt-toler-
ant seeds and it doesn’t take long for
sea spurge to cover a beach and choke
out all the native plants. The changes
to the beach can be so dramatic that
seabirds can be prevented from nest-
ing and breeding.

a cheque to buy small items of equip-

when it comes to weed management

holiday time, members of the group
bring along their visitors to help boost
the numbers.

The last issue of the Triangle er-
roneously referred to the 1954 Brogo
bushfires. Mayda Jamieson of Quaama,
who was aged (almost) 11 years at the
time remembers very acutely that hor-
rific day on 25 January 1952. From her
perspective at her auntie’s place out
beyond Waterloo at the far reaches of
Upper Brogo Rd, out past where the
two bridges got burned, she says the
fire came over the ‘Knob’, the moun-
tain behind the dam. It was out past
Riverview and appeared as what
would now be described as a ‘fire
storm’, terrifying balls of flame rolling
across the ground consuming every-
thing in its path.

Brogo Fires: 25 January
1952
Mayda remembers in a hushed
voice the girls, Jenny and Marie Otton,
and the old man Rudolph Ogilvie who
were taken by the fire as they tried to
escape on horseback across the pad-
docks and up the hills. She warns,
‘Never go into the head of a gully, the
fire creates its own wind and burns up
the hill.’ Mayda recalls that Jenny and
Marie, aged about 14 and 15 or 16 years
were excellent show jumpers.
Back then, Mayda explains, the
country south of the Brogo River was
known as ‘Brogo’ and that lying to the
north of the river bank was recognised
as ‘Upper Brogo’. This was the coun-
try that the fire ravaged 55 years ago
in 1952. Mayda also recalled that the
wooden toilet block at the Verona
School - now Shipton’s - was burnt
down. ‘After that they closed the school
down,’ she says.
Mayda expresses much concern
for people living up in the hills without
adequate fire breaks. ‘They don’t know
what it [the fire] will do until it hap-
pens. Don’t think that dried-off pad-
docks offer protection; it will burn eve-
rything – stubbles of grass, dried ma-
nure. Everything!’ Thanks, Mayda, for
your insights.

longer daylight enables us to ride in
the cooler part of the day and this year
was perfect - light cloud overhead and
not humid in the bush.
A large number of riders gath-
ered so we were pretty busy taking
registrations beforehand. Thanks to
Julie for her secretarial skills, which
had everyone in the book and ticked
off. With 21 members and 26 day rid-
ers we were quite a large group.

everyone, explained a few rules and
courtesies which are necessary as this
is a walking ride, not a gallop, and we
were away.
The start of the ride was
through open paddocks, along the road
then off through the bush following a
fire trail Richard, Noel, Pauline and
Marea had checked the week before. A
few horses were a bit excited at first,
the usual dancing and jogging but once
in the bush they settled.

good while we all noticed quite a dif-
ference in the levels of water in the creek
crossings. This would be quite differ-
ent now though!

sually large number of red bellies
curled up enjoying their afternoon
naps, more than we might see all sum-
mer on rides. Our fearless leader and
Roger and Shane also had to deal with
a tiger right on the track under Star-

the least! Ask Roger how he felt at the
time.

was reached by most but some went a
bit further to check out a deckchair

hideaway for reading?

walks and in no time we were back in
Richard’s paddocks.

One of the
great lessons I
learnt from my
Dad was to al-
ways have the
courage of my
convictions so, at
the risk of knowing that not everyone
will agree with what I say here, say it I
must, because I’m fed up with the un-
ion bashing that rears its ugly head
whenever there’s an election looming.
I started work at the age of 13,
standing on concrete floors for ten
hours a day under the most appalling
conditions. Without the union move-
ment nothing would have changed,
because people who are willing to ex-
ploit other people will always be in our
midst.
This country was built on hard-
working men and women who,
through their unions, demanded a more
humane and civilised society while we
watched Australia evolve into a more
egalitarian place with integrity and
principles on both sides of politics.
Sadly in the last 15 years we have seen
these hard-won conditions, alongside
some of our most precious democratic
institutions, dismantled in the name of
the economy, while governments have
blamed the most deprived in our soci-
ety for their own lot in life.
Have you ever heard Mr
Howard, Mr Costello or Mr Andrews
attacking the power of The Miners Fed-
eration, The Bankers Association or
The United Retailers Board? Yet these
are institutions set up to protect the
interests of their members and under-
neath their superior facades they serve
the same purpose as a union and con-
tain some of the most powerful men in
this country, with governments well
and truly in their pockets.
The real truth lies in the fact that
ordinary working men and women
only have their labour to sell and when
that so-called ‘Australian’ Company,
Blundstone, sent 300 jobs off shore,
there was nothing the unions could do.
Did someone say something about
power? You’re looking in the wrong
places I’m afraid.
               Pat Thompson
