Mrs Eileen Mary Morgan died on
21st  January, 2011 aged 88. Her funeral
was on 28th January at the Anglican
Church in Cobargo and the ceremony
continued at the Cobargo Cemetery.
Eileen Morgan wrote the book The
Calling of the Spirits, published in 1994. With
due respect to Eileen’s family, I would like
to share some of my impressions of her
book, which I found of great value not only
as a recognised source of reference at the
University and the National Archives of
Australia but because it is Eileen’s own
biography, her story in her own words that
allows the reader to understand a little of
what it was like to live in the Triangle as a
First Australian. Eileen was a proud and
respected elder of the Yuin tribe of Wallaga
Lake, who moved with her husband to
Cobargo in 1950. Aptly and not without
humour, Eileen describes the experience
of being one of the first families to move to
Cobargo from Wallaga Lake mission as
part of the assimilation policy. There are
great stories of growing up around Wallaga
Lake, her community, bush tucker and the
stories of her elders (“the Old-timers”).
There are the memories of the mission
managers such as Mr Sampey who posed
as the schoolteacher for a while. In Culture
Days are photographs of Eileen looking for
wild cherries with her great-grand children
Bhavita Vandermeer.
Vale Aunty Eileen
and another where she shows
them the grass used for
making baskets.
I feel privileged Eileen
left this book for all to read, a
book 
she 
started 
in
December 1977 after her
husband passed away. In the
foreword, How I came to write
this book, Eileen mentions that
it was inspired by one of her
great-grandchildren, Aaron
Norris, who always liked
listening to her stories and
kept asking her questions. She
explains that she wanted to
leave the culture and the
legends of their ancestors for
the younger generations.
I think everyone in the
Triangle is privileged to be able to read
these stories. Anyone living in Cobargo
who would like a better understanding
of their history should read the chapter
Guineapigs in Cobargo and everyone who
enjoys seeing Mount Gulaga would
benefit in their experience of calling this
area home by reading Easter on Gulaga
Mountain (pages 163-164 in the chapter
called This Beautiful Light).
Thank you, Eileen, for also
leaving your stories for me to read -

International Women’s Day, held
each year on the 8th March, is an occasion
that is marked by women in 69 countries
around the world.  It is a day when women
are recognized for their achievements,
can look back on past struggles and, more
importantly, look ahead to the untapped
potential and opportunities that await
future generations of women.
The 
origin 
of 
International
Women’s Day is one steeped in the
history of women’s age old struggles to
participate in society on an equal footing
with men. During the turn of the century,
thousands of women and girls, some as
young at 10, were employed in factories,
working long hours in sweat shop
conditions for less than half
the wages paid to men. In
1908, women and girls
working in the garment
industry in New York held a
massive protest rally to
demand equal pay for equal
work, the end to child labour,
and the right to vote.  These
protesters were met with a
police force determined to
stop them. The rally became
violent as mounted police
charged, 
leaving 
many
women dead and injured.
As a recognition of
this, and subsequent events
involving women workers, in
1910 at the 2nd International
Conference of Working
Women, it was proposed
that each year, in every
country, these events would
be 
commemorated 
as
International Women’s Day.
Australia has a long history of
involvement with women’s issues. In the
early 1960’s, the emergence of feminism
swept the world, and, like it or not, those
International Women’s Day - Celebrating 100 years
early “bra burning
feminists” changed
the course of history
for Australian women
as 
they 
raised
awareness of the inequalities in our
society, and presented a log of claims to
government, including paid maternity
leave, which has only taken some 40
years to become a reality!
We have come a long way since
the days when, for example, in the 1930’s
married women were barred from the
workforce.  Some may say we have
achieved it all – we are liberated, we are
equal.  But, the reality is that many
women are not enjoying the freedoms of
others.  Women are still by far the majority
of victims of domestic violence.  Statistics
tell us that one in every four women in
Australia will experience domestic
violence at some time in their lives, with
an average of 74 women being murdered
by intimate partners annually.  Many
young girls are the silent victims of sexual
abuse by male members of their families.
And, by far, women are the main targets
for rape, with men defending themselves
by saying “they asked for it”.
But, by and large,
Australian women are free to
follow whatever path in life
they choose, but not so of our
sisters 
in 
some 
other
countries where baby girls
are unwelcome and often
killed at birth,  where young
girls are sold into prostitution
by impoverished families,
where girls as young as 7 are
genitally mutilated in the
name of ‘chastity”, where
women are second class
citizens, forced into arranged
marriages and generally
denied any semblance of
equality with men.
This year marks the
100th 
anniversary 
of
International Women’s Day –
so women of the Triangle –
enjoy your special day, be
proud of the achievements Australian
women have won, but spare some
thoughts for the plight of hundreds of
thousands of women across the world
who still live miserable lives just because
they are women.
Bermagui and District Lions Club
say farewell to one of nature’s gentlemen,
lion Kenneth Barry Latitmer, who passed
away on the 12th January.
Barry was born in Cobargo in 1934,
but moved to Sydney to finish his
education. It was in Sydney that he met
and married Shirley, who still lives at
Fairhaven.
Barry worked as a factory manager
at the Punchbowl Metal Spinning Works,
and  joined the Beverly Hills/ Roseland
Bermagui Lions farewell one of their own
Lions Club in 1986.  He transferred to
Bermagui and District Lions Club in
September 1994 when he and Shirley
returned to the district.
During his service to Lions Barry
held the positions of President,
Secretary, and Treasurer for six years.
He was awarded one of Lions
International Awards -  a Melvin Jones
Fellow from the club for his dedication
and services over the years.
A quietly spoken man,  his
services to the community,  were
legendary.  When Barry volunteered to
do a project he could always be relied to
see ot through.  Such was his commitment
to what ever he took on.
The members of the Lions club will
remember him as a great raconteur for
his jokes and humour. One could say he
was the backbone on the Lions Club.
Farewell Barry we will miss you.

Pat’s Piece
What a delightful surprise it was
to receive a request for a “Xmas Pat’s
Piece” and how sophisticated our little
newspaper is looking? Congratulations to
all concerned. Life in Canberra continues
to be full and interesting, but of course
there will always be that little ache in my
heart for what I’ve left behind, particularly
dear friends. Thought I might take a
wander down memory lane and relate a
couple of stories about tripping around
the countryside in my little old Corolla with
my beautiful friend Honor Coutts (who is
no longer with us). My reason will be
apparent at the end of the article.
I’ll begin with the story of us being
on our way to a festival in the Grampians
in the heart of Victoria. We were bowling
along in the middle of nowhere when we
blew a tyre. As I pulled over to the edge
of the road a battered old station wagon
drove in alongside of us. A rather heavily
built, middle-aged woman emerged
dressed in old jeans and a sleeveless
singlet. Her complexion was the same
colour as her well-worn RM Williams
boots and she was covered in tattoos. We
were, to say the least, a little
apprehensive. But she turned out to be
the kindest soul in the world who not only
changed our tyre but insisted on sharing
her thermos flask of tea and home made
Patricia Thompson
(Editor’s note: sorry Pat – this got “lost”

honey and ginger cake before sending
us on our way.
The 
“piece 
de
resistance”however happened in
Cooma on another trip where we had
stopped for coffee on our way to the
Canberra Festival. Half way across the
road I realised I had locked the keys in
the car. Honor suggested a coffee first
while we thought about how to sort it
out. I might say that we had noticed a
number of motorbikes outside the cafe.
On entering we were confronted by a
room full of leather jackets, long

To dear, sweet Po, our valiant and

Rain, rain and more rain! The
ground is saturated, and while this is a
good thing in some respects, the
accompanying humidity is hard on those
things in the garden that are subject to
moulds and mildews. Brown rot is
prevalent on fruit trees like plums and
peaches.The wet ground is making plants
like bamboo, palms and cannas very
happy; these are making strong and rapid
growth. It is an opportunity to make
changes if you have waterlogged areas;
perhaps you should replace plants that are
unsuitable with something that doesn’t
mind getting its feet wet.
The lawn mowers are getting a
good workout, but what to do with the
lawn clippings? One idea is to use it as
mulch in garden beds. It can introduce
weed seeds, but if the grass is cut before
it seeds this should not be a problem.
Spread the clippings evenly and not thick
so that it doesn’t form a gooey mass.
Keep it well away from the trunks of trees
and shrubs as it can create conditions
ideal for the collar rot fungus
phytopthera.
Another approach to deal with
grass clippings is to incorporate them
into a compost heap. It is best to put
down a layer of coarse material like
shredded prunings and then a layer
of clippings, a layer of manure if you
have it, if not, shredded paper would
be good.After each couple of layers
are put down, a generous application
of blood and bone and some ground
limestone will help the composting
process along. If it is not raining the
heap should be kept moist.The
compost will become quite hot and
after a week or two it should be turned
over. I use a heap directly on the
ground and turn it with a fork ensuring
that it is composted evenly.
When the compost has cooled
and is of a nice, sweet smelling
consistency it is ready for use.
Compost can be added to vegie beds
or incorporated into potting mix; plants
and worms love it.
Check  your roses, they may
benefit from an application of fertiliser
or blood and bone to keep them
growing. When dead heading or
cutting flowers, cut back heavily and they
should make a good autumn flush.
The large citrus bugs are active
and can do quite a bit of damage to
tender new growth. They can be
sprayed with an insecticide or an
alternative is to put on heavy gloves
and catch and squash them. Eye
protection is a good idea, they spray
nasty stuff and if it gets in your eyes
can be very painful.
Any time of year is a good time
to apply a thick layer of mulch to your
garden beds, the benefits are many. As
mulch breaks down, it makes nutrients
available, thus feeding your plants.
Mulch suppresses weed growth,
conserves 
moisture 
(hardly 
a
consideration at the moment!) and can
provide a run for feeder roots above
waterlogged soil. Remember, it must
be thick and is best kept topped up,
perhaps once each year.
Keep toiling, the rewards are
many, for both the body and soul.
