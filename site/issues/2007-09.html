Tilba Bites
Members of Central Tilba’s
School of Arts Trust Inc., are the proud
recipients of The Triangle’s 5th birthday
gift of $1,000. This volunteer commit-
tee manages the two halls in Central
Tilba. These two fine buildings are ac-
tually owned by the Tilba Community
and despite the fact that Eurobodalla
Shire Council do whatever they can to
help in maintenance and restoration,
it is the local community who does the
fundraising and organises whatever
work takes place. Committee members
Rici, Judyanne and Caroline penned the
following comments. ‘We are de-
lighted to receive this gift from The Tri-
angle. Recently major works were car-
ried out on re-lining the ceiling and
gable ends of the Big Hall. As with all
building maintenance and restora-
tion the costs can be surprisingly
high and the committee finds itself
in a familiar position of needing to
make up a shortfall in funds. This
contribution is very timely and we
dedicate the triangular gable ends to
The Triangle. Plans are afoot for a
fundraising curry night with Janine
and Val Halasz in the kitchen – watch
this space for date and details’.
Central Tilba’s small hall was
built in 1894 by SW Bate, prior to the
sale of town allotments in 1895. It
was built as a Temperance Hall and
would have been the first public/
community facility in the village. The
big hall was built about 1914 and
was first used as a roller skating rink,
but was then converted into a dance
hall. Can you imagine the stories
the walls could tell over 102 years
of public use and gatherings? Cur-
rent use of the two halls is frequent
and varied. It can be a wedding re-
ception one day, a meeting space or
a show venue the next.
Specific community use of the
two halls includes Central Tilba’s
Food Coop. This group was formed
in 1985 and bulk buys about $10,000
worth of wholefoods three times a
year to distribute to up to 110 fami-
lies. The group used to order
through Northern Wholefoods at
Coffs Harbour, but now use South
Coast Distributors from Ulladulla
and our own Tilba Dromadeli. These
Coop days, where members get to-
gether and weigh up and package
ordered items, are quite a social day
and allow periodic socialising for
far-flung families. Rici Tandy and
Judyanne Donnellan are founding
members and recollect funny mo-
ments over the years (and some disas-
ters) one of which was young Rhys
Davies from Cobargo as a three-year-
old helping to weigh and package 20
kilos of yummy cashews, which, at the
end of the day, were all found in his
mother’s box! Other regular users of
the halls include SEMAG (South East
Modern Art Group), who have held
their meetings and workshops in Cen-
tral Tilba for many years, and the
Narooma Woodies with their annual
Exhibition over the June long weekend.

Pat’s Piece
What is it
about an open
fire? I remember
when I first came
to live in my little
unit in Bermagui
I didn’t know if I wanted to stay as I
had never lived without an open fire to
gaze into on a cold winter’s night. It’s
such a comfort when you live alone to
have a fire burning, especially when
you first put a match into it in the late
afternoon. The sound of the twigs
crackling is like a friend entering the
house. Then there’s ritual that goes
with it like taking my Grandies into the
surrounding bush to fill the boot of my
car with kindling where you’d hear the
sound of their tiny voices calling ‘here’s
a good bit Nan’. Ah precious memories.
When my own kids were grow-
ing up in Melbourne I would on Friday
night make a very large pot of Min-
estrone or Mulligatawny soup, then on
Saturday we’d either go to the football
or watch it on TV. In the evening we’d
gather round the fire along with any-
one the family would care to bring
home and with a big bowl of soup in
one hand, a slice of fresh crusty bread
in the other we’d have endless argu-
ments about the success or failure of
our respective footie teams. On reflec-
tion there was something quite tribal
and comforting about those gatherings.
One of the most spectacular fire-
places I ever encountered was in a
mansion built by convict labour on a
property where we worked in Tasma-
nia. It was in the reception room, which
measured 60 feet by 40 feet and was
full of antique treasures and a grand
piano with a beautiful parquetry floor
scattered with priceless Persian rugs.
The fireplace itself took up the whole of
one end of the room – you could virtu-
ally walk around in it and if you closed
your eyes you could slip back in time
and hear the carriages pulling up out-
side the French windows, dispensing
the landed gentry in all their finery to
take drinks in front of that massive
blaze.
Finally let me tell you about a
gig we did in the tiniest pub we’d ever
seen. Standing at one end of the bar was
an elderly gent with battered old hat
and long grey beard looking as though
he had been there forever. Tony, our
bass player, exclaimed ‘what a tiny
pub’, whereupon a gravelly voice from
under the hat replied ‘been a lot of big
logs through her though mate’, which
puzzled us until we walked into the
lounge where there was half a tree
burning at the heart of the open fire.
Sadly this modern world no longer al-
lows us time to gaze into those pictur-
esque flames and enter a world of the
imagination and unfulfilled dreams
because I believe that making money
is being reinforced as the only thing
that matters.
Pat Thompson

Residents of Brogo were sad-
dened by the passing away of one time
resident, George Claydon, in August.
George and his partner Beth were long
term residents of Brogo before moving
into Bega a couple of years ago. They

Hawkshead Rd painting scenes of his
beloved Mumbulla Mountain. Our
sympathies and thoughts go to Beth at
this time.
Brogo Carpool Website Now Up
and Running ... Being innovative with
telecommunications to save energy is
something Brogo has done very well in
recent times. The local Brogo Mailing
List is one, and now another great idea
has been launched … a local carpooling
website.
Go to www.tripmeeter.org, type
in the password ‘brogo’ and you can
access this new neighbourhood
carpool. Brogo residents Liam
McManus and Averil Fink have organ-
ised the website with help from the
technical expertise of Josh Brown. This
has the potential to save enormous
amounts of fossil fuels and to cut down
some of the many fuel km being used
each day as so many Brogo residents
travel in to Bega for work. Well done to
Averil Fink and her son Liam for com-
ing up with this great idea.
Although many neighbours al-
ready make the effort to car-share when
they can, this is often very hard to or-
ganise since people need to leave Bega
at different times.The website brings
together information about arrival and
departure times, and it is hoped this
will increase the likelihood of people
being able to travel together – perhaps
going in to Bega with one person and
coming back with someone else.
Brogo Carpool is the first initia-
tive of tripmeeter.org. If it is successful it
is hoped to add other areas in the Bega
Valley. It is also possible to arrange long
distance trips on this website. This
should be useful for people wishing to
travel to capital cities.
Wwoofing ... If you worked it
out in proportion of ‘per head of popu-
lation’, then Brogo could claim to be the
WWOOFing capital of Australia.
WWOOF stands for Willing Workers
On Organic Farms and it’s an exchange
of half a day’s labour in return for a
bed and a feed. People travel from all
over the world to Australia with a list
of WWOOF host properties they can
make connection with. It provides a
cheap way to travel for wwoofers and
an opportunity for hosts to get some
jobs done around the property. Brogo
has at least six properties listed and
the benefit of cultural exchange is very
rewarding. Anyone interested in be-
coming a Host Farm, or indeed, even
becoming a wwoofer, could visit
www.wwoof.com.au.

Many Australians used to say
they were ‘going home’ when travel-
ling to the UK. Nothing could be fur-
ther from the truth these days as the
contrasts on that little island with Aus-
tralia make the expression totally re-
dundant.
The weather for one thing is al-
ways discussed and having travelled
widely I can understand why, as it is
very miserable when wet. The contrast
in the appearance of the mostly stone
buildings in the sunlight compared to
the grey days makes going out pleas-
urable for anyone, resident or tourist
alike.
The busyness of the cities and the
size of even the smallest towns has to
be seen to be believed, and handling the
traffic going into and out of any area is
a major problem for everyone – driver,
passenger, public transport etc. Yes,
there is even public transport in the
country and along the little country
roads, something that Australians can’t
even begin to imagine. Those wretched
speed cameras are everywhere too, and
uglier than ours if that is any consola-
tion and the speed limits vary just as
much.
The other major thing from
Travelling Notepad
which we can all learn is the Tourism
with a capital T. Tourism makes the
world go around and really Australia
needs to get on the band wagon and
improve all our facilities if we want to
benefit in the long run. Every town and
village (Europe & UK) makes a huge
effort in Spring to produce colourful
baskets and tubs of flowers that are
displayed throughout the areas. From
observation, the hanging baskets and
tubs do not get ‘knocked off’ as hap-
pens in our country to the detriment
of those concerned with providing
these features.
There is such a pride amongst
people in giving pleasure and service
to others, which we could do well to
emulate in Australia and which costs
nothing and produces many long term
benefits.
We have a wonderful country
and live in one of the most picturesque
places in the world and if we can just
make the most of its natural facilities,
we can compensate for the losses that
have changed the fishing and other
industries in our region.
