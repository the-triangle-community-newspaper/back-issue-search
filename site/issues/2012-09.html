Council Elections for the Bega Valley Shire  
- your vote counts on Saturday September 8th
Sunday August 19 a group of 14 potential 
Bega Valley Shire councilors came to a meeting 
in Tarraganda to speak to the public and present 
their policy views. Sixteen are running in this 
election and 9 will be elected. In the group of 
14 there were 6 women and 5 of these women 
are new to the potential role. 
I am writing about the women in this 
article as we have only had one woman 
representative on council in the last 4 year term 
and with 6 candidates this time it is evident that 
a more equal gender base will produce a council 
that is representative of the shire’s population. 
The last 4 to 8 years evidently have not provided 
a council that has been consultative with the 
community on a number of developmental 
issues across the shire. This was evident in the 
comments and questions that were asked of 
the councilors at the end of their policy talks.
The 5 new women candidates Mary 
Dawson, Ann Mawhinney, Christine McBain, 
Judy Geary, and Sharon Tapscott are from 
a range of backgrounds and as each woman 
spoke it was evident that they have the drive 
and enthusiasm to make a difference. Their 
backgrounds are impressive; solicitors, nursing, 
management, but what I heard was a strong 
desire to include the shire’s residents in decision 
making – a strong desire for consultation from 
the people before major decisions can be made 
that will have long term impact on our area.

The Triangle has recently received a report 
published by Master Grocers Australia titled 
‘Let’s Have Fair Competition’. This document 
outlines some of the problems associated with 
losing retail diversity and ‘true competition’ in 
the Australian supermarket industry.
In the foreword, Associate Professor 
Robin Goodman (School of Global Studies, 
Social Science and Planning, RMIT University) 
acknowledges that: ‘The economic competition 
that a new business might provide for existing 
businesses is not generally considered a valid 
planning issue and cannot be utilised as an 
argument to oppose development.’ However 
he also states: ‘Local government planners 
should be concerned about nurturing and 
protecting the existing town centre as a 
whole. This is not the same as acting to 
protect individual businesses.’
In Australia, the grocery market is 
dominated by Coles and Woolworths. It has 
been estimated that they account for about 80% 
of the market share. So what? Apparently this is 
extreme in relation to other developed countries. 
In America the two largest supermarket chains 
have a 20% share and in the U.K. the 5 top 
chains account for about 83%. Thus a loss of 
retail diversity and of ‘true competition’ as the 
‘big boys’ push others out of the marketplace.
So how do the members of this Australian

despite small, or even negative, population

RMS publicly displayed a preferred

road alignment displayed in December 2010 
needed large scale bridge and road work. A 
presentation outlining three options for the 
upgrade was placed on the RMS website soon 
after. 
As part of the ongoing planning process, 
RMS is now reviewing the proposed road 
corridor and is investigating an alignment to 
the west of the existing Dignams Creek Bridge. 
This is a result of community concerns and the 
need to progress a design which gives better 
value for money. The western option now 
being investigated would reduce the impact on 
Dignams Creek valley and could be built at a 
considerably lower cost.
The option displayed in December 2010 
was costed at an estimated $90 million to 
build. The option now being investigated could 
possibly be built at less than half.

Dignams Creek family will be heavily affected 
by whatever option is decided.

geotechnical studies are being carried out in the

The Dignams Creek upgrade aims to 
improve the highway to current standards to 
improve safety and reduce crashes, with a 
full realignment in the northern portion of the 
project. In the southern part of the upgrade RMS 
is investigating using the existing alignment 
where possible with some road widening and 
a central median to further reduce the chance 
of vehicles crossing on to the wrong side of 
the road.
RMS has reviewed the road to the north 
of Dignams Creek and will start development 
of this portion of the highway when funding 
becomes available.
South of Dignams Creek the Princes

Dinner with  
Dr Ken Henry
Thursday, September 27th  
at the Wharf Restaurant Merrimbula
The Mumbulla Foundation will be hosting 
a fund raising dinner on Thursday the 27th of 
Sep 2012 with Dr Ken Henry, one of Australia’s 
leading economists and policy advisors as the 
principal speaker.  The dinner will be held at 
the Wharf restaurant in Merimbula, and will 
provide an excellent opportunity for interested 
local people to ask questions about Australia’s 
economic future and at the same time support 
the Foundation’s activities in providing grants 
to local community groups.
Dr Henry, who resides part time in 
Bermagui, was head of the Federal Treasury 
for 10 years. During that time he was the 
Government’s principal economic policy 
adviser, sitting on the Board of the Reserve Bank 
which sets interest rates, and was author of the 
major tax review that included mining industry 
taxation. He was also architect of Australia’s 
response to the Global Financial Crisis and 
has a particular interest in environmental and 
sustainability issues.
Dr Henry has offered to speak at the dinner 
pro bono to support Mumbulla Foundation’s 
fund raising efforts. He will discuss issues of 
general interest about the international and 
Australian economic outlook, as well as issues 
of particular interest to local business.  It will 
also give local businesses a chance to air any 
concerns they have to someone who is listened 
to in Canberra.
Ken Henry is an engaging person, not 
afraid to speak his mind. The dinner represents 
an important event for the Bega Valley area 
and a unique opportunity to learn about how 
government works in Canberra and what are 
likely to be the big issues facing Australia going 
forward.  There will be ample opportunity to ask 
questions and quiz him.
The cost of the dinner per person is $50 
plus a tax deductible donation of $100 to the 
Mumbulla Foundation. Persons interested in 
attending should contact Gary Potts on 0418 
720 908.

Well. Here it is again. Spring that is. 
Doesn’t it come around quickly?
Spring brings with it an enormous 
amount of work in the garden with the last 
days of deciduous roses and fruit tree pruning 
completed and hopefully all your winter 
spraying of these plants done. Remember 
if your plants have 
started to blossom 
then it is too late to 
winter spray as the 
chemical will burn 
the flowers off and 
hence no fruit.
You will have 
no doubt noticed 
t o m a t o e s a n d 
associated summer 
vegetables on display 
in nurseries and 
garden centres and 
although they are 
for sale, it is really 
only for those of you who have a temperature 
controlled glass house or similar. Planting these 
plants out before mid to late October in soils 
that have no heat in them as yet, is basically and 
absolute waste of time. Tomatoes especially do 
not like cool soils and in fact will just sit there 
not growing and when the weather and soil 
does warm up they will still sit there, sulking. 
My old dad had a policy of listening to the 
Melbourne Cup and after the disappointment 
of not picking the winner would go out into 
the vegetable garden and plant his tomatoes. 
Even then he would cover them with an old tin 
at night for a couple of weeks “just in case”.
If you haven’t thought about the 
preparation of the vegetable garden at this stage, 
now is the time to start. Spading or forking 
over the soil to a depth of 25cm or so and then 
topping it off with some animal manure will do 
the trick and then leave it for a couple of weeks 
to settle. At the time of planting fork over again 
and level and add some garden lime and rake it 
in. The lime sweetens the soil and a sweet soil 
will give a sweet vegetable. 
The last chance to plant bare root fruit, 
shade trees and roses is now, and from now 
on only good nurseries will have these plants 
available in containers. If you have left your run 
a bit late, buy containered plants, as these then 
have time to establish in the pot and thus give 
you a lot more 
time to plant.
There is a 
bit of a fallacy 
out there that 
container grown 
plants can be only 
planted at certain 
time of the year. 
As a horticulturist 
of some 45 years I 
can guarantee you 
that if you follow 
sensible planting 
techniques, plants 
can be planted all 
year round. Some consideration should be taken 
on extremely hot dry days, but with good soil 
preparation and a drink (the plant not you) after 
planting, every success will be had.
And on the matter of planting all year 
round I think it timely to mention plant 
availability. Wholesale nurseryman who 
supply to the retail garden industry, operate 
fairly similarly to farmers, that is they also 
plant crops. To avail themselves of a steady 
income throughout the year they will spread 
the varieties out over the year so we as retailers 
will have to wait until that next “crop” becomes 
available. The grower will have greater success 
in selling his product when it is at its best,

I read this book on the recommendation 
of the wonderful staff at Candelo Books, 
who nominated it as their choice for the 
Prime Minister’s Literary Award, which it 
subsequently won. I remember reading her 
earlier novel, The Mint Lawn, years ago and 
loving it (must read it again because I’ve 
forgotten all about it!)
I found Foal’s Bread deeply disturbing and 
at one point thought I’d have to stop reading it. 
But I persisted and was glad I did, even though it 
brought on a couple of horrible nightmares. It is 
set prior to the Second World War in rural NSW, 
when show jumping was a big part of country 
life, and tells the story of a young couple, both at 
the height of their skills as show-jumpers. They 
marry, move in with the husband’s family and 
have two children of their own, before tragedy 
strikes, leaving the husband a virtual cripple. 
Gillian Mears, interviewed when she won 
the Prize recently, tells of her own love of horses 
as a young woman, and this comes through on 
every page,  so anyone with a similar interest 
will love the treatment of lives lived around 
the passion for the animals, all of which was a 
bit lost on me I have to say. I’m not sure that I 
would have chosen this as the novel of the year 
ahead of Anna Funder’s, All That I Am, but it 
is certainly one of the most challenging books 
I’ve read for ages.

Habitat for Birds
Pictured are two bird houses.  One has a 
more traditional appearance.  The other bird 
house started out looking the same but was 
altered in order to lure birds that normally 
nest in native tree hollows.  Because many 
native habitat trees have been cleared, people 
are providing bird homes that resemble bird’s 
natural nesting sites.  These lovely bird houses 
were made by WIRES’ friend Kevin Kimber. 
He passed them over to his neighbour Dan Katz 
who then drilled a hole in a native branch and 
put the branch in the opening to create a tunnel 
leading into the bird house.  The tunnel is small 
enough to keep out cats that are allowed to 
wander. Clean brackets were used to attach the 
bird house to a sturdy tree.  It had to be secured 
correctly as you don’t want it to fall down with 
baby chicks in it!    Bird houses must be hung 
as high as possible to try to deter free roaming 
cats from attacking the birds.  The idea for this
