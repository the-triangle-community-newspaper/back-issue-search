They say winter slows things down, 
but in the Triangle, it never really slows 
but there is a noticeable change of tone. 
Sure, there are fewer tourists around 
but we locals are doing the same things, 
appropriate to the season.
The Cobargo rebuild is nearing 
completion and there is excitement and 
curiosity for this new section of town. All 
the community members who have been 
involved in the herculean task to get this 
multi-faceted project to completion and 
beyond deserve huge recognition. These 
are citizens who have jobs and families, 
or may be retired, but have committed 
to a project years in the making and who 
mostly haven’t had experience at this level 
of multiple buildings and budgets. After the 
fanfare of an opening, that will be just the 
beginning of managing and maintaining a 
partially new town. Cobargo will grow into 
itself; it will take time, capacity and faith to 
invest in new businesses and new residents 
in town will bring a different energy.
Time to go …
Coming to live in Yowrie after leaving 
Sydney in 2003, and then moving to 
Cobargo in 2015, I’ve seen so much 
change between then and now. Working at 
Well Thumbed Books in Cobargo, a main 
street business since 2010, one could see 
the changes weekly. Another big part of 
my life in Cobargo has been my abiding 
involvement with The Triangle. I was 
originally asked by Sarah Gardiner and Jen 
Severn, long-time denizens of the paper, to 
learn how to do the layout using InDesign 
software. I struggled with the learning as I 
was not a computer person, but with time 
I managed to fudge along till I stepped 
away from that role and found a better 
fit. Since that time, I have had to become 
conversant with computers, software, filing 
and managing. Living and working in 
Cobargo, being involved, getting to know 
other organisations and people, creating 
connections was a good fit for my role 
in The Triangle. But the best part was 
working with colleagues who became my 
friends and family, all with a singular focus 
on creating the best community paper we

for about twelve years, but it’s time to go. 
I don’t know how I’ll be not working on 
it, it’s been totally engaging, enjoyable 
and sometimes frustrating, and it’s taken 
up probably too much of my time; but it 
has given me an identity to be proud of.

Koalas in our Backyard
Yes, there are koalas locally, though 
not quite in our backyards – yet with 
support that could be our future. From 
being endemic throughout our region, 
koalas are now in ‘remnant communities’. 
Koala reduction is mainly due to habitat 
loss from wood-chipping and land clearing, 
and additionally fire and chlamydia 
infection. Each threat can be addressed. 
Local data shows that koalas thrive 
again when native forest ecosystems 
are protected, when good conditions are 
restored for all our biodiversity.
The Far South Coast Koala Action 
Network (KAN) is hosting a community 
information and engagement event, 
‘Koalas in Our Backyard’, on Saturday 
22 August from 10.00 am to 3.00 pm in 
the Bermagui Community Hall during 
Science Week. The event focuses on the 
koala communities south of Bermagui and 
in the Dignams Creek areas.
The day is intended to be family-
friendly, informative and interactive. 
The morning is big-picture information 
and includes Indigenous science and 
relationships with koalas, historical effects 
on koalas, their present situation, location 
mapping from recent thermal drone 
surveys, DNA studies, current threats and 
protections, with engaged networking for 
locals interested in becoming involved with 
ongoing citizen science activities.
The afternoon is more science based 
with information on thermal drones, 
DNA research, viral loads, fire protection 
strategies, healthy habitat identification 
and how to register a koala sighting.
Specific stalls circling the hall will 
enable direct interaction with experts 
during morning tea and lunch breaks. 
Presentations include strategies for low-
intensity fire protection and buffer zone 
creation, networking with engaged koala 
neighbours, ‘koala facts’ expertise, maps of 
koala locations (maybe near you), examples 
of koala feed trees, KAN membership 
and recruitment for field work training, 
information about sustaining healthy 
forest ecology and scat identification – a 
favourite with children. Two local authors 
of children’s books illustrated with local 
native animals and flora will be there, 
together with story-telling and a puppet 
show. KAN has an informative website 
– search for ‘far south coast koala action 
network’.
Lunch and morning tea are provided. 
All are very welcome. We would appreciate 
registration via the QR code on the poster 
for catering purposes.

The Bega Valley Women’s Giving Circle
A new kind of local giving is taking 
shape in the Bega Valley. The Bega Valley 
Women’s Giving Circle, an initiative of 
Mumbulla Community Foundation, brings 
together women who care deeply about 
their community and want to create change 
through collective giving.
Launched late last year following 
strong community interest, nearly 50 
women are now involved, with each 
member making an annual financial 
contribution. Together, members decide 
how funds are directed, ensuring every 
woman has an equal voice in supporting 
projects that reflect the priorities of the 
Bega Valley and that matter to them.
The model is simple but powerful. 
Each year, 45% of the fund is distributed 
through grants to local initiatives, 45% is 
invested in a growing perpetual fund, and 
10% supports administration – ensuring 
sustainability so that the Circle can 
continue to create an impact and keep 
giving for generations to come.
The Giving Circle’s newly launched 
SheCares grants are open to local 
organisations and grassroots groups with 
ideas that support people across the region. 
Projects focused on connection, wellbeing, 
leadership and strengthening opportunities 
for people to thrive are encouraged, with a 
particular emphasis on uplifting women as 
well as young people.
Beyond the dollars, the Circle creates 
a space where women can connect, share 
knowledge, support one another and be 
part of something bigger through a shared 
purpose. While members can become 
involved through events, working groups 
and grant activities, there is no expectation 
beyond making their annual contribution 
and voting on grant decisions.
At its heart, the Giving Circle is about 
local women backing their community. 
Through collective giving, members 
combine resources, experience and 
networks to invest in solutions that reflect 
local needs.
Find out more on the Mumbulla 
Community Foundation website or email 
wgc@mumbullafoundation.org.au.

Threatened bats found at Cuttagee Bridge
The Restoration of Cuttagee Bridge Group is calling for a 
full Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) after it identified the 
Southern Myotis bats at the bridge. Also known as the Fishing Bat, 
the microbat is listed as vulnerable and protected by legislation.
Group member Stuart Meagher monitored the bridge area 
with his thermal imaging and infra-red equipment over a two-
month period. He sent his footage to Dr Anna McConville, an 
ecologist who runs a company called Echo Ecology and Surveying 
on the North Coast.
In her letter, she said Cuttagee Bridge appeared to have 
suitable roosting and breeding habitat for Southern Myotis and 
that Stuart’s footage showed the bats foraging nearby and activity 
from multiple bats near the bridge structure.
The group is now calling on Bega Valley Shire Council 
(BVSC), Member for Bega Dr Michael Holland and NSW Roads 
and Regional Transport Minister Jenny Aitchison to insist on an 
EIS.
Stuart said the Review of Environmental Factors (REF) 
being conducted for the bridge project was superficial and lacked 
details that councillors needed to make informed decisions about 
the project.
‘Our group has no confidence in an REF to address the 
serious impacts likely to occur on the environment, biodiversity, 
fisheries, tourism, community safety and cultural heritage,’ he said.
In February 2026 the group asked Dr Holland for permission 
and about $3200 funding for a professionally prepared Microbat 
Survey. Dr Holland forwarded the request to the Environment 
Minister Penny Sharpe, but there was no response.
Council’s website says that an EIS is not required and 
that an REF is the appropriate and legislated pathway for the 
Cuttagee Bridge project. ‘An REF is inadequate for the sensitive 
environment in which the bridge is situated,’ Stuart said.

Calling curious naturalists, big or small, around the region
A huge number of bat species world-wide are known as 
microbats. Most microbats are insectivorous but there is one 
Australian species, Myotis macropus, that has a more diverse diet 
and an unusual way of acquiring food.
There is a lot of information contained within its scientific 
name, Myotis macrocarpus – this translates as mouse-eared 
big foot. And, indeed, it has small mouse-like ears, and those 
almost transparent ears are sensitive to the smallest sounds in its 
environment. And big foot? For a very small animal (63 mm long 
with a 28 mm wide wing-span and weighing a mere 15 grams) it 
does have outsized feet. Those outsized feet are the next clue to 
a very unusual capability. Their feet, with long, hairy, distinctly 
separate toes finishing in long, curved claws, are used for fishing.
They fly above a body of water, trailing their claws along 
the surface of the water to scoop up prey – aquatic insects and 
tiny fish that they eat as they fly. Because of this specialised diet, 
they are never found far from permanent water.
Myotis macropus can be found from the far north-western 
corner of Western Australia right around the eastern Australian 
coast to South Australia, and even up some of our larger river 
systems. They live in colonies that roost in a wide variety of 
places such as caves and tree hollows, but they can also utilise 
structures such as tunnels, mines and the undersides of bridges.
It is thought that the old Cuttagee Bridge may have a colony 
of these bats. If you sit still at sunset, you may be lucky enough 
to see some of these tiny animals flying and swooping above the 
water as they hunt in the twilight.

What is Death Literacy, and how can we achieve it?
Death is a journey we all take, yet few of us receive the map 
– or know how to navigate it kindly and safely when we do. Death 
literacy fills that gap. It equips us with the wisdom and practical 
tools needed to make informed end of life decisions, from guiding 
palliative care to holding those deeply personal conversations with 
family before it’s too late.
The term grew out of Australian research by Western Sydney 
academics between 2009 and 2015, who discovered that caring 
for someone dying at home builds practical wisdom through 
lived experience. This research led to the creation of the Death 
Literacy Index.
Out of this broader movement grew the annual Dying to 
Know Day on 8 August, with this year’s theme, ‘Nobody Knows’, 
shining a light on a simple truth: keeping our final wishes a secret 
leaves loved ones navigating by painful guesswork.
Today, the Death Literacy framework underpins the mission 
of the Compassionate Care Collective. Across the Eurobodalla and 
Bega Valley shires, our network of end of life doulas, deathcare 
practitioners, community members, health professionals and 
advocates are coming together to bring death and grief back into 
the heart of community care.
To turn those secrets into supportive conversations, the 
Compassionate Care Collective is hosting a free community event 
on Saturday 8 August from 10.00 am to 1.00 pm at Kyla Park 
Hall, Tuross Head. With Shanna Provost as MC, the morning 
features local voices experienced in end of life work – including 
representatives from Palliative Care NSW, end of life doulas and 
natural burial specialists – ready to share practical insights and 
answer your questions.
Come explore what it means to plan ahead. Registrations are 
essential via Humanitix.

‘Upgrade’ works to the beach’s track 
head and carpark area have diminished 
the natural setting forever, with large-
scale clearing of ancient burrawangs, 
removal of several spotted gums, bitumen 
sealing of the carpark, edged by extensive 
concrete and an obtrusive ‘bollard forest’ 
– amounting to ugly engineering works. 
Like most ‘bush’ destinations, Nelson 
should be unsealed to highlight the natural 
environment.
NSW National Parks and Wildlife 
Service (NPWS) said the works address 
supposed flooding, sediment runoff and 
summer tourists. Lifelong Nelson visitors 
argue flooding is not an issue, nor does 
minor sediment runoff warrant such 
drastic measures. Short-term visitor influx 
will still require responsive operational 
management, regardless of expanded 
parking space.
While NPWS undertook some 
community liaison, many who received 
information were unaware of the extent 
and nature of the outcome. Large swathes 
of native bushland have been cleared, 
including burrawangs that are especially 
valued by Indigenous culture. The 
‘upgrades’ undermine the stated project 
and national park plan management 
objectives: to protect cultural, natural and 
social values and use of sustainable design 
and construction.
NPWS only recently publicly 
displayed the Review of Environmental 
Factors underpinning the work. Given the 
scale, a draft design plan and environmental 
assessment should have been exhibited for 
public comment. When it is re-opened, 
there will likely be dismay that Nelson 
Beach entry now resembles a suburban 
carpark. Fortunately, the rest of the Nelson 
landscape and Mimosa Rocks beaches will 
not have a similar fate.
NPWS generally does an excellent 
environmental protection job with scarce 
resources. However, there is a fear Nelson 
could represent a trend towards over-
engineered, heavy-handed inappropriate 
development of our national parks.

Ordinary People as 
Mystics and Monks
Open Sanctuary at Tilba recently held 
a reflection day on the theme Ordinary 
People as Mystics and Monks with a 
particular emphasis on the Benedictine way 
of monasticism.
Traditionally, monks have lived 
within a cloister of some kind and mystics 
were considered a kind of spiritual elite. 
Today we recognise the experience of 
ordinary people and those who are drawn 
to live a monastic rhythm of life in the 
world, a monastery without walls. During 
the day we covered the essential precepts 
of the Benedictine way, the pattern of days 
lived in prayer and meditation, lectio divina 
(sacred reading) and work to sustain the 
community.
I am a Benedictine Oblate and I would 
suggest that the stability, stillness, silence 
and simplicity of monasticism could be a 
counter to the social and ecological crises 
of our times. Monks and mystics are 
generally contemplatives. Contemplation 
has been described as a ‘long, loving look 
at the real’. We reflected on what supports 
the contemplative stance in life. In a world 
distracted by so much unreality it seems 
important to pay attention, with love, to 
discern reality. I suggested that developing 
a monastic rhythm of days in our ordinary 
lives can help.
For more information about Open 
Sanctuary go to the ‘open sanctuary 
tilba’ website. If you’re interested you 
can also visit the ‘Bonnevaux Centre for 
Peace’ website.

Deb Worgan
It’s nice to be home.
I have been travelling and, coming back to Australia and the 
far south coast, I can see it with fresh eyes. Some things I found 
to be different, and others were comfortingly similar.
Those different things included the shock of how early the 
winter night comes on here. We had been in a European summer 
with evening stretching until about 9.30 or 10.00 pm. Here we are 
in darkness by 5.30 pm, but at least now the days are starting to get 
longer. Also, of course, the temperature was a big shock. Europe 
was in the middle of a heatwave when we left so it was almost 
a relief to come to more manageable climes. The temperature of 
the water was also vastly different with very pleasant 22-24º C 
water being replaced by a chilly 16º C or lower temperatures here.
Driving and using the roads in Europe as pedestrians or 
cyclists required a brain adjustment to the right side. I forgot 
a few times and was almost wiped out. There are fewer large 
shops, at least that I saw, and more small corner stores, chemists 
and lottery shops. Language of course was a big adjustment. We 
found almost all young people were able to help us in English 
but signage on public transport unreadable and menus in Greece 
were frustratingly similar, but not. Croatia seems tto have very 
few vowels in their words and has so many inflections it is hard 
to know how to read it. At least Italian and Spanish seemed 
more familiar and logical to us. Queues were long and slow but 
Australian airports and events are equally bad in this respect. 
Crowds at attractions were expected.
In Croatia we found everyone smokes – in cafés too – all day. 
As well as smoking, coffee is an obsession, with people drinking 
strong black coffee from morning until late at night – five or six 
cups a day! In a pub, people would often order coffee instead 
of a cold drink. No one wears hats – ever – and helmets are 
optional. They have clever windows that open on a side hinge or 
alternatively open on a bottom hinge. Also, heavy metal shutters 
on a lot of buildings were great to keep that early morning sun 
out. The standard size of a doona is single so at least you don’t 
have to share. 
Greece is a land of neighbourhood cats. People leave food 
and water out for them, and they are generally accepted and looked 
after. Public toilets cost one or two euros, but they are clean and 
often have a resident cleaner on site. Also, no paper goes into the 
toilet, but a small bin is provided. That was a bit hard to get used 
to. Church bells sounded morning and night, possibly for mass. 
Restaurants use paper tablecloths that are changed with each 
sitting. Shops and cafés open late but stay open until very late too. 
Although ubiquitous in Australia, I didn’t see one plastic chair 
in Europe. Greece did offer some similarities and reminders of 
home. Roads were often lined with flowering oleander bushes in 
alternating pink and white, there was the occasional large eucalypt 
tree and bougainvillea in vibrant purple was the plant of choice 
on many tall buildings.
Now to Italy. We had noted the absence of seagulls in other 
countries, but we saw them in Italy and they were enormous! Very 
frightening. Venice has perfectly good drinking water and also 
maintains its free public drinking water fountains. During the hot 
days we experienced, they were popular spots.
These are just some of the small things I noted while we 
were away.
It is so good to be home. To slot back into familiar routines 
and talk face to face with those we left behind. To be able to 
cook at home, wash clothes whenever we like and sleep in the 
same bed for more than a few days at a time, is great. We live in 
such a beautiful, safe and peaceful part of the world, travelling 
reminds you of that.

Bermagui Blue Balls Contingent Swims in Greece
The water was warm, crystal clear, 
calm … and salty. No sharks. No stingers. 
That was what struck us every day as 
we swam in the Ionian Sea off Greece’s 
spectacular west coast.
In June this year, a lucky group of 
Bermagui residents – all members of the 
Bermagui Blue Balls winter swimming 
group – travelled to Greece for a seven-
day swimming adventure. Escaping part 
of the Australian winter was a major 
attraction, along with the opportunity to 
enjoy supported swims through beautiful 
waters, savour wonderful food and soak up 
long, warm Mediterranean days. It proved 
to be all that and more.
The tour, operated by Big Blue 
Swim, provided safe, fully supported 
daily swims that were well organised, 
friendly and flexible enough to cater for 
individual abilities and needs. There were 
21 swimmers aboard our mother ship, 
Mowgli, a beautifully restored 15-metre, 
wooden motor cruiser.
After a trial swim on the first day, 
each swimmer was filmed for individual 
stroke analysis before being divided into 
three pace groups – pink, orange and 
yellow. These groups quickly became 
our swimming families for the rest of the 
week. Each group was accompanied by a 
rigid, inflatable support boat whose crew 
supplied water, mouthwash, jelly babies 
and constant encouragement throughout 
every swim.
With hundreds of islands scattered 
throughout Greece, no two swims were 
alike. Local knowledge ensured each 
route was sheltered from the wind 
while showcasing diverse underwater 
landscapes, secluded beaches, dramatic 
coastlines, hidden sea caves and the 
stunning turquoise waters for which the 
Ionian Sea is famous. We followed coastal 
routes, circumnavigated islands, explored 
caves and swam between islands, with each 
day offering a new adventure.
For many of us, the biggest concern 
was the distance. After the initial trial swim, 
the program called for five kilometres 
each day, culminating in a non-stop five-
kilometre swim at the end of the week. 
That was further than most of us had 
ever swum, and backing up day after day 
seemed almost impossible. But we did it – 
all of us. Twelve islands and 25 kilometres 
were promised, and twelve islands and 25 
kilometres were delivered.
The camaraderie of the group played 
a huge part in our success. The buoyancy 
of the salty water, the encouragement from 
the support crews and the freedom to swim 
at our own pace made each day’s challenge 
achievable and gave us the confidence to 
front up again the next morning.
Of course, there was more to the 
experience than swimming. Every day 
we sampled a different local taverna for 
lunch, afternoon drinks or dinner. One of 
the rewards of swimming so much is the 
licence tto enjoy eating whatever you like 
– fresh seafood, pasta, stuffed vegetables, 
rice dishes, desserts and drinks. Our guides 
delivered us to different harbours each day 
and recommended local specialties, while 
the evenings were ours to discover special 
tavernas and bars.
Looking back, I feel immensely proud 
to have completed the week and so glad to 
have shared an unforgettable experience 
with friends new and old. It was a magical 
adventure that exceeded every expectation 
and one that will stay with me for years 
to come.
Debbie Worgan

Bermagui Blue Balls greets sunrise
There appears to be some confusion 
in the wider community as to the origins 
of Bermagui’s ocean swim group. The 
founder of Bermagui Blue Balls in 2013 
was Gary Pearse. The name of the group 
and its official mascot, the two blue 75 
cm inflatable gym balls tethered in PVC 
fishing net, were Gary’s inspired idea and 
creation. In the early days of the BBB, the 
two inflatable balls would be anchored 
approximately 100 metres offshore and 
the group of winter swimmers would swim 
out and around the balls. When the swell 
in Horseshoe Bay was up, the balls despite 
their size could be briefly obscured from 
the shoreline. Hence the mantra was, ‘To 
be a winter swimmer you need big balls’. 
Whatever other connotation people place 
on the name of the swim group is due to 
their own imagination.
The BBB commenced as an organised 
swimming group that only assembled on 
Sunday mornings between Mother’s Day 
and Father’s Day, and that remains our 
official winter swim season. However, over 
the years the group has morphed into a 
365 days a year swimming group, weather 
permitting. The name has always been the 
Bermagui Blue Balls and will continue to 
be so. Anyone who is prepared to accept 
the ethos of the ocean swim community is 
welcome to come and join us.
And so, on Tuesday, 2 June, 
Destination NSW in conjunction with the 
Sunrise television program prevailed on 
the BBB to be part of their swing through 
the Far South Coast. Sunrise requested 
that BBB’s 82-year-old Sergeant-At-Arms, 
Maggi, be the interviewee. The rest of the 
group were background fillers and then 
encouraged to run into the water on cue like 
elite Ironwomen/men competitors. Wary of 
the perils of the shore break’s underwater 
sand undulations that are treacherous 
for ankles, knees, backs and hips, those 
directions were duly ignored as a more 
cautious water entry was undertaken. 
Apparently, the studio anchorman sagely

Romy Ash is a young Australian 
female author of extraordinary talent. Her 
first novel, Floundering, published in 2013 
was short listed for the Miles Franklin and

Minister’s Literary Award. An amazing 
accomplishment for a thirty-year-old, first-
time author.
Now, thirteen years later she has 
published her wild, weird, watery and 
wonderful second novel, Mantle. It is 
dystopian, Tasmanian gothic (a crowded 
genre) and driven by climate change, but 
don’t let that deter you from this complex, 
marvellous novel. It depicts us and our 
world under pressure and it’s really about 
grief, love and the superiority of the natural 
world.
Ursula is our narrator. She is a 50-year-
old academic, a geologist/stratigrapher 
who studies the earth’s layers and sadly 
says that we are leaving ‘the plastic layer’. 
Ursula is childless, intelligent, self-aware 
and self-deprecating. She is real, funny and 
food obsessed. Ursula anchors us and the 
novel in both the science and the surreal. 
She has returned to this isolated, clifftop, 
self-built house on the D’Entrecasteaux 
Channel on Tasmania’s south-east coast to 
visit her mother. The landscape, the house, 
the cold and the ever-hungry fire are all 
characters in this novel.
Ursula’s mother, Delores, is dying 
because her lungs are failing and Ursula is 
caring for the mother that she has had little 
contact with for many years. They both 
carry the same grief from the accidental 
death of Ursula’s sister and father. Delores 
has chosen the simple life and has been 
‘prepping’ since the 2000 millennium bug. 
The distance between mother and daughter 
closes as death comes nearer.
When the ambulance arrives, both 
drivers are clothed in Hazmat suits and 
Delores’s body disappears from the 
hospital. Something is wrong. The rash that 
Ursula and, it seems, half the town have 
developed, refuses to disappear. Ursula 
and her younger lover awake attached to 
each other by fine filaments; the rash erupts 
into clumps of frightening yet beautiful 
mushrooms. Fungi are proliferating in the 
warming climate, and their spores are in 
the air we breathe. Is this a new epoch?
Soon the world is in lockdown again …
Ash divides the novel into sections: 
Civil, Nautical and Astronomical. These 
are the phases of twilight before complete 
darkness.
The last section is Night, and this 
section will not leave me, at least for a 
while.
There was one short description that 
caused my stomach to turn. But it was 
worth it. Strange, bizarre, beautiful and 
brilliant.

What’s Growin’ On
Mark Evans
Bare-rooted plants look like they shouldn’t work. 
A rose or fruit tree dug straight from the ground with 
no soil or pot, roots wrapped only in damp hessian or 
sawdust. Yet this has been the standard way to grow and 
sell deciduous plants for centuries, long before plastic 
pots existed, and it remains one of the cheapest ways 
to build a garden.
The trick lies in dormancy. Once a deciduous plant 
drops its leaves, it stops transpiring and its metabolism 
slows right down, so roots can sit exposed for days or 
weeks without harm – try that with an actively growing 
plant in summer and it would be dead within hours. 
Nurseries exploit this dormant window to lift plants 
straight from their growing rows, and skip the costs 
associated with transporting pots and potting mix, a 
saving passed on to us in lower-priced, often better 
quality, bare-rooted stock.
There’s another reason to make the most of bare-
root season while it lasts. Many heritage and less 
common varieties such as old-fashioned roses and 
unusual heirloom apple cultivars only ever get produced 
in bare-root form. It isn’t commercially viable for growers to pot 
up and hold rarer stock year-round, so once the season ends, these 
varieties often vanish from nurseries until next winter. And right 
now is the best time to purchase and plant them.
Buying bare-rooted is only half the job – planting technique 
matters just as much. If the roots feel dry, soak the whole root ball 
for an hour or two first. Dig a hole wide enough for roots to spread 
out naturally rather than curl, since curled roots often struggle for 
years. Plant at the same depth the tree was at in the nursery row, 
judged by the soil mark on the trunk and, for grafted roses and 
fruit trees, keep the graft union just above soil level. Firm the soil 
gently as you backfill, then water deeply to settle it around the 
roots. Do all that, and by spring you’ll barely recognise the bare, 
dormant stick you planted back in August.
Around the town gardens, the first jonquils and daffodils are 
nodding their heads in shadier corners, a reminder that spring bulbs 
are best bought and planted while the soil’s still cool, 
before it warms up and works against you. Pyrostegia 
venusta (orange trumpet vine) is also erupting in swathes 
of orange.
This month in the vegie garden we’re still mostly 
limited to planting leeks, shallots, onions, peas, broad 
beans and lettuce, plus rocket, silverbeet and the 
quick-growing radish (ready in twenty-three days). If 
you’re growing spuds, now’s the time to chit your seed 
potatoes: cut into sections, stand eyes-up in egg cartons 
on a sunny windowsill, and let them sprout ready for 
planting later in the month. It’s also your last chance 
to spray peaches, nectarines and apricots for peach leaf 
curl before the buds open, and a good time to plant 
asparagus crowns if you’re after a bed that rewards you 
for a decade or more.
In the wild places around the Triangle, the 
roadsides are having their moment, with several wattle 
species now flowering together in a proper golden flush.
Got a gardening question, plant mystery or pest 
problem? Drop us a line at gardening@thetriangle.org.
au. We’d love to hear from you.
Happy gardening.
