Chaos and the arts in 
Barragga Bay this month 
Regional Arts leaders are gathering at Four Winds 
in Bermagui on Thursday 6 July 2017 for an afternoon of 
discussion and collaboration. Come along and take part in a 
Creative Collaboration Forum, presented by South East Arts; 
then experience the next installment of Four Winds’ Radical 
Voice Series with Dr Justin Macdonnell presenting in person 
on his exploration of ‘Chaos Theory and the Arts’.
From 2 pm, Tathra-based artist Lindy Hume, Artistic 
Director of Opera Queensland, and Michael Brealey, CEO of 
Create NSW, will take to the stage. Join the discussion on how 
regionally-based artists and arts organisations can effectively 
collaborate within and beyond their own regions, build creative 
projects, engage with new audiences, and develop alliances with 
government, business and non-arts organisations. 
Andrew Gray, General Manager of South East Arts, will 
lead a panel discussion focusing on creative collaboration in 
the region with Iain Dawson, Director of Bega Valley Regional 
Gallery, David Francis, Executive Director of Four Winds, 
Shane O’Leary, of Destination Southern NSW, and Sophia 
Zachariou, Director from Create NSW. 
At 5.30 pm Four Winds will present Radical Voice #4 
with Dr Justin Macdonnell, leading arts historian and producer 
of Australian artists around the world, presenting on Chaos 
Theory and The Arts. Why are we always trying to force the 
arts and artists into boxes? Are we eroding wild and whimsical 
unpredictability … and are we getting duller in the process? 
Justin Macdonnell explores these questions and asks if it might 
be time to apply a little chaos theory to the arts scene.

Bermagui Seaside Fair - Public Meeting
A public meeting to determine the future of the Bermagui 
Seaside Fair will be held on Wednesday 19 July 2017, 6 pm in 
the auditorium at the Bermagui Country Club.
For the Seaside Fair to continue, at least six people are 
required to re-establish the organising committee following the 
pending resignations of several existing committee members. 
Direction and advice from outgoing members would be available.
Alternatively, if a Seaside Fair organising committee cannot 
be re-established and the Fair cannot proceed in the future, the 
opportunity exists with equipment, resources and funds available 
for a different, new or existing event that follows the charter of 
Bermagui Seasiders Inc – ie an event held in Bermagui in the 
off-peak tourism season that raises funds for local youth and 
community projects. An established and reputable organising 
committee, an event business plan and appropriate insurances 
would be requirements.

Local residents may not be aware 
that the Bega Valley Shire Council has 
spent much money over the last three years 
with the Spiire Landscape Service revising 
plans for the upgrade of Bermagui’s main 
street. Bermagui was next on the list for 
this upgrade, Eden being first.
There was a meeting of the Bermagui 
Chamber of Commerce last month where 
most of the attendees preferred to scrap 
the Spiire plan completely. There is now 
a proposal to amend the plan and redraft 
it, which is fortunate as – after three years 
of consultation starting with the public 
in street meetings – it has not been at all 
productive to knock back BVS Council’s 
offer of a makeover for the main street 
and the money involved without further 
community consultation.
After the beautification of much 
of Bermagui, ie the wharf, the cricket 
pavilion, the surf club and the community 
centre and with the arrival of Woolworths, 
the main street upgrade is long overdue and 
it would be timely for the businesses to 
profit from a new appearance as suggested 
by Spiire. It is also timely to offer elder 
citizens and the disabled better access and 
ramps to the facilities on the main street. 
Delaying this is detrimental to businesses, 
residents, the environs and visitors to the 
area.  
Bermagui’s upgrade was destined to 
commence after Eden but now because 
of the negativity that has been created, 
Merimbula will be next in line for an 
upgrade. It is now appropriate to involve 
our wider community in any further 
discussion or planning for the town.
As of 20 June 2017 it was reported 
in state-wide press that, ‘NSW is rolling 
in a $4.5b surplus’ and the country areas 
have been promised additional funding. 
The time is now right for us all to confer 
about the future image of Lamont Street 
and the adjoining areas so that it services 
businesses, residents and visitors for the 
future.  
With regards,
Rosemary Millard

June was a sad month for Cobargo, with the loss of some of our 
most treasured friends. I’d like to say a few words about one of those 
losses in particular; a dear friend to me and in fact, to most of us.
Neil Davies was an unusual human being, to say the least. 
Everybody knew him by his daunting appearance and many enjoyed 
the fruits of his generosity, most without knowing it. 
A huge man of muscle, with a passion for wearing shorts all year 
round, Neil contributed to the lives of many. His legendary generosity, 
both of spirit and the tangible, was enjoyed and appreciated by us all 
in one way or another. He would fix anything, make anything and do 
anything that was asked of him, from making gas heaters to keep us 
warm in the School of Arts Hall to making a mobile cool room for 
the folk club for their first licensed festival. 
He helped people out in times of hardship, with extra cash, an 
extra pair of hands, a warm heart, the delivery of a paper in the middle 
of a flood, a market stall, a broken mixer. Whatever the need, Neil 
never had to be asked. He just knew and stepped in to help where he 
could, quietly and without fuss. His contributions, vast as they were, 
provided infrastructure and support to many in the development of 
their ventures and lives. He was instrumental in creating one of the 
first Waste Wise events at the Cobargo Folk Festival, where he sorted 
and inspected waste for contamination, ensuring that the event was 
a ‘zero waste to landfill’ event. Neil was passionate about rubbish!
I met Neil back in the 1990s when he arrived in the community 
and started coming to our monthly folk club concerts and jams. His 
shorts had got him into a bit of a bind because the mozzies were 
voracious and he was one of the tastiest morsels in the room, as far as 
the mozzies were concerned. I had the repellent and he was delighted 
that it was natural. That was the beginning. Along with many others, 
I am proud and delighted to have been his friend.
Our love and condolences go to his daughter Tasha, his son Jules 
and to his five grandchildren, all of whom travelled to Cobargo for 
his send-off.
I’ll be taking a break from you next month in order to 
cross another destination off my ‘bucket list’. The wonderful 
and very capable June Tarlinton will be your correspondent 
for August’s issue and I’ll be back with you again for 
September, refreshed and ready for another year. 
Elizabeth
Vale Neil Davies, with love

 Cobargo RFS report: changes in management
The past few months have been 
relatively quiet on the fi re scene in the 
Cobargo area, with no major wildfires 
or structure fi res attended by the Brigade 
during that period. 
We have been kept busy, however, 
providing assistance with several motor 
vehicle accidents, fallen trees across the 
highway, and responding to unauthorised 
burn offs. Property owners now undertaking 
measures for the coming summer are 
reminded that any hazard reduction and/
or pile burns require notifi cation at least 
24 hours prior to lighting up to Bega Fire 
Control (call 6494 7400, Mon–Fri, 9 am – 
5 pm), and to adjoining property owners. 
Cobargo Rural Fire Brigade held 
its AGM on 1 June. We saw some 
signifi cant changes in the management 
team following the stepping down of 
long serving Captain, Chris King, and 
President Alan Burdon. Mark Ayliffe was 
elected incoming Captain, Max Niemeier 
– Senior Deputy Captain and Training 
Offi cer, and Ron Cole and Tony Martin –

incoming President, and Lisa Ayliffe 
re-elected as Secretary Treasurer. Other 
appointments included Dave Rugendyke – 
Community Engagement, Tarnie Long and 
Jay Rugendyke – Equipment Offi cers, and 
Carolyn Cole – Fundraising.
Special tributes were made in 
recognition of the outstanding service

and Captain of the Cobargo Brigade over 
the past 26 years, and has clocked up 
44 years as a serving Rural Fire Service 
volunteer since fi rst joining the Brigade in 
1973. The Brigade wishes Chris and Lynda 
a well-deserved, happy retirement in their 
new home in Queensland.
The Brigade also invites interest 
from potential new members to consider 
joining us and experiencing the rewards of 
volunteering, and to protect and serve our 
wonderful Cobargo community.

the Bake for Babies project, which raises 
funds to assist the Newborn Intensive 
Care Foundation (NICF) for the Neonatal 
Intensive Care Unit (NICU) at Canberra’s 
Women’s and Children’s Hospital. The 
NICU at Canberra Hospital is the only 
tertiary neonatal intensive 
care unit in southern New 
South Wales. As such, 
critically ill newborn babies 
are transported to Canberra 
hospital from surrounding 
regional hospitals. The Unit 
services an area from the 
southern highlands down 
to the Victorian border and 
from the coast to beyond 
the Riverina. The NICF 
is unique in that no funds 
come out of donations for 
administration. They are 100 
per cent voluntary and 100 per 
cent of donations go to fund 
medical equipment, research 
and nurse education to help 
sick newborn babies of the Canberra and 
southern NSW regions.

Babies we intend holding a soup lunch 
on Saturday 15 July at 11 am at the CWA 
cottage on the Bermagui Road, next to 
the playground. We will be serving hot, 
delicious soups with crusty bread, followed 
by tea or coffee and a selection of cakes 
and slices. There will even be a lucky door 
prize! All are welcome for a donation of 
$10 for this worthy cause. 
All proceeds raised by the Bake for 
Babies promotion will go towards the 
purchase of medical equipment, research 
or nurse education to directly benefi t sick 
newborn babies admitted to the 
Neonatal Intensive Care Unit 
at Canberra Hospital. CWA is 
committed to help mothers and 
babies, and this underpins all 
our work.

Sweet chimes in our Riverbank project
Some people really love the sound 
of the Bell Miner, but their ringing tones 
herald the death of eucalypt trees and 
forests, and it’s happening all up and down 
coastal areas of Australia from as far north 
as Gympie and south to Melbourne.
There is a scale-like creature called 
the lerp that is the larval stage of psyllids, 
and they exude a sugary substance that Bell 
Miners love to feed on. These birds drive 
out smaller birds that might be a threat to 
this food source. The result of that is that 
the lerp numbers get out of control and 
kill off the trees. This allows pest plants to 
thrive, as has been seen with the invasion 
of honeysuckle and blackberry and other 
weeds.
Evidence of this destruction can be 
seen in patches all around the far south 
coast, and it is one of the reasons why the 
original native vegetation along Dry River 
has disappeared. Land clearing obviously 
is another major contributor. All along 
the riverbank in Quaama are skeletons of 
massive trees that once provided habitat 
for a wide range of small birds such 
as pardalotes and thornbills, and other 
predators such as spiders, that feed on the 
lerp and keep numbers under control and 
in balance. 
The Reclaim the Riverbank project 
is making inroads into the denuded areas 
along the riverbank. In June we planted 
about forty trees and shrubs into the area 
that was slashed in May. Sturdy tree 
guards were put around each plant and 
securely staked to keep the wombats from 
dislodging them. Then we had plenty of 
rain to water them in.
In a couple of years the trees will 
reach up to the sky, and by then the Bell 
Miners will have moved on, and hopefully 
never return. The area adjacent to the 
bridge in Quaama is already showing signs 
of progress. The honeysuckle is dead and 
once the trees get taller, the weeds will 
recede.
Our group welcomes new members 
to this project. Come along and help us 
plant and reclaim our riverbank. Our next 
target is to create an access path to one of 
the bigger waterholes in the river. Put these 
dates on your calendar, and give it a go: 
Sundays 2 July, 6 August, 3 September, 1

Historian Peter Lacey recollects the South Coast
Have you seen the two issues of 
Recollections? These publications on 
the history of the NSW south coast have 
caused quite a stir. The founder of the 
NSW South Coast History Society and 
creator of Recollections is Peter Lacey, 
who has been interested in history since 
high school, when he was inspired by his 
teacher to enquire upon the past.
Peter entered Macquarie University 
in 1967, the very first year its doors 
opened, where, in addition to history, he 
studied Measurements and Evaluations 
(statistics in modern speak). Being in 
that inaugural class had its benefits, 
as it inspired students to create the 
education they sought. You wanted to 
sing in a choir? Well, create one! You 
wanted to join in a bushwalking club? 
You organised one! Because it was all 
new, that first year was a ripe atmosphere 
where students could form their school 
according to their own interests. Peter 
enjoyed that freedom and spirit of 
engagement and has kept it alive in his 
interest in historical research.
He also used it as well when 
engaged by Bankcard as it started up in 
Australia to provide statistical analysis 
for the scheme and the participating 
banks. Bankcard was a whole new world 
for banks (can we even imagine a time 
before credit cards?). Peter was proud to 
have been an integral part of rolling out 
this new, profitable service.
Sydney born and bred, Peter and 
his wife Helen joined their daughter 
Jane Woolacott (of Bega and Cobargo 
Veterinary fame) in this area just five years 
ago. Now that they’re happily residing near 
Quaama on a few acres with a handful of 
cows, they’re wondering why they didn’t 
make the move here decades earlier. 
Retirement has given Peter the time to 
pursue his old interest in history. Despite 
not having any roots here himself, he 
has taken to local history with a passion. 
Initially, he combined this with his lifelong 
interest in World War I, researching and 
authoring ‘The unreal story of World War 
I & the NSW south coast’. This is the 
story of how badly the soldiers’ families 
back home were treated by the army 
and government. The tale is a sad one of 
personal loss and bureaucratic failure told 
as only a historian-cum-statistician could! 
Peter helped organise a South Coast 
History Day seminar in February 
this year where 90-plus people filled 
and spilled out of the University of 
Wollongong lecture room. Folks 
were hanging on every word from 
the speakers as the stories were so 
fascinating. A second seminar, ‘Our 
History, Our Heritage’ will be held in 
September. Welcome to all who are 
interested, this all-day event will be only 
$20, including lunch and afternoon tea.
In fact, Recollections too is 
affordably priced. It’s free! Peter feels 
that history belongs to everyone, not 
just to members of those clubs and 
societies that ponder the subject. He 
wants everyone to be inspired by our 
fascinating local history, to research 
their areas of particular interest and have 
their work included in future editions of 
Recollections. He’s hoping to publish 
stories from differing south coast 
groups and people, whether aboriginal, 
fishing, farming, forestry, or feminist, 
you name it! 
Until now, Recollections has been 
printed quarterly but will soon move to 
bi-monthly.
I, for one, am grateful to him for 
this enormous, relevant gift to our 
community and hope that Peter achieves 
his goal of inspiring folks young and old 
to delve into the past and become local 
historians themselves. If you haven’t 
seen Recollections, the first two issues 
can be obtained in electronic format 
by emailing ‘Send Recollections’ to 
southcoasthistory@yahoo.com.

too long to get an early

greeted with three welcoming signs telling 
stories of local environment and heritage.

through the main gateways of Eden, 
Bemboka and Cobargo started last 
year when working with Sapphire 
Coast Tourism on promoting the 
Shire and the uniqueness of its 
individual locations.
Council’s General Manager, 
Leanne Barnes, said the signage at 
each location has been developed to 
refl ect the variety of elements that 
makes the Shire a special place.
‘From the outset we wanted the 
gateway signs to do more than just welcome 
people to the Shire,’ she said.
‘They needed to be signs that told a 
story of individual places, highlighting the 
histories and diversity of a Shire known for 
coast, country and wilderness.
‘Visitors entering from the north of 
the Shire will be greeted by a rural scene 
reflecting the importance of the local 
dairy industry; entering from the west 
through Bemboka will refl ect the stunning 
wilderness of nearby Brown Mountain 
and the Wadbilliga range; and visitors are 
welcomed from the south with a scene 
referencing the importance of whales in 
both the past and future.
‘Each of the signs will acknowledge 
the people of the Yuin and Monaro Nations; 
their culture and position as traditional 
custodians of the land and waters of the 
Shire through the use of the local Aboriginal 
word for welcome, Njindjiwaan. It is 
important to recognise that the People of 
the Yuin and Monaro Nations formed the 
fi rst human chapter in our local story.
‘Choice of materials was an 
important part of the design process, 
and settling upon the use of large 
repurposed timber from historic 
local sources reflects the role of 
timber production, wooden bridges, 
wharves and sleeper cutters in 
forming part of the Shire’s unique 
story.
‘The gateway signage is the 
fi rst step in a plan to introduce other signs 
highlighting the unique nature of towns, 
villages and localities in the Shire,’ Ms 
Barnes said.
The Gateway signs are planned for 
installation before spring 2017, pending 
planning and NSW Roads and Maritime 
Services approval.

Winter pruning 
Winter is well and truly here and the 
time is right to catch up with pruning of 
roses, fruit trees and many other deciduous 
plants in the garden that need to be done 
to form new wood for future flowers and 
fruiting.
It is important to remember that 
pruning too early while the sap is 
still flowing can cause the plant 
to stress with “bleeding” and also 
allows disease to enter these wounds. 
Also pruning roses too early will 
encourage them to shoot with these 
shoots being possibly burnt off with 
a heavy frost. 
Roses should be pruned 
reasonably hard, as the more new 
growth that is encouraged the more 
blooms will appear in late spring. 
If you are unsure of the severity of 
pruning then these few tips will help.
Initially remove all dead or damaged 
wood.
Remove any branches that are crossing 
over another branch or are growing into the 
centre of the bush. The idea is to keep the 
centre of the bush open in a vase shape to 
allow for better air circulation. This will 
decrease the possibility of fungal diseases 
when the plants come into leaf again in 
spring.
Reduce all the other growth by as 
much as half, remembering to ensure 
you have live buds below the cut and the 
upper-most bud is pointing away from the 
centre of the plant, basically pointing in the 
direction you want the new growth to point.
An important fact to remember when 
doing your roses is not to prune those 
that have only one flowering period in 
spring. This usually means many of the 
old fashioned forms and several of the 
climbing varieties. These roses flower on 
the current season growth and if pruned in 
winter there will be no flowers. Pruning of 
these roses takes place in late spring after 
they have flowered. 
The pruning of deciduous fruit trees 
is a bit more complicated and if you are 
unsure of the technique I suggest you 
contact a professional horticulturist for 
some help. Fruit trees develop fruit buds on 
different types of wood so it is important to 
know these so you don’t cut off established 
fruiting spurs, particularly on apples and 
pears. 
Once all the roses and trees have 
been pruned complete a spray of winter 
insecticide and fungicide to remove any 
over-wintering insect pests and fungal 
problems. 
Pruning of hydrangeas should also 
now take place and 
a similar method 
of pruning to roses 
is recommended. 
Hydrangeas flower 
on growth that has 
been made in spring 
on new season wood, 
so the more that this 
is encouraged the 
more flowers you 
will have. 
Now is also a 
good time to divide 
and relocate summer flowering bulbs like 
dahlia and other herbaceous perennials. 
Divide them with a sharpened spade 
and dust with a fungicide to prevent any 
damage to the cut. Plant them in the new 
position with a combination of cow manure 
and a handful of blood and bone to the 
existing soil. Remember that most 
herbaceous perennials like a well-
drained soil, so slightly raised garden 
beds in full sun are the ideal.
Still on the theme of pruning 
it should be well remembered that 
not every flowering tree or shrub 
in the garden needs to be pruned 
in winter. I have seen many spring 
flowering plants heavily pruned in 
winter and then no blossom appears 
in spring. Spring flowering trees and 
shrubs develop their flowering buds 
in late summer and autumn and if 
these plants require pruning, do it in late 
spring after flowering. They then have 
all the following seasons to develop new 
flowering wood.
Remember to ensure all your pruning 
tools are well sharpened and clean before 
you start. Cleaning the cutting tools 
between plants with a diluted Dettol or 
similar will decrease the spread of infection 
from one plant to another.

cucumbers from summer. For a ‘saucier’ 
pasta version, I add a little of the hot pasta

Creamy Roast Chicken 
‘Stroganoff’
olive oil
25g unsalted butter
200g leftover roast chicken
½ onion fi nely diced
1 garlic clove fi nely diced
100g button mushrooms (any mushroom 
will do), thickly sliced
75ml brandy
1 gherkin fi nely chopped
pinch smoked paprika
100ml sour cream
spiralli pasta cooked, or steamed rice to 
serve
fi nely chopped parsley for topping
1. Heat olive oil and butter in wide 
frypan. Over medium heat, cook onions 
and garlic until soft.
2. Add mushrooms, cook 1 minute, 
then add brandy, cook to burn off excess 
alcohol (30–40 secs.)
3. Add gherkin, paprika and sour 
cream, reduce sauce a little for about 5 
minutes, stirring over gentle heat.
4. Add chicken, stir through to warm, 
season, serve with pasta or rice and 
sprinkle parsley over.
Serves 2
Note: you can 
use chicken breast 
strips instead of left 
over chicken, sauté in 
heated oil/butter until 
browned, set aside/
cover and continue 
from step 1, adding 
the chicken as per 
step 4.

½ tsp vanilla sugar

250 g plain fl our

sugar: 2 tsp vanilla

hour. Break off pieces roughly weighing

Exit West
$32.99
Many of you will have read The 
Reluctant Fundamentalist (or seen the 
film), an earlier work of the author. In Exit 
West, he brings a really interesting take 
on the refugee experience, 
writing with a mix of stark 
reality and a touch of magical 
realism which you don’t 
expect in books dealing with 
displacement and incredible 
hardship. 
You don’t know the 
identity of the city about 
which he writes, but it is 
readily recognisable as a 
site of civil war, destruction, 
displacement, death and 
despair – we see it every 
night on TV news programs. 
The central characters are a 
young couple who meet and fall in love 
amid the chaos. They agonise about the 
decision to flee the city, leaving behind 
family and friends. Their escape is through 
a series of magical windows, the first of 
which lands them in Greece. From there, 
they make their way to London, only to 
face a new series of challenges, finding 
housing and jobs and fitting in with the 
wave of refugees who preceded them. 
This book provides a different focus 
on the refugee experience; it explains why 
people leave their homes, and what they 
face when (or if) they arrive at 
their final destination. Often 
this includes barriers which 
appear almost insurmountable 
for individuals, and explains 
why refugees naturally 
gravitate to communities of 
fellow displaced countrymen 
and women for survival and 
support. A central theme of 
the book is the tension which 
arises when desperate people 
see no alternative but to leave 
their homes, yet find little or 
no welcome at the end of their 
truly awful journeys. 
The book is an important contribution 
to what will remain a world-wide problem 
for decades. The element of magical 
realism perhaps reminds us that in a world 
where distance is collapsing, “windows” 
will open through which desperate people 
will escape.
