Meads Bay - just the start
Congratulations to the Triangle
for providing such a consistently
open forum, information and
enjoyable reading for so many
people in our community. A big
Thank You to all those who work to
achieve this.
Other volunteers who have
been working for umpteen years to
protect the environmentally
vulnerable Meads Bay Wallaga Lake
are very pleased with the recent
State Government decision that
upholds its protection.
Meads Bay health is essential
to the health of Wallaga Lake and the
local & wider fishing industry – so
anyone who ever drops a line or a net
is directly involved!  As a local
commercial fisho said: ‘Every fin fish
and crustacean that swims through
Wallaga Lake has spent part of its life
cycle in Meads Bay’.
The significance of Meads Bay’s
environmental importance has been
established by thorough scientific
research and recognised by the
protection recommended by the
Wallaga Lake Estuary Management
Plan, Sanctuary Zone status in the
Batemans Marine Park, Refshauge
Panel, and now the NSW State
Planning Department.
Government aerial photos
dating back to 1944 show old heavily
forested areas. These areas include
significant rain forest, endangered
fauna & birds, and a permanent
spring.
The waters of this spring
evidently nurture optimum
conditions for prawn development
when they merge into the Bay.
Meads Bay was an idyllic
habitat when Aboriginal people
freely lived here as shown by the
significant evidences of those lives
which can still be seen. It remains a
place of great beauty and landscape
value.
And there’s more – much
more; suffice to say, Meads Bay
protection needs to increase still
further.
Marlene Wall

Just a couple of observations
about the change of government and
the fact that we should give thanks
every day that we live in a democracy.
There is, I feel, an air of expectancy
around the country that has been lack-
ing in recent years, particularly when
it came to our cultural soul and our
overriding belief in a fair go. We were, I
felt, in danger of becoming a nation of
‘Hand over our Hearts’ flag waving
patriots. That’s okay if it is part of your
physique, but it’s definitely not ours. If
you watch our body language at any
ceremonial gathering, sporting or oth-
erwise, you will notice a kind of em-
barrassing humility alongside a smat-
tering of irreverence, which is exactly
who we are. The hand over the heart
symbolic gesture has been part of the
American way since time immemorial.
Since we have allowed so much of US
culture to insidiously invade our lives,
it is my passionate hope that this par-
ticular one doesn’t. Here’s to a touch of
irreverence when needed.
On a different note, have you
been to visit our new library in
Bermagui? Such an excellent space has
been created; please visit it if you
haven’t already done so. I had the privi-
lege of sharing an afternoon with the
talented Ian McFarlane and Gayle
Kennedy. Gayle is a young Indigenous
writer who has written an award-
winning account of her life while grow-
ing up out west in Ivanhoe. She was
presenting a copy of her book to the
library and the three of us had been
asked to read to an audience of book
lovers. It was a joyful and heartwarm-
ing afternoon. Gayle is wheelchair
bound due to contracting polio as a
child. Her readings were a hilarious,
inspirational and poignant account of
her life. We were both doubled up with
laughter or fighting back the tears.
Ah, how the soul of this little
village never ceases to amaze me, I just
feel so incredibly blessed to live here.

Anyone reading Richard Tilzey’s
article in the December – January edi-
tion of the Triangle could be forgiven for
reaching the conclusion that there was
no need for marine conservation in our
South Coast waters. If only that were
the case.
Mr Tilzey shows no sign of being
aware of the actual history of Austral-
ian and international efforts to counter
threats to the health of the world’s
oceans. Marine protected areas (no-take
zones, or sanctuary zones, in the NSW
marine park system) have always been
a major component of plans for reme-
dial action.
Australian governments have
been building action for nearly 20 years
to protect the marine environment. The
Howard government adopted Austral-
ia’s Oceans Policy, which included a
commitment to ‘accelerate the devel-
opment of the National Representative
System of Marine Protected Areas’, in
1998. NSW passed its Marine Parks Bill
in 1997 (ie well before the time of the
alleged ‘deal’ claimed by Mr Tilzey). In
The Ocean’s View
this context, the anti marine park lob-
by’s mantra that the whole thing is
nothing more than a parochial vote
grab is ridiculous.
Mr Tilzey refers to his back-
ground as a fisheries scientist, presum-
ably in an effort to persuade readers
that he speaks with authority. How-
ever, nothing could be further from the
truth. The Australian Marine Sciences
Association and the Australian Coral
Reef Society, the peak professional bod-
ies for marine scientists in Australia,
with over 1500 members, stated in
2006, that ‘the vast majority of ecolo-
gists and marine scientists agree on
the value of Marine Protected Areas (ie
no-take or sanctuary zones) to marine
biodiversity and sustainable fisheries
… Comments to the effect that no good
evidence exists for the value of MPAs
and that most scientists dispute their
value, are incorrect or misguided’.
The statement goes on to say
that the benefits of MPAs have been
demonstrated in many scientific stud-
ies. To quote one review of the litera-
ture, no-take zones ‘have led to in-
creases in abundance, body size,
biomass and reproductive output of
exploited species. Such measures often
increase many times over … Popula-
tion build up is usually rapid … In-
creases are sustained over extended
periods … Reserves have benefited
species from a wide spectrum’.
Similar consensus statements
supporting the long-term benefits of
marine sanctuaries have been made by
hundreds of marine scientists in Eu-
rope and the USA.
As time goes on, more and more
people are seeing the benefit of the park.
There has been a bumper season for
tourists, no doubt to the chagrin of the
doomsayers, and the sky hasn’t fallen
in. Surely it is time to get on with it,
and to view the park – especially its
sanctuary zones – as a positive step
toward the preservation of our marine
environment for future generations, as
well as an opportunity that can pro-
mote recreation and tourism.
Bill Barker, Secretary,
Nature Coast Marine Group

Brenda Stone was saddened
recently when two bottlebrushes on
either side of her father’s grave at
Cobargo Cemetry were cut off at the
ground. Her father was Hughie
Williamson and Brenda had nur-
tured the shrubs through the
drought on her visits to his grave.
One shrub had been there for 12
years and there seemed no obvious
reason to remove them. Brenda is
awaiting an explanation from Bega
Valley Shire.
Not everyone in the community
will be aware that the showground is
a Crown Reserve, which is managed
by trustees appointed by the Minister
for Lands, all of whom are local resi-
dents who serve in a voluntary and
honorary capacity.
As well as being the home of the
Cobargo 
Show 
Society, 
the
showground is also the home ground
of the Cobargo District Pony Club, the
venue for the Yuin Folk Club’s folk fes-
tival and also for the Cobargo Horse
and Trail Riders Club’s horse and gear
sale, and the regular meeting place of
the Cobargo scout group.
Without doubt the most visible
change that has occurred at the
showground in recent years has been
the construction of Cobargo’s new
sewage treatment plant with its asso-
ciated holding pond and irrigation sys-
tem. The showground now has access
to a regular supply of treated water for
irrigation of the grassed areas.
Another change has been the
disappearance of the apple gums,
which used to overhang the seating
beside the main ring. These were re-
moved with Council permission as
they were considered a hazard. To re-
place them, a local nursery generously
donated a number of shade trees.
Changes necessary for Cobargo Showground
U

quently comment favourably on its at-
tractive appearance and the high
standard of its amenities, which the
trustees are continually striving to
improve, and such comments are very
gratifying.
The trustees have, however,
been forced to discontinue forthwith
the hiring of the showground for cer-
tain night time private functions. The
reason for this action is the increasing
prevalence of antisocial behaviour and
malicious acts, which seem to occur at
the showground and elsewhere in the
town whenever such functions are
held. All the precautions and security
measures hirers take seem to be no de-
terrent, and the trustees regret having
to take this step.

new Community Centre
Here we are with our long
awaited Bermagui Community Centre
and it has opened up all sorts of av-
enues for enjoyment and enlighten-

the Visitor Centre and last but not least
the Library are also housed.

space and light may not have the views
of the previous one but it has given us
a venue for having book readings and
the Triangle hosted the first one in early
December. Three authors assembled for
what we thought would be a small
gathering. News had got around and
we used every spare chair and our af-
ternoon tea was enjoyed by those who
attended to hear visitor Gayle Kennedy
and locals Ian McFarlane and Pat
Thompson read from their books.
Reading is one thing but to hear
passages and poems from the authors
themselves read aloud with all the
emphasis in the necessary areas was a
special experience and one which we
hope to be able to repeat every few
months. Any thoughts on participants
for our next event would be appreci-
ated.

Techo Advice for this month
(from Craig Tulau, of DNC Alternatives)
It’s thunderstorm season and we
have been busy in the last few weeks
fixing and replacing computers and
modems which have been fried by
lightning. To protect your valuable
equipment from damage caused by
power spikes and surges, it’s a good
idea to invest in a quality electrical
surge protection device. It’s wise to pro-
tect your equipment from spikes trans-
mitted along both electrical and tel-
ephone wires and as such a surge pro-
tector that provides both levels of pro-
tection is recommended. For business
and some home users a combination
surge protector and uninterruptible
power supply (UPS) may be required.
The UPS will provide you with imme-
diate power backup in the case of black
and brown outs. They are designed to
give you enough time to save and close
your work without losing critical data.
Some models also come with a guaran-
tee for the products connected.

Garden Magic
And so we embark upon an-
other year of battling the elements, in
an effort to beautify our surroundings
to meet our own perception and of
course, to try and reduce climate
change. 
It always amazes me when peo-
ple move into wonderful, natural sur-
roundings such as we enjoy and then
want to rid their patch of native
animals. The bottom line is that pos-
sums and wallabies don’t deliberately
single out your address to chomp your
roses and lavender. They’re just trying
to survive, like all of us. However, there
are products on the market which may
move possums on – D’ter and quassia
chips being a couple. They’re not poi-
son; 
they 
just 
taste 
yukky,
apparently. Good luck with the walla-
bies – from what I’ve heard, the only
plant they don’t enjoy is Lomandra
longifolia.
On the subject of pruning, did
you realise that annuals can be cut
back to freshen them up? Plants such
as petunias, marigolds, snapdragons,
etc, tend to look a bit tired after very
hot days, so nip them back and feed
with one of the soluble fertilisers such
as Aquasol, Thrive, Miracle Gro or
Maxicrop. By the way, Seasol is not a
fertiliser – it’s classified as a tonic and
is best used when transplanting, in
order to overcome shock to the plant’s
root system. It also helps annuals set-
tle in. Another product for that pur-
pose is Plant Starter.
