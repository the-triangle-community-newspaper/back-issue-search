Creating a Little Winter Magic in Cobargo
Some of life’s loveliest memories are made on simple 
evenings – sharing a laugh around a fire, wandering 
beneath twinkling lights, catching up with neighbours and 
watching children delight in something magical. That’s 
the inspiration behind Cobargo’s first Lanterns and Lights 
Winter Festival.
The inaugural Cobargo Lanterns and Lights Winter 
Festival will be held on Friday, 31 July, from 4.30 pm to 
7.00 pm, bringing the community together to celebrate the 
simple pleasures of winter while supporting our wonderful 
local businesses and organisations.
One of the highlights of the evening will be the Street 
Lantern Parade. Join in the fun and magic by bringing 
your own lantern for the parade. Families, friends and 
community groups are encouraged to make or decorate 
their own lanterns and meet at Cobargo Park at 5.30 pm. 
The parade will travel under the bridge, following the 
footpath, crossing over at the RSL and then returning to 
the park, creating a beautiful procession of lights through 
the heart of town. 
As the sun goes down, the main street will come 
alive with fairy lights, late-night shopping, market stalls, 
delicious food and warm hospitality. Enjoy complimentary 
tastings of traditional Glühwein, hot chocolate and 
chocolate treats at Twig & Feather, warming soup and 
children’s treats from the Cobargo Primary School P&C, 
homemade cakes and slices from the CWA, along with 
other stalls and surprises.
Plans are also underway for live acoustic music and 
busking in cosy corners around town, a dance performance 
at the RSL and fire pits where everyone can toast 
marshmallows. Bring along a cosy blanket and a roasting 
stick, settle in beside the fire and enjoy the atmosphere.
More activities will be announced over the coming 
weeks, so keep an eye out for posters around town. If 
you’d like to volunteer, be involved or set up your own 
market stall, we’d love to hear from you. Please contact 
Monique from Twig & Feather info@twigandfeather.com.
au or Zoe from Zoe Pook Jewellery zoe@zoepook.com.
We hope you’ll join us for an evening of warmth, 
laughter and community as Cobargo embraces the beauty 
of winter together.

Australian Values
On the June long weekend, I 
experienced two days when my faith 
in the decency, talents and generosity 
of Australians was confirmed at a time 
when it’s so easy to despair at the state 
of the world that sometimes resembles a 
bad dream. This weekend reminded me 
that ‘Australian values’ is a term we can 
rightfully reclaim. Day one was spent at 
Four Winds listening in awe to three of 
Australia’s leading musicians, brought to 
us by a woman of Asian heritage, Andrea 
Lam, accompanied by a British-born 
violinist and an Italian cellist. One of our 
local schoolboys, also of Asian heritage, 
performed two beautiful piano pieces, 
after which Andrea gave him professional 
feedback. I wondered, which of these 
four national treasures would some of our 
national ‘leaders’ nominate as unwelcome 
migrants? This event happened because 
of the generosity of the Myer family, 
descendants of Sidney Myer who escaped 
from persecution because of his Jewish 
heritage. Hundreds of subsequent donors, 
volunteers and dedicated professional staff 
ensure the family’s legacy lives on.
Day two – nearly 100,000 people 
packed the MCG, wearing blue beanies 
to signify their support for the late 
Neale Daniher and his family in their 
determination to fight the ‘beast’ of Motor 
Neurone Disease. One of those who 
plunged into the frozen water was our 
current Governor General, Sam Mostyn – 
where else but Australia?
It’s days like these when I believe that, 
at a community level, we are a generous, 
tolerant nation that benefits enormously 
from its diversity and from our commitment 
to social justice. Let’s not allow a minority 
of our national ‘leaders’ define who we are. 
We know and appreciate the value of our 
neighbours, our local leaders; we admire 
and treasure our musicians, writers and 
artists; we are sincerely grateful to our 
local doctors, first responders, volunteers, 
those who work in our libraries, sell us 
books, teach our children, operate local 
businesses and farms. We understand the 
world of difference between homes and 
investment properties. We are grateful to 
live in a democratic country.
Perhaps we need to be a bit more 
assertive about our positive attributes 
and need to call out racism in all forms 
more loudly. Above all, let’s celebrate 
more vocally our belief that we are decent 
people deserving of thoughtful national 
leadership. We certainly have it at our 
local level!
Heather O’Connor

 Can the Bega Valley keep up with the EV Boom?
The Tesla Model Y made Australian 
history in May 2026 by becoming the 
first electric vehicle to top the nation’s 
monthly sales charts, outselling traditional 
favourites such as the Ford Ranger and 
Toyota HiLux. It also took out the overall 
Drive Car of the Year award.
More importantly, it wasn’t a surprise. 
EVs accounted for almost 20 per cent 
of all new vehicle sales in May, while 
hybrids, plug-in hybrids and battery-
electric vehicles together represented 
almost half of the new-car market. More 
than 21,000 EVs were sold during the 
month – roughly double the number sold 
a year earlier.
The message is clear: electric vehicles 
have moved from the fringe into the 
mainstream.
Petrol and diesel vehicles still 
dominate Australia’s roads, but the trend 
is unmistakable. EV sales are rising rapidly, 
hybrid sales are growing even faster and 
increased competition is driving prices 
down. At the same time, concerns about 
driving range are fading, with many 
modern EVs comfortably exceeding 400 
kilometres between charges.
The question for regional communities 
is whether charging infrastructure is 
keeping pace.
In the Bega Valley, the answer is 
mixed. Merimbula and Narooma have 
Tesla Superchargers (available to non-
Tesla EVs) and additional charging 
options. Bega and Tathra are comparatively 
well served, with a range of public and 
destination chargers. However, Cobargo, 
Bermagui and parts of the wider region 
remain under served.
That matters. Visitors increasingly 
travel in EVs, and convenient charging 
influences where they stop, stay and 
spend. If we want to remain competitive 
as a tourism destination and support the 
transition already underway, investment 
in charging infrastructure needs to happen 
now.
Australia has moved beyond the EV 
experiment. The Bega Valley cannot afford 
to be left behind.
Donna Portland

Koalas detected for the first time  
in new areas of Wadbilliga National Park
In a major conservation win, koalas 
have been detected in multiple locations 
in Wadbilliga National Park for the first 
time. Their detection follows the most 
comprehensive koala survey ever done 
in that section of the rugged escarpment. 
The survey was done by Gadhu Bagan, 
the Southern Yuin Firesticks team of 
Cultural Fire Practitioners, in partnership 
with the NSW Department of Climate 
Change, Energy, the Environment and 
Water.
It took the team of twelve three 
weeks to put specialised acoustic 
monitors in 48 selected spots across 
the rough and remote landscape. They 
recorded whatever called out over a 
two-week period. The work forms part 
of two projects, the Southern-Yuin Koala 
Project and the government’s NSW Koala 
Strategy.
Djiringanj man Koorin Campbell 
has worked with the national not-
for-profit for four years. He said the 
Southern-Yuin Koala Project began in 
2018, helped by a Protecting Our Places 
grant. The care of Country by Koorin and 
his fellow Gadhu Bagan Cultural Fire 
Practitioner Durramah Parsons Campbell 
is reshaping how koala conservation 
is understood and delivered across the 
Murrah, Biamanga, Kooraban, Gulaga 
and broader Wadbilliga landscape.
Historically, koala records in the park 
have been scarce, with most detections 
previously concentrated along the park’s 
western fringe. Considering the damage 
from the Black Summer bushfires the 
detections are very encouraging. None 
of the sites were impacted by last 
December’s Brassknocker bushfire, 
indicating key habitat areas remain intact 
to support the koala population. They 
hope to do a follow-up drone survey in 
the next year.
‘It is ground-breaking for us because 
the koala songlines reach out that way 
from the south coast to northern Yuin 
Country up Nowra way,’ Koorin said. 
‘We believe the absence of Cultural Fire 
has had a massive impact on some of 
our threatened species like koalas and 
potoroos.’

Health Check Convoy coming to Bermagui and Bega 
Free skin checks, health checks, dental checks and a 
safe space to talk are coming to the Bega Valley thanks to a 
partnership between health and community service providers.
COORDINARE (South Eastern NSW Primary Health 
Network), Skin Check Champions, Walkabout Barber, 
Medibank on the Move, Katungul Aboriginal Corporation, 
Southern NSW Local Health District, Directions Health, 
Breastscreen NSW and a number of local health providers have 
teamed up to bring free health screening and more to Bermagui 
and Bega in August.
COORDINARE’s CEO Prudence Buist said, ‘All services 
are free and available to everyone. This is a great opportunity 
for locals to get screened for melanoma, check in on their 
mental health, catch up on immunisations, chat with a GP plus 
much more.’
Skin Check Champions Founder and CEO, Scott John 
Maggs, said, ‘The prevalence of melanoma skin cancer is 120 
times higher in surfers than in the general population, 60 times 
higher in swimmers and 80 times higher in regular walkers/
runners, which perhaps explains the high skin cancer rates in 
coastal regions like the Bega Valley. Early detection genuinely 
saves lives, and even identifying a single melanoma early can 
completely change someone’s future.’
Medibank’s SulliVAN can help you understand, track and 
improve your health, including your heart health and diabetes 
risk. The Walkabout Barber bus, which is fitted out as a mobile 
salon offering professional barber, hairdressing and beauty 
appointments, works as a perfect setting for people to open up 
about their mental health. Directions Health’s Rural Outreach 
Bus, affectionately known as ROB, will also provide a relaxed 
and comfortable setting for people to chat.
The Health Check Convoy will offer free health checks 
between 10.00 am and 3.00 pm on these dates:
•	
Bermagui: Wednesday 5 August, Dickinson Park Reserve 
car park, Lamont St. 
•	
Bega: Thursday 6 August, Bega Recreation Ground 
(netball courts car park), Carp St.

Coastrek’s New Munjip Trail Coastal Walk
Australia’s iconic team hiking and fundraising challenge, 
Coastrek, is heading to the NSW South Coast for the first time.
On 14 August, Coastrek South Coast NSW will invite teams 
of two or more to take on a 20-kilometre or 30-kilometre coastal 
hike from Corrigans Beach or Malua Bay through Surf Beach to 
Batemans Bay, finishing in Batehaven. The event marks a major 
milestone for Coastrek as it expands into one of Australia’s most 
unspoiled and breathtaking hiking regions.
The event will shine a spotlight on the award winning Munjip 
Trail, a 15-kilometre coastal headland walk, officially opened last 
year. The trail name means ‘walking together, let’s go’ in local 
Dhurga language.
Walkers will encounter Indigenous art, interpretive signage, 
audio posts, murals and carved timber message poles that share 
stories of Country, culture and connection.
The South Coast event will support the mental health 
charity Beyond Blue, reinforcing Coastrek’s mission to promote 
wellbeing through movement, nature and connection.
Coastrek founder Di Westaway OAM said the expansion into 
the South Coast represents an exciting new chapter for Coastrek.
‘This is one of the most breathtaking coastal environments 
in Australia, and we are incredibly excited to bring Coastrek 
participants here for the first time. Getting outdoors and moving in 
nature isn’t just good for you, it’s transformative. Time in nature, 
walking with others, is one of the most powerful ways to reset 
mentally and physically,’ Di said.
Mayor Hatcher welcomed the introduction of Coastrek to the 
Eurobodalla Shire. ‘Events like Coastrek bring important benefits 
to the local community, supporting tourism and local businesses, 
and showcasing the Shire as a premier destination for outdoor 
adventure and wellbeing,’ he said.
Participants receive the 12-week Coastrek Training Program 
and access to a supportive community hosted by the Wild Women 
Coastrek team.
Registrations for Coastrek South Coast NSW are now open on 
the Coastrek website and close on 5 July.

Finding Treasure in Overlooked Places
What if the things we call weeds, pests 
and waste were actually treasure? For the 
past five years, I’ve been exploring that 
question across beaches, forests, wetlands 
and riverbanks.
Along the way I’ve discovered edible 
seaweeds on the shoreline, useful wild 
plants growing beside footpaths, edible 
mushrooms that are easy to find, and 
creative ways to turn natural materials into 
art, food and practical household items.
Recently, while travelling the Far 
South Coast, I invited local residents 
to join the Shoreline Treasurehunt 
project, collecting rubbish from beaches 
while slowing down to notice the often-
overlooked gifts of the natural world.
The same philosophy was also behind 
the Carpe exitus (river chicken) village at 
a NSW festival, where hundreds of people 
explored how invasive carp could become 
a valuable food source rather than simply 
a problem.
These experiences eventually inspired 
the creation of Treasurehunt, a full colour, 
visually beautiful, 300-page field guide 
that brings together food, creativity, 
ecology, folklore, practical skills and my 
experience as a commercial photographer 
and ecologist.

protect what they feel connected to. My 
hope is that with the help of this book, 
more people begin to look at their local

What is a home funeral, and how could it help us grieve?
A home funeral invites family and 
community to take a more active role in 
caring for someone after death, creating 
time, closeness and meaningful ritual that 
can gently support grief.
On 25 and 26 April, 28 people from 
across the Far South Coast and beyond 
gathered in Bodalla for a Creating Death 
Care Leaders workshop with acclaimed 
death care educator Bec Lyons, founder 
and President of the Australian Home 
Funeral Alliance (AHFA) and Chair of 
the Natural Death Advocacy Network 
(NDAN). The event was co-hosted by 
Walawaani Way and the Death Literacy 
Institute.
Across two days, participants were 
welcomed into a practical exploration 
of what it means to reclaim death care 
at home and grow death literacy in local 
communities. The workshop focused on 
end-of-life conversations, home funerals 
and funeral planning, caring for the body 
at home, and creating ceremonies. We 
considered affordable, self-directed care 
and creative ways to enable family, friends 
and community to participate in this sacred 
space with confidence.
Attendees learned that in New South 
Wales, when a death is expected, and 
legal and health requirements are met, 
families are allowed to keep the body of 
their person at home for up to five days. 
During this time, they may wash and dress 
the deceased, sit vigil, hold ceremony and 
grieve in ways that feel right for them. 
A shroud‑wrapping exercise showed just 
how simple and accessible these final acts 
of care can be, offering a sense of service, 
closeness and meaning at a time of loss.
Some reflected on how ready they feel 
to return to older, more connected ways. 
As one Compassionate Care Collective 
member shared, ‘I found this workshop 
totally inspiring and empowering … 
A home ceremony and ritual will be so 
powerful for family and friends. Grateful 
for this education.’
The Compassionate Care Collective 
hosts ongoing events that deepen death 
literacy and allow many different modes 
of experiencing and living with grief to 
move gently through our communities. 
You can connect with us via email 
at thecompassionatecarecollective@ 
gmail.com or follow us on Facebook for 
updates on our monthly Life and Death 
Conversation Circles, and future events 
and workshops.
Kerryn Davey

Drive to winter conditions
This week, The Triangle received a 
press release from AAMI, an insurance 
company, reminding us all of the costs to 
wildlife and our own back pockets when 
we collide with animals on the road, 
particularly in winter. It is a timely reminder 
as there were 24,000 insurance claims in 
2025 resulting from collisions with wildlife 
and 80% of those involved kangaroos, 
followed by wombats, wallabies and deer, 
all of which are common in the Triangle 
region. 
Driving in winter with limited 
daylight hours, low angles of sunlight 
creating glare in early mornings and late 
afternoons, often wet roads, fog and rain, 
pose particular challenges. If you are 
driving in a signed wildlife warning zone 
be particularly alert and cautious – hitting a 
large animal is traumatic for all concerned.
So, it is time to remind ourselves to 
drive defensively and to always drive to 
the conditions. In winter this can mean 
reducing speed significantly, scanning 
the roadside for any animals that may be 
about to dash across the road and always be 
prepared to keep you, your passengers and 
other road-users safe. Come rain or shine 
always maintain a safe distance between 
you and the car ahead. You never know 
when you might need it. Do not add to the 
carnage on the verges of our major and 
minor roads, be prepared to stop and check 
any animal you hit and call WIRES (or any 
other wildlife rescue service) especially if

Open Sanctuary at Tilba Tilba in May
In early May, Zen teacher Dr Susan Murphy led around 35 
people at a weekend retreat exploring Thinking Along with the 
Earth. With a strong background in eco-dharma, Susan has a 
special interest in the places where Zen and the deep taproot of 
Indigenous Australian ‘care for country’ speak with each other. 
Through the practice of haiku and engaging with koans, Susan 
invited the group through her teaching, to listen deeply, to simply 
become present to what is, by way of un-knowing – that is to say, 
dropping our discursive thinking, our judgements, our forming 
and holding of opinions about things. As Susan says, ‘living so 
close to just what is happening means opinions about it need not 
apply; instead, a plain kind of wonder – an engaged not-knowing 
– can come into play.’
The weekend concluded with a Council of All Beings. 
Developed in Australia in the mid-1980s by deep ecology 
practitioners Joanna Macy and John Seed, it is a form of practice 
that seeks to heal the felt divide between humans and the other-
than-human world. Sitting at the foot of Gulaga, Open Sanctuary 
is a place that enables such healing.
And then, towards the end of May, Dan Scollay’s community 
choir, That Bunch of Singers, put on a rare concert. Linda 
Chapman welcomed the choir, that has been singing together 
for seventeen years, still with some of the original members. 
Presenting an eclectic mix of songs, all with good messages 
particularly around hope, there were several opportunities for 
everyone to sing together – Leunig’s poignant song ‘Let it Go’, a 
Taizé song and a song of hope written during the devastating ICE 
raids in the US. The choir was in good voice and filled this special 
place close to Gulaga with the messages of hope, perseverance 
and peace. With an invitation from Linda to return any time and 
special thanks to Dan, everyone enjoyed the afternoon tea, the 
good coffee – thanks James – and the company.

 William Zappa
William Zappa has an impressive 
body of work, appearing on Australia’s 
stages, television and movie screens, and 
he lives right here in the Triangle! He was 
recently in The Twelve with Sam Neill as 
the villain – or was he unjustly accused? 
You will have to watch. He was very much 
the villain in The Dry and a very British cop 
in Miss Fisher and the Crypt of Tears. He 
was in Mad Max 2 and also Rake, and I’m 
pretty jealous about that. With this body 
of work behind him, which also includes 
some great audiobooks, I asked William 
how he knew he wanted to be an actor.
‘I was about eight-years old, and my 
cousin Avril was in a Christmas pantomime 
that needed some extras. My cousin Eddie 
and I got roped in. We did something that 
made the audience laugh their heads off, 
and I thought, wow, this is fantastic! It was 
the drama department at high school that 
really got me into acting. When I was 14 
or 15 and had a leading role in Brecht’s 
Caucasian Chalk Circle, I got my first 
review in a newspaper. It said, “William 
tells me he wants to be an actor. He is.”’
It’s very difficult to be an actor living 
in regional Australia, William explains, 
‘Very few plays or films or TV shows get 
done down on the far south coast, so travel 
is inevitable. Since COVID, auditions have 
changed, whether it’s for a commercial or 
for film or TV. 
Instead of being with people who can 
give direction or a sense of what they are 
looking for, you get the script and find a 
suitable spot in your house to do the scene 
as a selfie. 
It’s really, really hard to know what 
direction to settle on before you send it off. 
And if you get a callback, it’s a lot of time 
and money driving and flying.’
William’s passion project since 2011 
has been adapting and performing Homer’s 
Iliad, which you might have seen in June 
at Four Winds. ‘It is one of the most 
extraordinary stories of humanity and our 
problems ever written. I often describe 
it as like the CSI of ancient Greece. The 
descriptions of swords slicing off arms 
and spears piercing chests are quite 
phenomenal. It’s also got some absolutely 
fantastic humour in it too. To do the whole 
thing would take sixteen hours, so my 
recent show was only part.’
Based on seventeen translations of 
this 3,500-year-old poem, William decided 
to create his own version, ‘To put it into 
our language today. I’ve used very much 
a modern idiom, even swearing, but it’s 
not colloquial because I really appreciate 
the classic nature of the story and structure 
of the poem. Most of the translators are 
academics, whereas I’m an actor who 
speaks other people’s words. The whole 
process for me has been about making 
this story as accessible as possible, and 
the translators – the work that they have 
done – are phenomenal. Every one of 
those seventeen translations is as different 
from the others as mine is from theirs. I’m 
extremely proud of it, I have to say.’

After experiencing weeks of smoke and red skies in Sydney

Summer bushfires in Cobargo, artist Fiona Henderson wanted to 
create some art pieces that would memorialise the horrific fires.

foundations. She also visited Gabrielle and Dan to collect things

Fiona, who was a complete stranger, access to our burned sheds 
and studios, because as artists ourselves we wanted to support her 
idea for an artistic project to document and commemorate the fires.
Fiona had also been a practicing lawyer and, with her friend 
and lawyer Paul Mallam, set up an office in the Cobargo pub for 
a few days for anyone who needed legal advice or just help filling 
in forms or talking through problems. And, as winter approached, 
Fiona ran a blanket and fundraising drive in Sydney to collect 
warm bedding and clothes, as well as financial donations to buy 
water tanks for people who still had no access to fresh water on 
their property.
Thinking about working with burned objects, Fiona said, ‘My 
doctorate explored the ability of things to tell stories in images 
that don’t contain people. The things I collected were imbued with 
stories of the fires, of tragedy and suffering, but also of survival, 
transformation and beauty. They were very powerful to work 
with, and it took me some time to find the right visual language 
that allowed them to share their stories as clearly as possible.’
It was an honour for me, as a friend and a representative of 
the Cobargo community, to attend the Sydney opening of Fiona’s 
show, Memento Bushfire.
At the opening Fiona expressed her profound gratitude to 
everyone in Cobargo who let her into their lives in the aftermath 
of the fires and enabled her to create the pieces for Memento.

names on leaves for the Cobargo Memorial tree.

the author of eight books, both fiction and

I was unsure about reviewing this 
fictional account of real things because 
I thought it might be too narrow in its 
appeal. Yes, it is set in the audacious 
dream factory of 1970s Italian cinema, 
but it is an investigation into the difficult 
relationship between artifice and truth, 
illusion and reality, love and power. It 
can also be read as a fascinating insight 
into the film-making process of the great 
filmmakers and as a murder mystery.

Italy’s ‘Years of Iron’. Neofascism was on 
the rise, and the far right was flourishing.

receiving world acclaim for its brilliance.

present throughout the novel.

While sketching by the canal, he

Right Plant, Wrong Place … Again!
One of the advantages of gardening 
is that very few decisions are permanent. 
Plants can be moved, divided and 
rearranged as your garden evolves and 
your ideas change. That supposedly 
short plant you tucked in along the edge 
grows much taller than expected, the one 
you planted in the shade needs more sun 
(or vice versa), that climber would be 
better suited elsewhere, that perennial 
needs dividing and those two plants 
would simply look better side by side.
July is one of the best times to 
make these changes. In mid-winter, 
many plants will have paused their 
growing, exhausted their flowering 
and fruit or seed production, and will 
be gathering energy for the upcoming 
spring. Digging them up now results in 
minimal disturbance, and they will be 
settled into their new position, ready 
to grow again when warmer weather 
arrives.
Before you move a plant, it is 
beneficial to prune it first, as moving 
a plant disturbs the root system and 
reduces its ability to absorb water and 
nutrients. Reducing the above-ground 
growth helps the smaller root ball 
support the whole plant while it re-
establishes itself. The other thing to do 
before moving a plant is to have the new 
planting hole prepared and ready to go, 
limiting the time the plant spends out of 
the ground.
Before you dig, imagine the plant in 
a pot. You should aim to dig out a root 
ball at least that size. The more roots 
you can bring with the plant, the more 
successful the move is likely to be. Chop 
or dig right the way around the plant 
first, then try to lever it out from below. 
Larger roots should be cut cleanly with 
sharp secateurs. Support the root ball 
while transferring the plant to its new 
hole and ensure it is sitting at the same 
depth as it was in its original location.
Once the plant is in position, 
flood the planting hole and root ball 
with water and allow it to soak away. 
Backfill, then give the plant another 
drink, this time with some added soil 
conditioner such as seaweed extract or 
fish emulsion. Hold off on fertilising 
until you begin to see signs of new 
growth.
Soon enough you’ll be looking 
around the garden and wondering 
whether that plant over there might be 
happier somewhere else. July may be 
one of the best times to move plants, 
but don’t let that stop you at other times 
of the year. Most gardeners know that 
the best time to do many jobs is when 
the weather is reasonable, the soil is 
moist and you happen to have a spare 
afternoon. As we like to say, sometimes 
the best time to prune is simply when 
the shears are sharp.
Meanwhile, around the town 
gardens, the camellias continue to shine 
and the normally nondescript, hardy, 
succulent shrub Crassula ovata (jade 
plant) is bursting into bloom as it enters 
peak flowering season. The banksias 
and grevilleas are also enjoying a 
flowering flush, and the citrus are 
beginning to ripen.
In the wild places, on the roadsides 
around the Triangle, the bushland is 
rather homogenously green at present, 
with few flowers currently obvious.
This month in the vegie garden we 
continue catering to the cooler weather 
and so are mostly still limited to planting 
leeks, shallots and onions, peas of any 
description, broad beans and lettuce.
Got a gardening question, plant 
mystery or pest problem? Drop us a line 
at gardening@thetriangle.org.au. We’d 
love to hear from you.
Happy gardening!
